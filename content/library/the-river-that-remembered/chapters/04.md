# Provisions

Bakary Vorn comes down the pier at first light with a sack of rope on one shoulder, and counts her barge before he says good morning.

He is an enormous man bent into a still bigger one, fifty-two years old and built like an overturned boat: broad, slow, slope-shouldered, with a grey plait down his back as thick as a hawser. He ducks the lantern-hook that hangs at head height for everyone else. The hand that holds the sack has a thumb and two fingers and a smooth place where the other two should be. Thida has known that hand for twenty-one years and never learned how it came to be so. Ibou said a rope, and talked about something else.

He steps aboard without asking. *Long Patience* takes him with a dip and a long creak, like a woman handed a debt. He goes down into the hold along the ribs, laying his palm flat on each as he passes, and a low murmur comes up out of the dark. "...five, six, seven. One, two, three..."

"Forty-two," Thida says from the stern.

"Six sevens."

"It's forty-two."

"Six sevens." He climbs out. His eyes, small and deep-set and the colour of tea, travel over her boots and the tiller and the bundled poles without once arriving at her face. "Thi."

"Bakary."

Six years since Ibou's burning. He came to that, she remembers, and stood at the back with his hat in his hands, and left before the rice. She did not go after him. She had not wanted rice either.

"I'd heard you were going upriver," he says.

"Who told you?"

"The well-man."

"The well-man told you."

"The well-man tells everyone. He tells the well." He sets the sack down on the deck, where it settles like a sleeping dog. "I've rope."

"I haven't posted for hands."

"No."

"I haven't posted for anything. My berth's frozen."

"I heard that too."

"From the well-man."

"From the well-man."

She looks at the sack. It is good rope, hand-laid hemp, the kind that costs three days' wages a fathom. At the pier's end the brown skiff has come back and lies in the early light with a man asleep in it, his hat over his face. He is not as asleep as he is pretending. "What do you want?"

"A place."

"I can give you a tenth of the first half. It's double the toll, but it's a long road."

"Nothing."

"Nobody works for nothing."

"I do."

"Then it isn't work, it's something else, and I'd like to know what."

He says nothing. He has always been the slowest talker in the family. Ibou used to say that a question put to Bakary on Monday got you an answer by Friday, and the answer was generally yes. Now he stands on her deck with his big hands hanging, and the silence has a smell. She knows that smell. She has had it across her kitchen table from the brothers and sons of the drowned who didn't say goodbye, the ones who have already decided to pay for something and have not yet been told the price.

"Say it plain," she says. It is his phrase. She hands it back to him like a dropped tool.

Something moves in his face, a flicker like a fish under dark water. "A seventh," he says.

"You said nothing."

"You said nobody works for it. A seventh, of what's left when you've paid for the poles."

"A seventh of what?"

"Of what's left." He considers the sack. "A seventh's a fair number."

"It's a number."

"It's the one I've got." He lifts his eyes at last and lays them on the tiller, on the blue paint she renews every dry season, and nowhere else. "Ibou would have wanted someone with you."

"Ibou isn't here to want."

"No," Bakary agrees, and the word comes out of him like a stone out of a well. "That's rather the trouble."

She could send him off. She thinks of the Hen's Ladder and the Stair, of poling two hundred and seventy miles with one pair of arms and a priest, of the way the sack of rope sits on her deck as if it had always sat there. She is a ferry. Carry all, ask nothing. And if she asks, he will answer on Friday.

"A seventh," she says.

He takes it the way a man takes a tooth being drawn.

* * *

The rest of the day goes to the work of making a barge ready for forty days.

Vuthy comes at noon with a purse of silver bits, the first half of the toll, and a message that the lady is grateful and the priest will be delivered at dusk. He puts the purse in her hand as though it were a dish of something hot. Thida spends it, a bit at a time, in the lanes, and finds out what a frozen berth is worth. The market sells to her at three times the price and will not meet her eye. A fishwife she has known for twenty years weighs the mudfish with her thumb on the scale and says it is the drought, Mistress, it's all the drought. The man who sells poles will not take her credit. At the noodle raft the two women who stood with their ladles at the Steps say nothing at all, but one of them slips a twist of pickled lime into her hand as she passes, wrapped in a banana leaf and still wet, and turns away to stir a pot that is already stirred.

Bakary counts. He buys everything in sevens. Seven sacks of rice, seven strings of seven smoked mudfish, seven crocks of lime, seven poles to add to the seven she has. "We need thirty fish," Thida says. "Forty-nine," Bakary says, and carries them down the pier in two bundles as though they weighed nothing. A bladder of sweet water from the well-man, who has been paid by half of Low Tamber for the pleasure of telling it everything, goes into the stern where she can reach it. Bakary hefts it, sniffs the neck, and says, "Sweet." It is the closest thing to praise he gives all day.

She buys eels, too. Pov the well-man's brother sells her thirty pounds of live yellow eels at a price that would make a thief blush, and they slide into the bilge tank amidships in a dark muscular heap, thick as wrists, and begin at once to hate each other. A ferry with eels is a ferry going to market. It is not the reason she buys them. She does not examine the reason.

By the time the lanterns are being lit, the barge sits a hand lower in the water and smells of tar and smoked fish and lime. Thida stands on the stern looking at the pier, and the pier is empty of everyone but her cousin-by-marriage, who is replacing a lashing that did not need replacing, and the skiff, and her own long shadow.

The priest arrives in a litter.

Two men in unbleached cotton with black bands on their arms carry it down the pier at a trot, and set it on the planks beside the barge, and lift Sovann out of it as one lifts a crate: a hand under each arm, a heave, a lowering onto the deck. He keeps both arms wrapped round a basket the whole time. It is a round basket packed with rice-husk, and out of the husk a red-clay collar shows, and under the collar the dull brown shoulder of the jar.

"I did ask to walk," Sovann says to the deck, apologetically. "She said the pier was dangerous."

"It's a pier."

"I did point that out." He looks up. His robe is clean, which she has never seen, and patched at the knee in three yellows, and his round head has been shaved that afternoon and nicked in two places. The smell of rice-wine on him has been scrubbed and has come back through the scrubbing. "Forgive me. Lady Ruan thought it best that the Drawer travel with the Drawing."

"In case it wants drawing again?"

"I do hope not."

The servants bow to Thida, very deeply, and go back up the pier at the same trot without a word, as though they had delivered a cheese.

Bakary has come aft. He stands looking at the priest with the patient expression of a man at a ford. "Say it plain," he says.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Why you."

"Ah." Sovann hugs the basket. "She does not trust me out of her sight."

"Why not?"

"Forgive me. I have a reputation."

"For what?"

"For being the sort of man who has to be delivered," says Sovann, with great dignity, and sits down on the nearest hatch-cover, and begins to cry a little, softly, with no visible cause, the way other men sneeze. Thida looks at Bakary. Bakary looks at the pier. Neither asks.

It is Bakary who finds the boy. He goes forward to shift the eel-crate, the slatted box the size of a coffin set on end that lives lashed on the foredeck and carries eels to market when the tank is full, and which is, tonight, as she told him, empty. He puts his good hand on one side and his bad on the other, and lifts, and goes still.

Thida watches him. He sets the crate down again, very gently, the way you put down a sleeping child. He counts. She can see his lips. At seven he takes the latch off the lid.

Inside, folded up with his knees against his ears, a chart-case under his arm, sits a boy in a grey coat that comes to his shins.

"Good evening," says Ife Lamin. "I was told this was the Salt Line ferry."

"Over," says Bakary.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Over. The side." He has the boy by the collar of the coat already, a hand like a shovel round a fistful of wool, and Ife is lifted out of the crate and held up in the lantern light with his boots a foot above the deck, still holding the chart-case against his chest, like a cat that has been picked up wrong. "Seven. Six. Five."

"I can't swim!"

"Then you'll float, or you won't."

"It's a lie," the boy says, quite calm. "I can swim. I only said that because it sounds worse."

"Leave him," Thida says.

"He'll sell us."

"To whom?"

"To anyone." Bakary doesn't look at her. He looks at the coat. "Gaugeman's coat."

"It fell off one," Ife says.

"Off the back of a living one?"

"Off a hook. In a laundry. Which isn't stealing, it's rescue." He is put down. He straightens the coat with enormous dignity and rolls the sleeves, one by one, four turns each. "I'm the person you were looking for. You just hadn't had the pleasure yet."

"I wasn't looking for anyone."

"That's what they all say." He pushes the fishhook in his ear a little straighter. "I did your Salt Line chart this morning. I have it here. The one you didn't buy, for free, since you're hiring me."

"I'm not hiring you."

"You'll be hiring me by the Hen's Ladder. I'm saving you the trouble."

Thida looks at him: thirteen, ink to the second joint, with the look of a child who has spent a long afternoon folded in a box and does not intend to say whether it was worth it. The skiff at the pier-end has not moved. The lantern at its stern has not been lit. She looks at the boy, and at the skiff, and at the boy again, and thinks of eleven callers, nine of them the well-man.

"How old?"

"Thirteen. Fourteen in the wet."

"Who knows you're here?"

"Nobody." It comes a hair too quick. "I'm very hard to find. It's why I'm good at charts."

"Charts," she says. "That's the fare. A fresh one for every mile we pass, drawn at night from what you've seen with your own eyes and not what you've heard. You eat what we eat and sleep where you're put. You don't touch the tiller."

"I never touch tillers. They touch back."

"If I find you've sold a single mile of it to anyone, I'll put you ashore in the first mud we come to. Do you understand?"

"Absolutely." He holds out his hand, black to the second joint. She does not take it. He looks at his own hand with mild interest, and puts it away. "Is he drunk?" he asks, looking at the priest. "He smells drunk."

"He's reverent," says Sovann, from the hatch.

"He smells reverent," says Ife, "in a way that's very close to drunk."

* * *

There is an argument about where everyone is to sleep, and it goes on until the second bell of the night.

The stern-shelter of *Long Patience* is a hood of palm thatch two arm-spans wide, and Thida has always slept there alone with the tiller at her head. She says the jar will sleep there too, under her eye, and the priest will sleep wherever he is put. The priest says that a Drawing wants a warm body near it for the first month, it's the practice. She asks whose practice. He says an old one. Bakary says, "Say it plain." Sovann says, with the air of a man who has been cornered, that it will be lonely.

"It's a jar," says Thida.

"Forgive me. It's a man."

"It's a man and a coin," says Ife, from the crate, where he has gone back by his own choice and from which the voice emerges with the smug authority of a bird in a nest. "I heard him say so at the Cup. Everyone heard."

"Nobody was meant to hear."

"That's what makes it a good thing to hear."

In the end the priest sleeps on the foredeck on a rush mat with the basket under his head for a pillow, and Bakary, with a face that gives nothing away, throws a blanket over him, and the priest snores in a small, polite, apologetic way. Bakary lies across the hatch, wrapped in rope-smell, filling it from end to end, and counts quietly in the dark until the counting turns to breath. Ife is in the crate with the lid propped by his boot and the chart-case for a pillow. He is writing something, by feel, in the black, and when she asks what, he says "Mud," which is not an answer, and goes quiet.

Thida does not sleep. She sits at the tiller in the dark with the pin in her belt and listens to the barge: four sleepers, one jar, forty-two ribs, and under them all the small dry knocking of the eels turning over in their tank. A frozen berth is behind her. The black silk is knotted round her wrist, next to the grey cord with its loosened blue, and she has not taken it off. She is a ferry on charter, the one vessel on the river that may be looked at and not touched. It is a very small piece of law to stand on.

* * *

*Your hull sits a hand lower in me than it did yesterday, and I know why. I weigh everything that lies on me, and I do not need to be told. Teak, and tar, and rope. Seven sacks of rice. Fourteen poles, the long ones and the short. Mudfish in the bilge, lime in a crock, a bladder of sweet water from a well that has never heard of me. A man who weighs what two men weigh and carries it as if it were a debt. A priest, light as a bird, who stood in me to the hip yesterday and has drunk his own weight in rice-wine since. A boy who weighs less than his coat. And you at the stern, who weigh no more than you did eleven years ago, and carry that as though it were a great deal.*

*I weigh them all. Weighing is what I do, with planks, with people. But there is a jar in the bow, in a basket of husk, and it will not come out right. I have put it on the scale three times. It weighs a man. It weighs a man and a little more, and the little more is not heavy and is not light, and it will not stay in the pan.*

* * *

Low Tamber at dawn is a town of people who have come out to be quiet.

The lanes are full of them. They stand on the decks of their lashed boats with their arms at their sides, in the grey light, and nobody is cooking. The eel-sellers' stalls are shuttered. Smoke rises from perhaps a dozen braziers in all the thousand boats. A girl of six or seven lifts one hand to wave, and her mother folds the hand down against her chest, the way one folds a wing, without taking her eyes from the barge. They are not unkind. Thida has carried the dead of half these families, and they stand and look at her as at a boat going out through the fog with someone they once knew on it. It is the look the living give to a grave, and she sets her jaw against it.

Ife is on the cabin roof with his board across his knees and his fishhook swinging, pretending not to be looking at anything. Sovann sits on the hatch with the basket in his lap and his eyes shut. Bakary stands at the bow with the short pole in his three-fingered hand, taking the weight of the barge off the pier with the slow absorbed care of a man easing a cart out of a rut. The brown skiff has gone from the end of the pier. She finds that she misses it, which annoys her.

She slips the stern line herself. The rope comes in wet and cold, and she coils it the way it wants to lie.

At the top of the Drowning Steps, as the barge comes round, Kunthea is standing.

She stands beside her stool with the slate held across her chest in both arms, its written side against her ribs, like a woman holding a child she does not want seen. She does not wave. She has not got up for anyone since the old king was a prince. Thida looks at her across forty yards of brown water, and Kunthea looks back, and neither lifts a hand.

Thida sets the pole in the mud. She leans her weight into it, long arms straight, the way she has done it for twenty years, and lets the first stroke fall. The barge slides out from the pier. The Steps slide behind a raft of lashed boats, and the market closes up over them, and they are gone.
