# Salt Line

The pomelo trees at Pesh Alo are white to the hip.

They climb the slope behind the village in rows so straight that someone must once have stretched a cord for every one, grey trunks and brown dying leaves above, and from the ground to the height of a man's belt each trunk wears a crust of salt, glittering and thick, like a stocking. It hasn't flooded. The sea has not come up the river in a wave. It has come up through the ground, a handspan a season, and stood in the roots and drunk the trees from underneath. Thida sees the white on the slope from two miles out, and thinks it is frost, which is stupid, since the nearest frost is in the highlands, two hundred miles upriver. Then the village comes round the bend with its stilts and its landing and its post.

The post stands at the end of the landing, a pole of black wood as tall as a man, and up its length are painted lines. Each line has a year beside it in red. The lowest is below the waterline. The highest is a hand above Thida's head, and it is wet, with a yellow stain on the wood, and the date beside it says *this year*.

"The Salt Line," Ife says, delighted. "Look. It's moving." He has the chart-case open on his knees and is already inking. "It's the only line on the river that goes the wrong way. Mile sixty-eight. That'll want an arrow."

"It's a village," says Thida.

"It's a village *and* a line."

Pesh Alo does not look like a place where anything is dying. Nobody wails. Women in faded sarongs stand about the landing with the expression of people who have got used to a smell. Iron pans stand on bricks all along the bank, each with a lid, a wet rag draped over the lid, and a fire underfoot, and a child kneels at every one with a cup. The steam runs down the corner of each rag and drips into the cup one bright bead at a time.

"They boil the sea out of the wells," says Bakary, from the bow.

"What do they get?"

"A spoon an hour." He watches a girl tip her cup into a crock the size of a thimble. "Per pan."

"How many pans?"

"Forty-two."

Thida does not ask if he counted. She watches the girl's face, which is as patient as the pan, and she thinks of Ibou coughing in the corner of the kitchen for three winters, the white crust on the rag he held to his mouth. Salt-lung. Her husband drowned slowly, on dry land, in his own bed, and there was nothing to put him in the water for.

* * *

The widow Fanta meets them on the landing with a string of smoked fish in each hand.

She is fifty-five, with a laugh like a dropped pot and four front teeth missing, a small round woman as dark and polished as a nut. Her husband died last year. She says so cheerfully, as though it were an item of local news. Her grandson, Pich, stands at her hip with his thumb in his mouth. He is five, and he coughs without making any noise, a small tired shake of the ribs, like a kettle about to boil.

"I hear the ferry's got medicine," Fanta says. "I've fish."

"I have ginger and clove. Willow-bark. A little poppy." Thida opens the tin chest on the hatch. It is a good chest, kept for the dead's families, and she has never before been glad that it is not empty. "How long's he had it?"

"Since the pomelos went." Fanta puts a hand on the boy's head without looking at him. "Three months. It comes in the night."

Thida kneels. The child regards her over his thumb with immense grave eyes. She lays two fingers on his chest, feels the flutter and catch, and takes them away. "Honey and ginger in hot water, every night. Boiled water, not well-water. And keep him off the damp floor."

"Honey." Fanta laughs. "Where am I to get honey?"

"I have half a jar," says Sovann. It is the first thing he has said since the landing. He has come to stand at Thida's shoulder, holding the empty basket. "Forgive me, I don't know why. It's for the tuak. It keeps the cough down."

"Then give it."

"Yes. Of course." He goes to get it, looking pleased and shocked.

They trade. The smoked fish are snakeheads, split and black and tough as boot-leather. Thida takes four strings and gives clove, ginger, the willow-bark, a twist of poppy, and the honey, which Sovann brings back in a clay pot, holding it out like a man caught in an act of grace. Fanta looks at the pile. "You've given me twice what I gave."

"You've got a boy."

"So have a thousand." But she tucks the medicine into her sarong, and her face goes through a number of things quickly, and what it settles on is brisk. She pushes a basket of duck eggs, grey-green and heavy, into Thida's arms. "Don't argue. Come. I'll show you what you're sailing into. It's no use telling it. You've to see."

She takes them up the village path between the pans and the stilt-houses, with the boy riding her hip. The smell of the salt is clean and cold. At the end of the path, past the last house, a willow stands at the bank. It has been, Thida sees, an enormous tree, big as a granary, the kind that shades a whole bend. It is dead. The bark has come off in long grey sheets. Its great roots, which should be sunk in black mud, have been uncovered by a bank that has slumped, and the roots are white.

Not dried: white. Thick as a man's thigh at the root-crown and tapering to fingers, and all of it, root and rootlet, has turned to crystal. It hangs over the water in a pale, glittering, impossible mass, like a chandelier of bone.

"Drowned willow," says Fanta. "That's what we call it. It stood in the salt, see, and the salt went into it. All the way in. It was green at midsummer." She puts out a hand and flicks one white rootlet with a fingernail. It rings. It gives a thin clear note, like a finger on a glass, that runs along the whole hanging mass and fades.

Ife has gone quiet. He takes out his pen. He writes along the margin of the chart, in his small fast hand, *Here be a tree. Formerly*, and then, for a long time, does not write anything.

"It'll do the same to the orchards," says Fanta. "Next year, the year after. And the wells. And us." She says it with no hurry and no weight, the way you'd name tomorrow's market. "I've told the headman. The headman has told the Warden's man. The Warden's man has a form."

Thida looks at the pale root, and the water below it, and thinks of the black piers standing across the river singing to themselves. She thinks of a man named Oluwa Sen, whom she has seen twice, both times across a room: a small courteous person with a white goatee, who bowed to the ferrywomen. A form.

"Who put the dam there?" says Ife.

"The Lords," Fanta says. "To save us."

"From what?"

"From the Flood. Didn't they teach you the Flood, boy?" She puts out her hand to the white tree again, and again it rings. "Twenty thousand, in my grandmother's time. They say it was the last time the river forgot itself." She shrugs. "Better slow than sudden, the Lords say. I'll tell you something, though. It doesn't feel slow."

* * *

The dreamer sets up his stall at the landing at dusk.

He is a smooth man in his forties with a smooth round face and a copper badge on a cord at his neck, stamped with a gauge-rod: the Lords' licence. His stall is a folding stand under a blue awning, and on it a tray of thimbles, little glazed cups no bigger than a thumb-joint, each stoppered with wax and tied with a twist of coloured twine. The twine says what is in them. A storm. A wedding. A good dinner. A first kiss. A mother's kitchen. A night with no pain.

The last sells best. There is a short line for it already, a patient shuffle of women with their hands at their throats, and men leaning on sticks, and a girl who may be sixteen with her sarong held up to her mouth.

"Mistress." The dreamer bows to Thida over his tray with the practised sweetness of a man who has bowed to ten thousand women and hated them all, a little. "Sambath. Licensed by the Lords of the Water. I won't press. I only mention that I have some very particular waters. A small stock. Very respectful. From the Closing Reach, as it happens, if the Mistress would..."

"No."

"I only say..."

"No."

He bows again, unoffended. "Of course. Of course. Others will."

"Ugh," says Ife, from over her shoulder. He has a look of lofty disgust on his face. "Imagine. Drinking a dead person. You'd have to be mad."

"You'd have to be an idiot," says Thida.

"Yes," says Ife cheerfully, and goes back to the barge.

She watches the line. The women tip back their heads one by one and drink in a quick fierce gulp, like medicine, and stand a moment with their faces shut. Then something loosens in them. Their shoulders go down, and they walk away not talking to each other, with the look of people who have been somewhere warm.

She thinks of a tray of thimbles at Pa Tomloy, eleven years ago, and a face like a kind uncle.

At the end of the line Bakary has put down a coil of rope he did not need to be carrying. He is not in the line. He is looking, past the pans and the lamps, up the slope to where the lane climbs between two stilt-houses toward a little house with a red door. Thida knows the door. She knows the woman behind it, his sister, who sent a crock of lime pickle to Ibou's burning and had it set down, unopened, by a brother who did not come to the rice.

"Your sister's here," she says.

"She is."

"You'll go up?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"She'd ask me what I've been doing." He picks up the coil. "And I'd tell her."

* * *

In the night she hears him go.

She does not sleep in any real way these days. She lies on her mat in the stern-shelter listening to the village settle, a dog and a cough and the tick of cooling pans, and then a squeak at the crate, bare feet on the plank, light as a cat's, and the gangway taking a weight of ninety pounds and giving it back.

She could go after him. She is a ferry, though. Carry all, ask nothing. And there is a particular stillness to a boy leaving a boat at night with his shoulders pulled up that she has seen on her own kitchen floor, and she knows it is not for an adult to interrupt. After an hour or so the feet come back, slower, and the crate lid squeaks, and there is quiet.

* * *

Dawn finds him on the cabin roof, with his board on his knees and his pen in his hand.

Thida kneels at the brazier with the rice-pot. The village is just stirring, smoke rising thin and straight from the cook-fires. She watches him from the edge of her eye, and for a long time thinks nothing of it, because he is inking the cartouche in the bottom corner of the sheet, the little box with the wavy border that he draws on every chart: *Drawn by Ife, son of*, and then the name.

The pen doesn't move.

A drop of ink gathers on the nib and falls, and makes a black star on the paper. Another gathers. He sits quite still. He is not frowning. He looks like a person trying to hear a sound in the next room.

"It's gone," he says.

His voice is perfectly ordinary. Thida puts down the spoon.

"Her name." He looks at her and past her, and the look is puzzled and wide. "I've got her. I've got the way she did her hair, and she banged the pot twice before she put the lid on. I've got the song she sang at the stove, I could hum you the song. I just can't... it's like a tooth that's been pulled. I keep putting my tongue where it was." He laughs, a sharp small laugh like a cough. "It's *there*. There's a gap exactly that shape."

Her stomach has turned over. She knows. She knew, she realises, when she heard the feet on the gangway, and has let herself not know it.

"What did you drink?" she says.

"I..." He opens his mouth to lie, and she watches the lie fail in his face, unbuilt, brick by brick. He is thirteen. "A storm. A fisher's. It was the cheapest. I only wanted to know what it was *like*." And then something happens to his face, something she was not ready for, and the whole thing lights from within. "Thida, you should have it. It's the best thing I've ever... I was in a boat. A green one, with an eye on the bow, I had a *beard*, I've never had a beard, and the wave came over the prow like a wall, a green wall, and the whole boat stood on its end, and I laughed. I laughed! I wasn't frightened at all. I was so happy I..." He breaks off, breathless, flushed, his ink-black hands in the air as if holding the green wall. "I've never been happy like that. I never knew you could be happy at a thing like that."

Behind her the priest has woken. He is sitting up on his mat with the blanket round his shoulders and his shaven head tilted, looking at the boy with an expression she has not seen on his face before. It is not drunk, and not pious. It is grief, plain as a bruise.

"Forgive me," Sovann says. "Child. You drank."

"One thimble. It was only a thimble."

"Yes." He rubs his face. "I ought to have said. I did mean to say. The river keeps accounts. Every swallow of a stranger's memory displaces one of your own, of equal weight, and the one it displaces goes into the water for someone else to dream. A thimble takes small things. A draught takes great ones. There's no getting out of the ledger."

"A name isn't small," says Ife.

"It weighs what it weighs." The priest says it with infinite gentleness. "The river does not ask what a thing is worth. Only what it weighs."

"That's stupid."

"Yes."

"I'd have picked something else. If I'd known I could have picked. I'd have given it the mud under my nails, or my..." He stops. He looks at his own hands. For the first time Thida sees the thirteen years in him, in the narrow neck, in the puffy line under the eyes. "Can I get it back?"

"I don't know," Sovann says. "Forgive me. I don't think anyone does."

Thida finds that she is standing. She doesn't remember rising. Her right hand is up, open, at the height of her shoulder, and the whole of her arm is ready to bring it down on the side of that thin head, and the thing she wants is simple and total: to hit him. Not hard. Hard enough to make a noise. To put into the air of the morning a single flat clap that would say: *you stupid, stupid child, you had something, you had it and you gave it away for a storm.* She wants it so much that her arm shakes.

He doesn't flinch. That is the worst part. He does not look at her hand. He looks at her face, with the great wet clear eyes, as one looks at a thing that has always hit.

She puts the hand down.

She turns round and takes the iron pan off its hook. She sets it on the brazier with a clang that startles a heron. From Fanta's last present, the basket of duck eggs, grey-green and heavy, she takes four, cracks them one-handed into the pan with a violence that makes the fat spit, and breaks a fifth, and a sixth. She slices a pickled lime and throws it in. The smell goes up, sharp and sour and golden, into the cold morning.

"Is this a bribe?" says Ife, after a while.

"It's breakfast."

"It's a lot of eggs."

"Eat them."

He comes down off the roof with his board under his arm. He sits cross-legged on the deck with the plate on his knee, and picks up an egg with his fingers, and puts it in his mouth whole, and chews with his eyes shut. She watches the little muscle at the corner of his jaw. When he has eaten four he says, with his mouth full, "These are excellent," and for a moment his chin wobbles. Then he reaches for a fifth.

Bakary has come up from the bow. He looks at the pan, at the boy, at her. He takes the last two eggs and, for reasons of his own, counts them. "Seven," he says, "if you count the lime."

"Eat," she says. She does not trust her voice.

* * *

It is Bakary who goes up the path in the morning, with a knotted cloth.

She sees him from the barge, a very large slow man with his head down, carrying something small in his three-fingered hand. At the third house from the end he meets the well-man, a leathery old fellow with a hand-auger over his shoulder, and they stand together by the well-head in the cool. Bakary says something. The old man shakes his head. Bakary unknots the cloth with his bad hand and tips the contents into the good, and she sees them, bright in the sun: silver bits in a small neat pile. She counts them without meaning to. She is, she finds, counting in sevens.

The well-man looks at the silver, and at Bakary, and says something short and cracked, and points down the well-shaft with the auger. Bakary nods. The old man spits on his palm, shakes the huge hand, and tucks the money inside his shirt.

On the way back down, Bakary passes close below the stern. He does not look at her.

"Past the clay," he says, to the planks. "There's a sweet vein. A man's height deeper, he says. If he'd the hands."

"Whose well is it?"

"The lane's."

"And the lane is..."

"Fanta's." A pause. "And the others." He puts his foot on the gangway. "It's a number," he says, as though it needed defending. "It came out even."

She lets him go aboard. She says nothing. At the lip of the stern, with the sun on the white tree and the village boiling its patient spoonfuls beyond him, she watches his back and thinks of a rope with a knot in it, and eight inches of brown water, and the red door up the lane where he did not go.

* * *

They leave late. The morning is spent on the pans, and on Fanta's grandson, and on taking down and putting up again the sail-yard that Bakary did not like the sound of. At last Thida sets the pole and the barge slides off the landing with the village standing on it, the widow with her boy on her hip, the smooth dreamer under his blue awning, a line of women with their hands at their throats. The salt-white pomelos slide behind the bend.

It is when Ife has gone up on the roof to do his bearings, in the first sun, standing against the sky with his arms out and the grey coat blown flat against him, that she notices.

She has seen him in that coat a week. It comes to his shins, rolled four turns at the sleeve, swallowing his hands: a coat off a hook in a laundry. But the wind has pinned it to his body, and she sees, as she has not seen before, that the shoulders sit true. The seams lie on his shoulder-bones as if they had been measured. The collar is cut for a narrow neck, and the grey wool under it, where the sun hasn't reached, is as dark as the day it was spun. It was made for a small person, with a hem let down to be grown into.

A coat that came off a living gaugeman's back would not know where this boy's shoulders were.

She looks at it a little longer, and puts it away with the other things. She is getting a great many things put away.

* * *

That night they tie up in the reeds three miles above the village, in a pool the colour of tea. The frogs are loud. Overhead a great bank of stars lies across the sky, and the water holds them without a ripple, so that the barge seems to hang in the middle of a bowl of light. Sovann is asleep with his head on the basket. Bakary's breath goes slow and even, in sevens. Thida sits in the stern with the tiller under her scarred hand.

She hears the crate lid creak. Ife comes aft in the dark on bare feet, with the grey coat round his shoulders like a cloak, and sits down against the stern-post with his knees up. For a while he doesn't say anything. He picks at the weave of the deck-matting. A fish rises, loud, and the circle spreads, and is a long time vanishing.

"Thida."

"Mm."

"Did I ever say her name to you? When we were at the Steps. Or in the market. Before, I mean." His voice is small and level, the chart-seller's voice with the patter gone out of it. "I thought maybe I'd said it. You've got the sort of face people say things to. And you've carried half of Low Tamber's dead, you'd know the names. All those tins. You must know what she was called."

Four hundred and some tins. Four hundred and some names, laid up in her head like the knots on a cord, in rows, in the dark. It might be among them. She has no way to take them out and turn them over one by one.

"Thida?"

"Ask me in the morning," she says.

"Why?"

"Because it's late."

He is silent. She feels the question stay on the dark between them, round and warm and still, like an egg. Then his shoulder shifts against the post, and he nods, slowly. He believes her. He is thirteen, and he believes that she will know it by morning.

She doesn't know it. She sits with her hand on the tiller, and the frogs go on, and she goes down into her own head after a name she never had, and finds in the place where it should be nothing at all, the bare shelf, the clean place in the dust.
