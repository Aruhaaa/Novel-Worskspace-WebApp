# A Jar for the Source

The barge on Tamber Channel is dressed in black from awning to waterline, and every yard of the cloth is wet.

It hangs in swags between the poles, heavy with night damp, and drips steadily onto the dark water with a sound like someone counting. No frog sings near it. Thida has come up the channel under the eyes of two gauge-skiffs at the lower mouth and a third at the upper, and on the roof of a laundry-boat tied nearby a man in a straw hat has been fishing for an hour without a line. When she lays *Long Patience* against the mourning-barge's flank, a hand she cannot see takes her rope and makes it fast.

"This way, Mistress." It is an old man with a stiff leg and a black cord at his wrist, who says his name is Vuthy as though it had been a long time since anyone asked. He leads her in under the swags. The wet cloth brushes her face and shoulders in the dark, cold as the flank of a fish, and she walks through it with her head up and her hands at her sides, the way she walks the Steps.

The hall inside is long and low and lit by lamps in black paper shades, so that the light lies on the floor in shallow puddles and does not climb the walls. A screen of painted cranes stands across one end. A table of black lacquer. A brazier with a kettle on it, ticking. At the table a woman sits writing, and she does not look up until she has finished her line, and blotted it, and laid the brush on its rest at an exact right angle to the paper's edge.

"Mistress Vorn. You've come."

"You sent a girl with a silk."

"Was it a sufficient lure?"

"It was a silk."

Channary Ruan very nearly smiles. She is thirty-six and looks older in some places and younger in others, younger at the mouth and older about the eyes. She is tall even seated. Her braid is as thick as a wrist and black, except for one streak of white that begins at the left temple as if someone had drawn a finger through wet paint. Her mourning silk is the colour of ink, and it makes her hands look naked: long, sharp hands, the nails cut straight across. They never stop moving a little. The cup is squared to the table's edge. The brush is squared to the paper. The lamp's base has been turned so its maker's mark faces her. Thida stands in the lamp's puddle and understands that this is a woman who keeps order in the only place left to her, which is within arm's reach.

"There is tea." Channary pours from the pot into one cup, and drinks, and sets it square. Then she pours another. "I pour it myself, from this pot, and I drink first. You will have heard why."

"I hear a good deal on the market."

"Then you will have heard that I fed my husband his fever."

"I heard you sat up with it."

"I did. That is both stories." She holds out the second cup, and after a moment Thida takes it, because refusing it would be an answer, and she does not yet know what the question is. It is hot and tastes of smoke and ginger. "Sit or stand. I shall not be offended by either. I will be brief, because this house has ears, and not all of them are paid by me."

Behind the screen of cranes something stirs: a child turning over in sleep, the small damp noise of a mouth. A foot comes out under the screen's lower edge, bare and brown, with a plaster on the heel, and goes back. Channary's eyes do not move toward it. They go with great care somewhere else. Thida, who has already seen, looks at the lamp.

"I want one sealed jar," Channary says, "carried from Low Tamber to the Mother Gate and poured into the pool there. It must be done before the fortieth day from the dawn it was drawn. That leaves thirty-nine days and the rest of this night. After that, the Drawing sours. It goes dull, and then there is nothing in it at all."

"The Gate is two hundred and seventy-two miles."

"Yes."

"In the dry. Against the current, with the Salt Line at sixty-eight and the Hen's Ladder at ninety-five." Thida sets down the cup. "Seven miles a day if nothing goes wrong. Something always goes wrong."

"You have calculated it already."

"I calculate everything. It doesn't mean I'm going."

"No." The widow turns a sheet of paper a quarter turn so that it lies parallel with the edge of the table. "I am told you have a saying. Pay the toll and ask me nothing. I'm a ferry."

"It's a true thing."

"Then I offer the toll, and I shall not be asked what is in the jar."

"A Drawing."

"Yes."

"Of whom?"

"You have held his tin."

"I held a net-mender's tin. I'm asking what's in the jar."

"A man's memory, drawn at his own wish, as he died." The voice does not rise. It never rises. It goes down instead, to a flat dry register like a hand laid on a table. "More than that I will not say, and I would rather you did not guess aloud. Guessing in this house is expensive."

"Then no." Thida stands. "A ferry carries the dead, and what the dead leave to their kin. It doesn't carry secrets. A secret has a side, Widow. The minute I carry it, I'm on it."

"He is dead. It is what he left."

"Not to you. To the mother of the pin."

The long hands go still on the lacquer. It is the first time in the whole interview that they have done so, and Thida watches it with the cold interest of someone who has spent twenty years watching water for the places where it changes its mind.

"So the priest told you," Channary says.

"The note told me. He wrote it in the same words to you, I suppose."

"In the same words. And more." The widow does not look away. "He kept that pin eleven years, Mistress Vorn. He wrote to me that he held your daughter."

The tea in the cup is still turning, in a slow circle, from the setting down.

Thida finds that she has not moved. The brazier ticks. Beyond the swags a frog tries one note and thinks better of it. Her left hand has gone to her hip and closed on the shape in her belt-pouch, the bone, the three small cuts, and she feels the lamplight on the back of her neck. She does not turn round. If she turns round she will have to have a face.

"When?" she says, to the wet black cloth.

"He did not say. I have read the letter more times than I care to confess. It says that he held her. It says that he has kept the pin eleven years, and meant to leave it for you when he was gone. It doesn't say how, or where, or why. I am sorry. I would have it say more."

"Where is the letter?"

"Burned. I don't keep letters from dead men. It is a habit of the house."

Now she turns. Channary's face has not changed. "That's convenient."

"It is not convenient, Mistress. It is a fire in a brazier at four o'clock in the morning, after I had learned every word of it by heart. I can say it to you. I cannot produce it." The widow lays her two hands flat. "I am sorry. I did not say it to be cruel. I said it because it is the only coin I have that I think you will take."

It's true, and it is outrageous, and Thida hates her for it with a clean direct hatred that is almost a relief. She sits. The chair is a low black one and she has to fold her long legs under her, boots and all, like a heron.

"Why me?"

"Because he asked for you. He named you in his letter, by the pin, by the Steps, by the boat. He knew the dawn rota. He knew the dawn boat." A tiny pause. "He knew that you do not drink. In a country where half the guild owes a licensed dreamer money, that is rarer than honesty. I made enquiries, Mistress Vorn. The dreamers speak of you with a kind of professional despair. There is not one of them who has not tried to sell you your own daughter in a thimble."

Thida says nothing. There was a man at Pa Tomloy, the year after, with a tray of thimbles and a face like a kind uncle. She had put him in the water, she remembers, with his tray. She had been forty yards from the bank before she noticed she had done it.

"And because," Channary goes on, "a ferry on charter is the one vessel on this river that the gaugemen may inspect but may not seize from. Carrier's peace. It is the old law, older than the Wardens, and they have never dared break it, because the day they do, no boat on the Amaru will carry them. Your neutrality is my cover. It is not much of a roof. But it is the only one left standing."

"And the third reason."

"There is a third?"

"There's always a third."

"He wrote to me because the Warden's steward had traced him to the Salt Marsh." The voice drops further. "Not by name. By the shape of a life: a man with no past, who mends nets with the wrong hand, who pays his debts in silver. He had weeks, he said, not years. He could not carry it himself as far as the Gate. Someone would have to."

"Carry what?"

"I have told you what I will tell you."

"You've told me nothing."

"I have told you a great deal. You have not yet decided to be offended." Channary lifts the second sheet from the table. "I will pay double the river toll for the whole distance, half at the quay tomorrow and half on the Gate's step. I will give you a charter under my seal, which puts you under carrier's peace from the moment you sign. The guild will be bound to honour it. And I will pay you in whatever else you please, Mistress, though I do not know what that would be."

"You could ask kindly."

"I am asking you to be useful, not kind." There is no heat in it at all. "Kind people have been very little use to me this year."

Thida's gaze goes, without her telling it to, to the cranes on the screen. A child's foot has come out again, brown and grubby. It's the smallness of the sole that does it. She has not looked at a child's sole in eleven years, and it is very much the size she remembered.

"Why can't you go?"

"Because my household is watched. Because if I leave Tamber Channel, the invitation takes effect that same hour." Channary says it as she might say the price of rice. "The Lords have asked that my son be guested at their court at Nine Sluices, for the dry. For his health. It is a very great honour. I have written to say that I am sensible of it."

"And if you stay?"

"If I stay, I am the Crown Widow in her mourning, and no one need take anything from her." The squared cup is turned a quarter inch. "You see how well it is arranged. They have thought of everything. They have not thought of a ferry."

"You're selling a ferry to save a boy."

"I am buying a ferry. The boy, I shall keep." For one second the voice has the ghost of a crack in it, and it is closed so fast and so exactly that Thida cannot swear it was ever there. "Take the paper. Read it on your own table. My barge stays on Tamber Channel until tomorrow night. After that, I shall have found another, and I shall be sorry, and she will not do half so well."

Thida takes the charter. It is a roll of heavy paper tied with black cord, and it weighs less than the tea. She gets to her feet in sections, as the old do. At the cranes she stops. The foot has gone back. A small voice from behind the screen says, quite clearly and without waking, "Not the duck." Channary's face changes. It is brief, like the passage of a bird's shadow over water, and it is the only thing in that hall that Thida does not wish to have seen.

"Good night," says Thida, and goes out through the wet cloth into the dark.

* * *

Her bird is waiting on the sill.

The guild keeps a pigeon for every berth, trained to the holder's window, and hers is a speckled hen she has never named. It sits on the shutter-ledge with its head under its wing, where it must have sat since the afternoon, waiting for a door to open. A quill is bound to its leg. Thida takes it from the pigeon's leg by lamplight, at the table, with the charter still in her belt, and breaks the grey wax. The seal is a gauge-rod with a notch cut in the head. She has seen that notch on every Marshal's letter in the delta. She has never seen it on one addressed to her.

> By the Guild of Ferrywomen of Low Tamber, on the writ of the Marshal of Waters: the berth-rights of Thida Vorn, of the barge *Long Patience*, are frozen pending inquiry into the withholding of a dead man's goods. The holder will loose her blue and wait. R., for the Guild.

Rotha's initial is wobbling. She looks at the word *withholding* for some time.

*A pin, perhaps. The ordinary things.* A skiff fifty yards off with a man in it who made a mark on a tablet and nodded. Kossi asked for a pin, and she said hooks, and then he went away and wrote to the guild, because it is not in him to do otherwise. Because for sixteen years he has been taught that if you want a thing done, you do it courteously, and then you do it.

A frozen berth means no moorage at any guild pier. No fares, no cargo, no hall-nights. The guild has never hanged a woman or drowned her; it ends her quietly, by withholding the places where she could be. Without her berth she is a boat without a river.

She holds up her left wrist. The cord there is grey, and at the bottom of its twenty years of knots sits the blue one: a small tight knot of thread the colour of a kingfisher's back, tied by her guildmother on the day she was first given a pole. It has never been undone. It is not hard to do. A slip, and a pull on the tail, and the loop falls open, and the blue hangs loose from the grey like a vein from a wrist.

She does it because the paper says so. She has always done what papers say. The thought arrives complete and quite cold, and she puts it somewhere she will not have to look at it.

The back room of the house is dark. She does not light the lamp. The indigo curtain is drawn aside, as she left it this morning, and in the doorway she stands in the heat that comes off the thatch. She can make out the shelf. The frog. The comb. The whistle. The bare hand's width at the end, which is a different dark from the rest. Nothing in the room is moving. Nothing in it has moved for eleven years, and she has made herself the keeper of that stillness the way Kunthea keeps the Steps.

*He held your daughter.*

She puts her hand on her belt, on the shape of the pin, and her scarred palm feels the three small grooves through the leather.

"Yes," Thida says.

Her voice is odd in the room, dry and small and too loud. Nothing answers. The shelf stands. The sky outside is as black as the mourning cloth, and the frogs have started again somewhere out on the channel, as if nothing had happened.

"Yes," she says again, to the empty room, to the place at the end of the shelf. "All right."

She goes back to the table. She unrolls the charter and weights its corners: the lamp, the tin, the dipper, and last of all the pin, laid down with its three notches turned up. The paper is plain, as Channary said. *From Low Tamber to the pool at the Mother Gate, two hundred and seventy-two miles, one sealed jar, delivered before the fortieth day.* A heron over water in black wax. A space for her name. She dips the pen.

Her signature has always been small and quick, the signature of a woman who believes a paper might change its mind. It goes down on the line in one stroke, *Thida Vorn*, and the end of the blue thread, loose from the cord on her wrist, trails across the wet ink and leaves a faint mark through the *V*, the way a hand leaves a mark on a window.
