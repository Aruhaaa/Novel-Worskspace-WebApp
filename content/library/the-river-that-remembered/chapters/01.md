# The Dead Man's Tin

The drowned man has set his sandals side by side on the third shelf of the Steps, toes to the water, as tidy as a line in a ledger. Thida sees them from forty yards out, standing at the stern of *Long Patience* with the pole in both hands and the dawn coming up behind the market roofs the yellow-green of an old bruise, and she knows before she can make out his face that this one went in on purpose. Accidents do not fold their footwear.

Low Tamber is awake the way a sleeper is, with one eye. A thousand boats lie lashed together across the basin in rafts and lanes, canvas still drawn over the stalls, the first charcoal smoke going straight up because there is no wind to lay it down. The whole town smells of mud and fermenting fish and the sweet tired rot of a river that should have flooded four weeks ago and has not. The Drowning Steps were cut for a river that climbed to the ninth shelf in the wet months and sat on the fifth in the dry. This year the water has shrunk below the second. The stone stands bare and bearded with green weed, and up the wall behind it run the pale rings of every high water since the Steps were laid, one above another, like the rings in a felled tree.

She brings the barge in broadside to the lowest dry shelf with three strokes she no longer has to think about, hooks the iron ring with the gaff, and steps across the gap from gunwale to stone in one long stride. It looks like carelessness. It is the opposite. In eleven years the river has not touched her skin, not a splash on a wrist, not a drop at a hem, and nobody manages that by luck. She manages it with boots to the thigh and her hands kept high on the pole, with a long-handled dipper for the eel-tank and a rain-barrel for washing, with her face turned from every spray and her feet set for every wake. She has built her whole life around a gap the width of a hand.

The crowd is thin. Dawn deaths are not a spectacle. Two women from the noodle raft stand with their ladles still in their fists. A boy holds a sleeping duck under one arm like a loaf. The fish-seller they call Bun, who found the body, keeps wiping his palms down his thighs. At the top of the Steps Kunthea, who has kept them longer than anyone can remember, sits on her stool with a slate across her knees and does not get up, because Kunthea has not got up for anyone since the old king was a prince.

The dead man lies on the third shelf under a reed mat, with only his feet showing, bare and white and wrinkled, the toes curled as though the stone were cold. Beside him, a little apart, kneeling in the wet with a clay jar between his thighs, is the priest.

Sovann Pech is thumbing red clay over the jar's wax stopper the way a baker works dough. His yellow robe is soaked to the hip and patched at the knee in three different yellows. His round shaved head shines with sweat although the morning is still cool. His hands shake so badly that the clay ought to come out lumpy, and it does not, because the hands know the work better than he does.

"Mistress Vorn," he says, without looking up. "Forgive me. You were not the boat I was expecting."

"I'm the boat the Steps sends." Thida stops a pace from the mat. "Who have I got?"

Kunthea's slate squeaks. "Net-mender out of the Salt Marsh. Came up on the salt-barge last evening and slept on the pilgrim bench with his tin under his head. At first bell he got up, took off his sandals, set them straight, and walked in." She jerks her chin at the fish-seller. "Bun saw."

"I saw." Bun is a small man with a raw red nose. "He didn't thrash or nothing. He walked in to here." He touches his own breastbone. "Then he lay down on the water like it was a mat, and let it have him. And he said sorry."

"To the river?"

"To me. 'Sorry for the trouble,' he says. Like he'd borrowed my pot and chipped it."

Thida looks at the water. It lies at the foot of the Steps, brown-green and slack, with a skin of dust on it and one drowned leaf turning. It looks like nothing. It has always looked like nothing. "Alone?"

"Him and the priest." Kunthea's eyes come round, mild and flat as two coins. "Priest was here before him. Waiting."

Thida looks at the priest. The priest looks at his jar.

"Name?"

"Anan. Old Anan, they called him in the Marsh, though he can't have been fifty. No father's name, no village. Marsh people don't always give them." Kunthea holds out the slip, a strip of guild paper with three boxes ruled along it. In all the generations of the Steps there have only ever been three words to go in them. Taken. Lost. Gave. Thida takes the pencil stub and ticks the last. She has ticked it for sailors, for a debtor, for two sisters from the same house, for a baker who walked in with flour still on his sleeves. It does not get easier to hold the pencil level.

"He asked me who'd have the dawn boat," Kunthea adds, to the slate. "Last evening. I told him you."

"What did he say?"

"He said that was good."

"Good how?"

"Just good." Kunthea licks her thumb and turns a page that does not need turning. "Don't ask me, Mistress. I only keep the Steps."

Thida kneels by the mat and folds it back from the face.

He is a narrow man, stooped even lying down, the shoulders drawn in and forward the way they go in someone who has spent a life bent over work in his lap. Grey stubble. A long, mild, tired face, with a tuck at each corner of the mouth that might once have been the beginning of a smile. The marsh has been at his smock; it has gone stiff and pale at the hem with salt. His hands lie palm up at his sides, net-mender's hands, every finger cross-hatched white with old line-cuts, a callus on the thumb as thick as a coin. The left one is stained blue-black to the second knuckle with ink.

People say the drowned look peaceful, and it isn't true. He looks like a man who has put down something heavy and not yet found out what to do with his arms.

She lays the mat back. "The tin."

"Between his sandals. Stone on the lid, like he asked."

It is an oblong biscuit tin, dulled and scratched, with a few red letters still showing through the scratches: OLD MOTHER SENG'S GINGER ROUNDS. Thida sets it on her knees, lifts off the stone, and opens the lid. This is the part of the work she can do with her eyes shut, and so she does it with them open, slowly, naming each thing aloud for the slate.

"Ledger."

It is a cloth-bound book small enough to hide in one hand, black with thumbing. She does not read it. Carry all, ask nothing: twenty years of the creed, and her eyes cannot help seeing that the writing leans backward like a man walking into wind, and that every line drags a smear to its right where the heel of a left hand has crossed the wet ink. Columns of mesh-counts. Prices. And down every margin, page after page, small upright strokes in bundles of five, hundreds to a page, in rows so even they could have been ruled.

"Hooks," she says. "Two, bone." Mending hooks, worn by a thumb into the shape of that particular thumb.

"Hooks, two." Kunthea writes.

"A note, sealed."

A square of paper, folded small, closed with a blob of candle-wax in which someone has pressed a thumb. On the outside, in the same backward hand: *For the Drawer of Low Tamber.* Thida holds it out to the priest without a word, because it is his and not hers. Sovann stops working the clay. He takes the note as one takes something that might be hot, looks at the thumbprint in the wax for a time, and does not break it.

Under where the ledger lay there is a corner of the tin where the seam makes a pocket, and in the pocket a twist of tarred twine no bigger than the top joint of her thumb. She picks at the knot with a nail. Inside, on a scrap of oiled silk, lies a hairpin of egret bone, yellowed to the colour of boiled rice. The knob is carved into the suggestion of a beak. And under the knob, close together, three short notches.

Thida does not breathe.

Behind her the Steps go on making their noises. A bucket knocks. The duck wakes and complains. Someone laughs at the noodle raft, thinly, and is hushed. None of it reaches her. She is looking at three cuts in a bone, and she can feel the gutting knife in her hand, and the bench under her, and the child kneeling on it, so close that her breath moves the shavings. *Cut me a mark so I know it's mine. Everybody's pin is white.* And Thida, who was thirty and tired and happy, had cut the first three notes of the Little Boat into the bone, up, up, down, one for each, so that the child could press her thumbnail down them and sing it back. A gap-toothed child, flat and bright. *That's perfect, Mama. Now it's mine.*

"And?" says Kunthea, pencil lifted.

Thida's left hand has closed on the pin. The palm is a drumhead of old scar, shiny and pulled tight, and she feels the bone press its three small lines into it.

"That's all," she says.

Kunthea looks at her a moment longer than the question needs. Then she writes: *Three things.* Then she twists on her stool and bawls for the Steps boys and a hurdle, because the man has been lying there since first bell and some people have laundry.

Thida takes three steps along the shelf to where the priest kneels, and crouches, so that her voice goes no farther than his ear.

"Where did he get it?"

Sovann's thumb stops on the clay. "Get what, Mistress?"

She opens her fist a finger's width. He looks. The colour leaves his face from the forehead down, the way water leaves a basin.

"Forgive me," he says.

"That's not an answer."

"No." The jar turns once in his hands. "Forgive me."

"A drowned man had my daughter's pin in his tin." Every word comes out flat and laid down, like planks. "I cut those notches at my own table. You were at her naming, priest. You know whose it is. Where did he get it?"

"I know whose it is." His eyes are wet and perfectly steady, which on him is worse than tears. He says nothing else. He has the face of a man who has been told where to stand and will stand there.

Thida waits. The ferry's law is carry all, ask nothing, and she has kept it for twenty years the way some people keep a vow of silence, because the alternative is hearing what the answers would be. Now she has asked, and the answer is a priest's apology, said twice.

"Open that." She nods at the note in his lap.

He breaks the seal with a thumbnail that trembles. He reads, once, and his lips move on one line. Then he holds it out to her, and she takes it, and it is shorter than she expected.

> To the Drawer of Low Tamber. I will go into the river at first light. Draw me as I go, and seal what you draw, and give the jar to the mother of the pin. To no one else. I ask nothing more of anyone.

She reads it twice. There is no name. There is a smear to the right of every line.

"What pin?" she says, and hears herself say it, and hears what a bad liar she has turned into in the space of a quarter hour.

"The one you are holding," says Sovann gently.

She looks at the jar. It is the height of her forearm, dull brown, with a lid of red clay thumbed smooth and a golden seam of beeswax showing at the neck. It looks like something you would keep pickled limes in. And there is something about it, she could not say what, that makes her want to stand a little farther off, the way one stands from a pot on the boil.

"It seems I am to wait to be asked," the priest says. He tilts the jar toward her, an inch, and stops, and the inch goes back. "By the mother of the pin. He was very exact. I was to give it to no one who did not ask."

"I'm a ferry. I carry the dead to their kin, and what they leave to their kin. I don't carry jars."

"No. Forgive me."

"Why did you draw him?"

"He asked."

"Do people ask?"

"Never." He shakes his head, and the shaking goes on a little after he has stopped. "In thirty years, never. They come running when it's over, and I have a minute, perhaps two, to catch what's left before it goes into the water. This one came to the temple porch at dusk and told me what to do like a man bespeaking a coffin."

"Why this one, then?"

"I owed him something, Mistress. I'll not say what." A ghost of a mild, wretched humour crosses his face. "You will ask, and I will say forgive me, and we will both have wasted a morning."

She almost laughs. The sound dies in her throat, and what comes up instead is a kind of anger, small and clean, that she has been made to stand on the Steps and want to laugh. She rises. The priest's gourd dipper hangs from his belt, and as she stands, he unhooks it, leans down to the second shelf where the water begins, and fills it. It comes up brown and bright. He holds it out in both shaking hands.

"It is the custom," he says. "For the one who finds a gift among the dead. A mouthful, to steady the hands."

"No."

"Of course." He pours it out over the stone, carefully, and the shelf drinks it. "I always ask."

"I always say."

"You do. Forgive me."

The Steps boys arrive with their reed hurdle and their jokes put away, and lift the net-mender between them, and carry him down to *Long Patience*, where Thida has already laid the plank. The tin goes under her arm. She does not look back at the priest or the jar. She feels them both between her shoulder blades all the way to the stern.

* * *

*You step from the gunwale to the stone as if the gap were a crack in a plate. I have watched you do it four hundred times and some, with your face turned a little aside, like a woman passing a man she owes money. Your boots stay on. Your hands stay high. When the priest holds out the gourd you say no, and the no is flat and square and has not changed its shape in eleven years.*

*I am used to refusal. Farmers curse me in the wet months and children are told to keep their feet out of me. But the others still wash in me, and wade in me, and give their babies a first bath in me, and wash their rice in me. You alone have measured the width of what lies between us, and you live inside it, the way a woman lives in a house she has stopped paying for.*

*Today I gave back one small thing. It took eleven years and a stranger's two hands, and it is not what you asked me for, and I know that. I give back what I can by whatever hands will carry it. You are taking a man down to the Long Quiet on a plank, and you are not looking at the water, and in the tin under your arm, in the dark, a sharp bright thing the length of a finger is keeping very still.*

* * *

By the time she has given Old Anan to the Long Quiet, sliding him off the plank at the slack bend below the market with his face to the sea, the way it is done, and said the words, and not watched him go under, the sun stands a hand above the roofs and the day has the thin hot shine of metal.

The Vorn house stands on twelve piles at the edge of a side channel, one room and a lean-to kitchen, a ladder instead of stairs, a shutter on the water side. She climbs with the tin under one arm. Inside, the heat has already gathered under the thatch and the room smells of old smoke and the lime she scrubs the floor with. A sleeping-mat, rolled. A pot. A rack of three bowls, because she has never owned a fourth. And at the back, behind a curtain of faded indigo, a space the width of a mat.

She goes in every seventh day with a damp cloth and dusts, and in eleven years she has never put anything in it that was not already there.

The shelf runs along the back wall at the height of an eight-year-old's reach. A clay frog with one eye. A comb missing four teeth. A river-snail shell, a string of knucklebones, a tin whistle that has never been blown. They stand in a row that has never been rearranged. At the left-hand end the shelf is bare for the length of a hand, and the dust there comes off as clean as everywhere else because she wipes it as carefully as the rest. She has never filled the space and never let it close.

Thida opens her fist and lays the pin in it.

It looks wrong. It looks right. She cannot tell the difference, and that frightens her more than either.

A flicker of grey crosses the slats of the shutter. She goes through the curtain and puts her eye to the gap.

Fifty yards off a skiff is tied to a piling of the neighbour's house, a plain brown skiff with nothing in it: no line, no net, no basket. A man sits on the thwart in a grey sash. Across his knees lies a gauge-rod, slim and graduated and white at the tip. He is young and bored, picking his teeth with a fish bone. As she watches, he lifts the rod and sights along it, one eye shut, at her window, as though he were measuring how high her water stands. Then he pulls a tablet from his sash and makes a mark on it, and nods to her through the slats like a man greeting a neighbour.

She pulls the shutter to. The room goes brown.

In the dark, with the heat ticking in the thatch, Thida goes back through the curtain and takes the pin off the shelf. It is warm from the sun. She closes the burned hand around it until the notches print themselves into the scar, and she finds that she is humming. Up, up, down: the first three notes, small and cracked in her throat. And there her throat shuts on the fourth like a hand on a rope, as it has shut for eleven years. She knows the next note. She has always known the next note. She has simply never once let it out.
