# Neutral Ground

The clerk ties a black knot into Thida's wrist-cord with the care of a man dressing a wound, and says, without looking up, "It isn't yours, you know."

"What isn't?"

"The tin." Rotha draws the knot tight with his teeth, which is not how it is done. The cord on her wrist is grey and thick with twenty years of knots, each one a death carried or a fare delivered, and the new black bead sits among them like a seed in a loaf. "Belongings go to next of kin. I have the name here."

The guild-house at Low Tamber is a single long hall on a raft of tarred timber, riding at the edge of the market. Its rafters are hung with rope, coil upon coil of it, grey and tan and tarry. Along one wall runs the Board of Knots, where a copy of every living ferrywoman's cord is hung in hemp the thickness of a thumb, two hundred of them in rows, and in the dark back corner the black-tied ones, the cords of the dead. The place smells of tar and cold rice. Rotha has kept it for fifteen years from a high desk by the window, a thin man with ink on his lip who cuts his own hair, and he is looking at the register open in front of him with the fixed attention of someone who prefers it to her face.

"Kin," Thida says. "A net-mender from the Salt Marsh with no father's name."

"Next of kin." He turns the register. One line in a palace hand, upright and cold, and under it a small black seal. "Channary Ruan. The Crown Widow."

She reads the line twice. "When did this come?"

"First light. A girl in mourning, at the door before the lamps were out."

"First light." The man went into the water at first bell. The girl would have had to leave her house while he was still walking down the Steps. "Before he was cold."

"I log what I'm brought, Thida." The pen in his hand has no ink on it. "If I asked what everything meant I'd be a clerk of nothing."

"You're a clerk of nothing now."

He flinches, a little, which she regrets and does not take back. "It's the rule. Carry all, ask nothing. It's on the wall, over the door. You were the one who read it out at my first hall-night, if you remember, when the old clerk was too drunk to stand."

She does remember. It is a good rule. It has kept her in work and out of trouble for twenty years, and this morning, for the first time, she feels it sit in her mouth like a stone she has been told to hold.

"I'll keep the tin until her people send for it," she says. "That's the rule too."

"That's the rule." Rotha will not meet her eye. "Mind how you go, Thida."

* * *

The wall above the Drowning Steps serves as a market for anyone with a mat and something to sell, and at this hour it is a thin one: a woman with limes, a man selling paper charms against the salt-cough. At the far end a boy has spread thirty or forty sheets of mulberry paper under river-pebbles, and he is addressing a couple in pilgrims' white as though they were a crowd.

"Low Tamber to the Salt Line, sixty-eight miles, drawn this week. Every channel as the water stands this morning, not as it stood when your grandmother's chart was inked. Here." A black finger. "Mud. Here, mud. Also here. And this one looks like a channel and is a liar, and four boats have gone aground in it since the new moon, which I mention only because I'm not charging extra for the warning."

He is thirteen, perhaps, all wrists and elbows, in a grey wool coat cut for a man twice his length. The sleeves are rolled four turns and still swallow his hands to the knuckle, and the hem flaps about his shins. A fishhook hangs from his left ear. His fingers are black with ink to the second joint.

The pilgrims buy. Thida, passing, does not mean to look, and looks. She finds the bar below Pesh Alo drawn where it lies and not where the guild charts put it. She finds a spit off the fortieth mile that was open water a month ago. She is bent over the corner of the sheet, looking at a bend she poled a hundred times, before she notices that she has stopped.

"Eleven seconds," the boy says.

"What?"

"You've looked at that corner eleven seconds and you haven't said it's wrong. Nobody does that. They say it's wrong first, and then they look." He sniffs. "I counted."

"Is it wrong?"

"It's right. That's why I'm telling you something for nothing." He glances along the wall, once, the quick glance of a boy who has been taught to check for anyone who might be listening. "Somebody paid me to count your berth."

Thida straightens. "Count it how?"

"Who ties up. Who steps off. Who steps on. Two coppers a day for three days." His shoulders roll, and the coat travels with them a moment late. "Eleven callers. Nine of them the well-man."

"Who paid you?"

"A man who said please."

"That's half of Low Tamber."

"Not the half that tips." He grins, and the grin makes him look nine and a hundred at once. "Ife Lamin. Charts, a bit of counting, anything that can be written down by Thursday. I'd do you the Salt Line for a silver, but you've the look of a woman who's going to say she knows this river."

"I do know this river."

"Everyone knows it. That's what the Hen's Ladder says about every boat it eats." He stops, as if he had put his foot somewhere he had not intended to. "Anyway. Now you know who's counting."

"Why tell me?"

"I'd rather be paid by two people than one." It comes lightly, a boy's dry shrug of a sentence. "And I didn't like him. And you looked eleven seconds."

She finds a copper in her belt and flips it onto his mat. "That's for the chart I'm not buying."

"Is it a tip?"

"It's a toll."

He bites it, which makes her think of the hooks in the tin, and puts it away. She walks on. It is only at the end of the wall that she lets herself notice she has been humming, and stops.

* * *

Her berth is the nineteenth on the west pier, a slot between a grain lighter and a boat that has been for sale since before the old king died. *Long Patience* sits in it low and patient, blue tiller shining. At the foot of the pier a man waits with his back to the Drowning Steps.

He straightens off the piling as she comes, and takes off his hat. It is the hat she sees first, because gaugemen do not take their hats off to ferrywomen, and then the shoulders, because he has grown into them. At seventeen he had the build of a boy handed a man's frame to carry before anyone taught him its balance. At thirty-three he moves like someone who has been taught nothing else. The grey sash across his chest is embroidered with a gauge-rod in white thread, and the Marshal's silver pin rides at the knot. His forearm, bare below a rolled sleeve, carries the tattoo: a ladder of blue-black ticks climbing from wrist to elbow. In a skiff against the pier, two of his men are examining the sky.

"Mistress."

"Kossi."

It is a small thing, the name, said in the voice she used across the stern when he was twelve and bailing. She sees it land. His squint flickers. The tattooed hand closes on the hat brim and opens again.

"Marshal, Mistress. For the men's sake."

"They can't hear."

"They can see my face."

She looks at his face. She would not have said there was anything on it. But the men in the skiff are studying the sky with such care, and she sees that he is right.

"Marshal, then." She doesn't give it as a gift. "What does the Marshal of Waters want with a ferry at breakfast?"

"A tin came off the Steps this morning. A net-mender. I'm told he walked in."

"He gave himself. That's the word on the slip."

"Gave." He turns it over. "It's a good word."

"It's the only one the Steps have."

"I'd like to see what he left."

"The guild-house has put it down to the Crown Widow. You'd best ask her."

"I'm not taking it. Looking." The courtesy never leaves his voice; it is the courtesy of a man carrying a full bowl across a crowded room. "The Lords like to know what drifts into their water. A man who dies with a Drawing on him is irregular."

"Is this the Lords' looking," Thida says, "or yours?"

"Is there a difference?"

"I used to think so."

Something crosses his face and is gone. She sets the tin on the head of a piling, lifts the lid, takes out the ledger, and holds it to him. He takes it as though it might bite. The rest of the tin she leaves as it is: two bone hooks lying in the dark like a pair of pale commas.

He thumbs through the pages. "Mesh-counts. Prices." His thumb stops at the margin, where the small upright strokes march in their bundles of five. "What are these?"

"I haven't read it."

"No. You wouldn't." He closes the book. "That's your whole creed. Carry all."

"Ask nothing."

"It's what you taught me."

"I taught you to coil a rope."

"That as well." He turns the ledger over in his big hands. "I still coil it the way you showed me. The way it wants to lie, and not the way I want it. It's the only thing I do that way."

She says nothing. She has never known what to say to the things Kossi says when he forgets the sash.

He bends and looks into the tin. Hooks. "Is there anything else, Mistress?"

"What would there be?"

"A net-mender's tin." He smiles. It is a good smile, and she remembers it on a boy of twelve who had just discovered he could not sink. "I'd have thought a scrap of string. A pin, perhaps. The ordinary things."

"Hooks," she says. "Two."

He holds her eye a little longer than the answer needs. Then he hands back the ledger, and she takes it and lays it in the tin and closes the lid on the lie.

"A word," Kossi says. "Not the Marshal's."

She waits.

"Keep your head down, Mistress. Same as eleven years."

The morning goes on around them: gulls, a pole knocking, someone calling the price of limes. Eleven years is how long it has been, and she knows precisely what he means, and would rather not name it even in her own head, where she is the only one listening.

"I don't know what you mean," she says.

"No," Kossi says. "I know." He puts his hat on. He steps down into the skiff, and the men stop looking at the sky and take up their oars. He sits with his back to the Steps and does not, in all the time she watches, turn his head toward them once.

* * *

The Patient Cup is a rice-wine raft at the back of the market, roofed with palm and floored with planks that give a little underfoot, and when Thida finds the priest it is where she knew he would be, which is where the river's priests have always been when they are not at the river: on a bench in the dark at the back, with his yellow robe hung to dry on a peg and a jar of rice-wine between his knees. A long bamboo pipe runs from the jar to his mouth. He takes it out when she sits.

"I am not asking," Thida says.

"Forgive me, Mistress, but you are asking with your whole body." His eyes, close to, are rimmed in red, and he smells of tuak and river. "Will you take a pipe? It's good for what it does."

"What does it do?"

"It makes the morning go away." He considers. "It doesn't make the pin go away. I have tried it on a good many things, and it has never once worked on the thing itself."

Two women at the next jar are sucking at their pipes and talking low, the way a market talks when it believes it is whispering. "—sat up with the fever herself, they say, and not a servant in the room—" "—a good wife—" "—or the other kind—" "—and the boy with his father's ears, poor lamb." Somebody laughs. Somebody else says, "Everybody knows nothing, Dim, that's how you tell it's everybody."

Thida does not turn her head. "Tell me what you can."

"Which is little." Sovann sets the pipe across his knee. "I drew him at his own request, as I said. Last night he sat in my porch at dusk and told me what to do and what to wait for. I stood in the water to the hip with both hands on him until he was gone. It is not the way it's done. It is a way that can kill the Drawer, if the Drawer breathes wrong." He looks at his hands. "I breathed right."

"And?"

"And it held. That is the trouble. It held." He drinks. "The jar holds more than a man."

"How do you mean, more?"

"A drawn man weighs what a man weighs. I have drawn forty-one. I know to a thumb how a jar sits after, you get so you can feel it in the wrist, the way you know a full bucket from half. This one sat low." He turns his hand over. "I lifted it out of the water and my wrist said, this is a man, and something besides. Something small, and bright, laid in with him. The way you would lay a coin in with the rice."

The women at the next jar have stopped talking. Thida feels the quiet along her back.

"What is it?"

"I don't know. Forgive me. I am not a clever priest."

"You are not any kind of priest. That's what they say."

"They are right most days." He looks at her with the awful gentleness of the very tired. "The note said the mother of the pin. You are the mother of the pin, Mistress. I'd carry it to you myself if I thought your hands would take it."

"I'm not a mother of anything."

"Forgive me," says Sovann. "You are."

She is on her feet. She doesn't remember rising. The bench scrapes and the whole raft sways a little, and a pipe-seller looks round. She is, she finds, going to hit a priest in a rice-wine shop, and for no reason except that he said it first, and it is a very long time since anyone said it.

"Ferrywoman Vorn?"

A girl stands at the edge of the raft, fifteen or so, in unbleached cotton with a black band sewn round her arm, her calves wet to the knee from the kettle-skiff she has tied at the rail. She has the careful voice of a child who has been told to say a thing exactly. Her hands hold out something small and black, and the whole Patient Cup looks at it and then, with great speed, does not.

It is a span of black silk, no longer than a hand, knotted back upon itself in a widow's knot, a loop with no beginning and no end, and fastened into the knot a tiny silver fish.

"My lady Ruan asks if the ferry will attend her tonight," the girl says, "at third bell after dark, on Tamber Channel. The house with the mourning cloth. She says you will know it by the quiet."

"And if the ferry has other work?"

"She said you would say that." The girl swallows. "She said to say it isn't a charter she sends for you about. It's a dead man's tin."

Thida takes the silk. It is cool and heavy, and the fish is no bigger than a fingernail, and she finds that her hand does not want to let it go. The two women at the next jar have developed a passionate interest in the bottom of their jar.

"Ah," Sovann says softly, behind her.

"What, ah?"

"Nothing. Forgive me." He has the pipe back in his mouth. Around it, to the jar, he says, "I thought she'd be slower."

She doesn't ask. It's the rule. It has never felt so much like a gag.

* * *

At lamp-lighting she casts off from the nineteenth berth, although third bell is hours away, because she would rather wait on the water than in the house. The market has changed its skin for evening. Paper lanterns come out along the lanes one at a time, orange, then red, until the whole basin glows like the inside of a mouth. She poles through them with the black silk knotted round her belt and the tin in the stern and the pin against her hip.

At the end of the pier a plain brown skiff has been lying since noon, two men in it who have done nothing for hours in a way that required real talent. It casts off when she does. She watches it swing out in a slow wide curve, and for a moment she thinks, good, he's gone, he has other work. Then it takes up her wake, a hundred yards behind her, and she sees that a lantern has been lit at its stern post though there is light enough yet to row by. Small, steady, yellow. Polite as a hand raised in greeting.

She cannot see who sits at its tiller. She doesn't need to.

At the first bend of Tamber Channel she looks back, and it is still there. At the second, too. When she has been an hour on the water, and the last lane lanterns have gone out behind her one by one, and the frogs have started up on the banks and there is nothing left on the whole dark channel but her own boat and the long pole dipping, the lantern is there. It follows her for the whole of that evening at the same patient interval, a small light in the dark, the courtesy of a man who wants her to know exactly where he is.
