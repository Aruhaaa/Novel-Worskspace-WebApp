# Leaf-Gates

On the fourth morning the wind dies, and the palm-sail hangs from its yard like a wet sheet on a line.

Thida lets it hang. She unships the long pole and sets her feet against the stern-bar, and Bakary takes the second pole forward without being told, and the barge goes on at the pace of two people leaning. Beyond the last fish-weir the river changes. It stops. Out in the channels it has been low and slow and complaining, but it has moved; here it lies in a long green lake with no current in it at all, scummed along the edges, smelling of washing left too long in a bucket. Four miles of it lie below the dam. Pole-men call it dead water. For eleven dry seasons the sluices have held the river back, and the river has learned how to wait.

She has not been this way since the Closing. She has taken every other road in the delta in eleven years, the long way round by Ketu Bend, the short way over the mud at Pek, any route that did not run through this water, and she has thought about it so little that she is startled by how exactly her hands remember where to stand.

"It says here," Ife says, from the roof, "that a dam is visible from nine miles in clear weather. It doesn't say what clear is."

"Where does it say that?"

"In my head. Somebody told me."

"Who?"

"A man in a laundry." He draws a line. "It's very good, you know, the dead water. Nothing moves. You could chart it blindfold."

She does not answer. The haze ahead has begun to thicken into something with edges. At first it is a line drawn across the sky with a ruler, a long low black stripe. Then it is not a line but a wall. Then it is nine walls, standing shoulder to shoulder across the whole breadth of the river, each one a stone pier as wide as a house and a pair of leaf-gates between them, black ironwood, forty feet of it going up out of the water like the sides of a throat.

She feels it before she hears it. It comes up through the planks into the soles of her boots, a note too low to be called a sound, the pitch of a very large animal humming in its sleep. The gates under load. Every one of them is holding back a river, and each is singing it. The note goes into her shins and her knees and her back teeth, and then it reaches her left hand, and the scar in the palm draws tight all at once as if someone had closed a fist round it.

Thida goes grey. She knows she has, because Ife says, "You've gone the colour of a pole."

"Pole," says Bakary, from the bow, without turning. He has not spoken in an hour.

"It's a compliment. Poles are sturdy."

She is humming. She hears it as one hears one's own name in another room: up, up, down, small and flat, under the great note of the gates. Bakary has stopped poling. He is looking at her over his shoulder with his tea-coloured eyes, and she stops, and he turns round and puts his eyes on his boots.

It is an odd thing to see, a man that size studying his boots. She notes it. She notes, too, that he has not looked up since the dam came out of the haze.

* * *

The inspection quay is a long raft at the foot of the first pier, and four boats wait in front of them: two rice lighters, a pilgrims' barge hung with white, a boat of timber-cutters sitting so low with teak that it seems to be drowning slowly and politely. Gaugemen go over each one in turn. They wear grey sashes with the gauge-rod stitched in white, and they carry the rods themselves, slim graduated poles with brass ferrules, and they use them like the probing-rods of grave-diggers. They poke the cargo, and write on tablets, and poke it again.

"Eleven on the quay," Ife says, low, from the roof. "Two on the wall. One in the wheel-house. One asleep in a skiff, which is the one I'd marry." He is not looking at any of them. He is drawing the pilgrims' barge with great absorption. "The gates are forty feet, and the guards are four feet from asking."

"Four feet from what?"

"Asking. They've got that look. Like a person at a wedding who's about to say, who's the bride?"

"Be quiet."

"I'm only annotating."

"Priest," Thida says. "The jar."

Sovann opens his eyes. He has been sitting with the basket in his lap and a small, patient smile that does not reach the rest of his face, the smile of a man who has made up his mind to be somewhere else. "Mistress?"

"Out of the basket. They'll go through the husk with a rod."

"Forgive me, I had not..." He looks at his hands. They are holding the basket so tight that the knuckles are two rows of little white stones. "It's only, I don't like it to be moved. It likes to be still."

"It's a jar."

"It's a man and a coin."

"Then he'll have to put up with it."

She lifts the grating of the eel-tank herself. The eels lie in the dark water in a slow heap, thick as wrists, yellow-bellied, turning over each other like the muscles of a very large single animal, and the smell comes up cold and green. The tank is two feet deep. Between the lid and the first eel there is eight inches of brown river.

She stands with her fingers on the grating.

It is eight inches. She has stood next to bigger water than this and not touched it. She has poled over the Long Quiet with the dead on her plank. She has carried a hundred drowned children and not once let a drop reach her skin. And there are four gaugemen aboard the pilgrims' barge, and one boat between them and the quay, and the jar in the priest's arms, wrapped now in oiled cloth with a stone tied to its foot, looks at her with all the patience of a thing that has never been in a hurry.

She does not move. She cannot move her hand. It is a very strange sensation, and not a new one: a hand that belongs to someone else.

Bakary comes aft. He does not say anything. He looks at her fingers on the grating and at the eight inches of water and at the priest with the jar, and then he rolls his right sleeve to the shoulder, slowly, as if he had all day. He takes the jar from Sovann.

"The husk will wet..." the priest begins.

"It'll keep."

He lowers his arm into the tank. The eels part and close around it. They slide over his wrists and his scarred forearm in a long cold glistening ripple, and he does not flinch, only breathes out, once, through his nose. The jar goes down. The eels fold in over it. A minute later his arm comes up streaming, and he stands with the water running off his fingers onto the deck, and Thida, without looking at his face, puts a cloth in the dry hand he is not using.

"Seven," Bakary says, to nobody.

"What?"

"Eels on the top." He is wiping his arm. "I counted. It's enough."

"Thank you," she says, to the grating, and lowers the lid.

* * *

The Gate-Sergeant of the inspection quay is a stout man named Sokun with a boil on his neck and the mild eyes of someone who would much rather be at supper. He comes aboard with a corporal, a woman with a gauge-rod, and a boy to hold the tablet. He reads the charter slowly. His lips move on every word, and when he reaches the heron over water in black wax they stop moving altogether.

"Crown Widow's seal."

"Carrier's peace," Thida says.

"I know what it says." He has a soft slow voice, a sorry voice. "It says look, don't take."

"It does."

"Then I'll look." He rolls the charter and gives it back with both hands, as though it were hot. "Cargo?"

"Rice, mudfish, lime, poles. Eels for Ketu Bend. A priest."

"Whose priest?"

"The Widow's."

"Going where?"

"Upriver."

"How far?"

"As far as the rice lasts."

"You're not going to tell me." He doesn't say it angrily. He says it as one says it will probably rain.

"Pay the toll and ask me nothing," says Thida. "I'm a ferry."

He sighs. "It's a good saying. My mother used it." He points his rod at the stern-shelter. "Corporal."

They are thorough, in a bored, dreadful way. The corporal goes through the rice with her rod and then with her arm. The boy writes down the number of poles. Sokun himself looks into the stern-shelter and the crocks of lime and under the mat where Thida sleeps, and finds the loosened blue and the black silk on her wrist and looks at them for some time, and says nothing. He walks to the bow. The priest sits on the hatch with an empty basket in his lap, packed with husk, and a face like a lamp somebody has blown out.

"You, Brother. Name and temple."

"Ah." Sovann blinks. "Forgive me. It's... Brother Phirun."

"Phirun."

"Phirun of... of Wat..."

"Which wat?"

"Samre!" Sovann says, with the air of a man grasping a rope. "Wat Samre. The half-drowned one. At... at mile a hundred and fifty. Mile... I'm not certain of the mile."

"Wat Samre's a ruin."

"It is," says Sovann. "It is. It is a ruin, Sergeant, and I'm, and I am in ruins, and I..." and his face crumples. It simply folds, like a paper bag in the rain, and the tears come down the round shaved cheeks in perfectly real streams, and he cries in great ugly hiccuping sobs with his mouth open and his shaking hands held out. "Forgive me. Forgive me. I only wanted to... I've never been any good at any... I had a temple, and now I haven't, and... and nobody asks a priest his name, they only ever ask for it to be... forgive me."

Silence on the quay. The corporal stares. The boy has stopped writing. Sergeant Sokun, who would much rather be at supper, goes red to the roots of his hair and says, "All right, Brother. All right. No need. I only wanted it for the tablet," and then, because the priest is still weeping, and the boy is still staring, he gives a gruff professional cough and says, "Put down Phirun," and turns away.

Thida looks at Ife. Ife is drawing, with every appearance of calm, the inside of his own knee.

The Sergeant has gone to the eel-tank. He lifts the lid. His face does something complicated at the smell. "Eels."

"Thirty pounds. For sale at Ketu Bend."

"It's water." He says it a little sadly. "Water in a tank is water out of the river, Mistress. Seven gauges for the tank. It's the Lords' rule." And he reaches for his rod, and dips it into the brown water between the eels.

She counts the rod's progress without looking: two spans, three, four. The graduated stem sinks through the heap of yellow bodies and strikes something solid, a dull knock that goes through the planking. Sokun frowns. He lifts it a little. He puts it down again, and again it knocks.

"There's something in there."

"A stone," says Bakary.

It is the first thing he has said since the quay. He stands at the Sergeant's shoulder with his arm hanging, still wet, hair-plait over the shoulder, and he says it like a man naming a tree. "Ballast. The eels like a stone to lie under."

"Under the eels?"

"Under, and round." Bakary looks at the tank and then at Sokun with perfect blankness. "They're shy."

A long silence. Somewhere at the back of the pilgrims' barge, a gaugeman laughs at something. The water in the tank turns its slow green and muscular turn.

"Two spans," says Sokun, writing. "Seven gauges. Put it on the toll." And he is already turning to the next boat, with a sigh that comes up from the very soles of his boots.

* * *

The Fifth Sluice is the only one of the nine that opens for boats.

They enter it at the third bell, in a line of four. It is a basin of dressed stone two hundred feet long, with a leaf-gate at each end, each leaf black and slick and forty feet high, so near on either side that Thida could touch the weeping stone from the gunwale if she dared to. Lock-hands in grey go along the coping with a chain, running it through iron rings. The lower gate comes down behind them in a long grinding fall, with a sound she feels in her molars, and the world narrows at once to wet stone and a strip of sky. The hum is no longer under her boots. It is round her, in her, in the water and the wood, and the hair on her arms stands up.

She was here, once. She was on the far side of that upper gate, in a line of ferries, with a barge full of silk and fruit and ambassadors, on a bright dry morning with flags on every pole. She remembers the sound, and no more. A row of doors slammed in a house the size of the sky.

She does not look at the east wall. She looks at it, then, because it is the only thing that does not hum. Forty yards of the coping have been rebuilt in stone a shade paler than the rest, the joins still sharp, the moss not yet thick enough to hide them. It is very neat work. She turns her eyes to the north side, where the wheel-house stands.

It is a squat stone hut built out over the basin's lip, and at its door a great wooden wheel as tall as two men stands in its housing, the spokes worn dark by hands. Beside the door, a rope hangs coiled from an iron cleat. It is as thick as her forearm, tarred black, and its end is whipped and frayed from long weather. And a little way up the rope from the whipping, tied not round anything but into the rope itself, where the strands part and fold back on themselves, is a knot.

She knows it. Seven turns round the standing part, seven back over them, the tail tucked under both: a small hard woven barrel, patient, a thing that takes a wright's hands an hour and the rest of a man's life to make look easy. She has seen it on her own mooring-piling. She has seen it on the lashing of her husband's coffin-boat. It has gone the colour of the rope, eleven years of sun and tar, and it is his.

She does not turn her head. But she feels him notice her notice, the way one feels a change in the wind. Bakary stands at the chain with his back to her and his hands on the iron, and for a moment all of him is still, the great shoulders rock-still, and she knows without looking that he is not hauling.

The lock-hands call. The chain rattles, and he takes up the slack, and the barge begins to warp along the wall, inch by inch. His voice, when it comes, is so low that she hears it through the hum rather than over it, a mutter into the wet stone, the voice of a man talking to himself in an empty room.

"Takes a man's whole weight, that wheel. All of it. Hung off the spokes." The chain rattles. "Gate'll come up by stages if there's somebody to hold it. Stage and hold. Stage and hold. You'd want a heavy man."

He stops. She turns her head.

"Bakary?"

"Chain's wet," he says.

It is. It is streaming. She looks at his back, the great bent shoulders and the grey plait, and he does not look round. She thinks of eight inches of brown water, and an arm sliding down into it without a word. She thinks, he knows this place, and then, as she has put away other thoughts, she puts that one away.

"What was that about a wheel?" Ife asks, from the roof, in his friendliest voice.

"Nothing," says Bakary.

"It sounded like something."

"Chain," says Bakary. "Wet."

Ife writes it down.

* * *

The water rises in the basin, silent and steady, a hand's breadth to every few minutes. The walls slide down past the gunwale. The paler stone slides down with them. A last strip of sky goes lemon, then rose, then the grey of a pigeon's back, and the barge climbs through it in the dark stone throat with the lamps unlit, and the water comes up behind her like a breath taken and held.

At dusk the upper gate lifts, a foot at a time, with a long groaning shudder, and a gateman on the wheel throws his weight on the spokes and holds. A foot, and a hold. A foot, and a hold. She watches the black ironwood go up out of the water in stages, streaming, and thinks of nothing at all, and the barge slides out under it into the upper pool with its chain falling away astern in a long cold rattle.

The upper pool is a mile across and as flat as a plate. There is wind on it, at last, a small dry wind from the west. Thida sets the sail and the barge leans and goes, and the hum behind them thins and thins and becomes a thing she can stand. Sovann is asleep on his hatch with his empty basket, red-eyed, faintly smiling. Ife is drawing the gates by memory, tongue between his teeth. Bakary stands in the bow with his back to everything.

She turns at the stern, to look back.

The dam stands against the last of the light, nine black piers and the eight shut gates between them, and in the Fifth the water is going down again for whoever comes next. The lower gate is rising. A skiff is sliding in under it, a plain brown skiff with one lantern lit at its prow and a man at the oars in a grey sash, slipping into the basin behind them as quietly as a hand into a glove.
