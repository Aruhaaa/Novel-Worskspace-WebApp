# The Honest Man

On Wednesday the rain stopped, which in Trevenna is treated as an announcement. By nine o'clock half of Fish Cellar Lane was standing in its doorway with a mug, looking up at a sky that had been replaced overnight by a better one, and the hydrangeas along the wall had gone from slate to a blue so hard it hurt to look at. The gulls, who had sulked on the chimneys for three days, rose in a body, circled the harbour once and settled again, having forgotten what for.

In the five days since the fair the Bindery had taken eleven pounds forty, most of it from a woman who wanted paste for her wallpaper. I had stopped looking at the till. One does not stare at a thing that cannot improve. I stood at the counter instead with one of Mrs Rosevear's hymnals, which wanted a new spine and a talking-to, and Quire lay on the paste shelf in a bar of sun with her belly turned to the ceiling, a position she permitted only on days when nobody had the least wish to touch her.

Tamsin had arrived at eight without being told the hour. She had stood on the step with her hands in her pockets and the bleached stripe of her fringe sticking up like a question, and I had put her in the yard with a ream of waste paper and one instruction, which was to tear it. Along the grain first, then across, and then tell me the difference. It is the first thing Ned ever taught me, in the spring of 1962, in that same yard, with a sheet of Whatman and a look of enormous patience. Through the open door I could hear her at it: a long clean rip; a pause; a short ragged one; a longer pause, in which I knew she was holding the two edges up to the light with her teeth in her lip; and then a small noise that was not quite an oath.

"The grain goes up and down," she called.

"The grain goes the way the paper wants."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only one there is," I said. "Again."

The bell went at ten past ten, and then nobody came in, which is how I knew it was somebody who had not yet decided to.

Imogen Mayne stood on the mat in the black leather jacket, in July, with a brown document case held out in front of her in both hands like a verger with the plate. Her eyes were red at the rims and she had made no attempt to do anything about it. There was a cigarette between two of her fingers, unlit, which she seemed to be carrying on someone else's behalf.

"No," I said.

"I haven't."

"You were going to. Everything in this room is paper or flour, and I should like to keep both."

She looked at the cigarette as if it had been put there without her knowledge, and tucked it behind her ear. It was exactly where a man keeps a pencil. I do not think she knew.

"I've come to show you something," she said. "I'd like you to read all of it before you say anything. Not even 'Well.'"

"How do you know I say 'Well'?"

"Daddy said you did. He said it was the most complete sentence in the English language." She told this to the shelf behind my head. "He said you could close a subject like a lid."

I had not known that Aubrey Trevithick repeated things I said. It is a peculiar sensation to learn that one has been quoted. I came out from behind the counter and turned the sign on the door to Closed, and she watched me do it with the faint surprise of a person who had expected to have to ask.

She set the case on the counter and opened it. "The lining's perished at one corner. Old glue. He's had this case since Cambridge, and I thought, I'll get it put right, because he'd have hated..." She stopped, and began again further along. "I ran my thumb under the flap and it was in there. Flat. Between the lining and the leather, the way you'd keep something you hadn't decided about."

It was a cream envelope of good laid paper, a little soft at the edges from a life spent against a man's other papers. The stamp was already on it, a nineteen-pence stamp, stuck down square in the corner. He had licked the stamp. He had addressed the thing in fountain pen, in the upright hand I knew from every receipt he ever gave me, and then he had put it away, and on one corner a cup had left a brown crescent where he had not noticed it standing.

*H. R. Tregear Esq., Messrs Tregear & Polglase, Boscawen Street, Truro.*

"Loveday's solicitor," I said.

"And the family's. He did the Trevithick wills." She touched the flap, which had been slit along the top with something fine. "I opened it. With a nail file, so the glue would still show it had been shut. I know I shouldn't. I'm his next of kin and I don't know what that allows. I'm taking it to Constable Kitto at one, I promise you I am. I wanted somebody to read it first who isn't paid to." She tried to smile. "Daddy's list of people who weren't paid to be kind to him was a short one."

She gave me two sheets. I read them standing at the counter, with the sun coming through the glass of the door and laying the word BINDERY backwards across my wrist.

> 12th July 1989

> Dear Hugh,

> You will have my valuation of the Penhallow library at the end of the month, and I shall ask you to read it before Loveday does. But there is one item on which I cannot wait, and I put it to you now in plain terms so that you may advise her accordingly. The Carne Rutter is not what it seems.

> I am not yet in a position to say what it is, nor since when, nor at whose hands, and I do not propose to guess in writing. A guess set down in ink has a way of becoming a fact by the time it has crossed a village. I shall have the volume taken apart on Friday in the presence of Mrs Pellow of the Marigold Bindery, who has not flattered me once in twenty-seven years and will therefore tell me whether I am a fool.

> Meanwhile I must ask three things of you. That the rutter is not offered in the September sale under any circumstances. That nobody handles it further. And that nothing of this is said to Loveday, who has loved that book for seventy years and may be spared a few days more.

> I am aware that this is a poor letter. I would rather be accurate than prompt, and I find that I am neither.

> Yours ever, Aubrey

I read it twice, and then I read one sentence a third time, and it was not the one about the rutter.

I have been called a number of things in thirty years in Trevenna. The bookbinder. Ned's widow. The one with the cat. Never, until that moment, the person you send for. It is a foolish thing to be undone by, with so much else lying on my counter, but one does not choose which part of a page goes in.

"Well," I said.

Imogen laughed, a wet, startled sound, and wiped her nose on the back of her wrist. "You're allowed now."

Nobody in Trevenna had ever quite believed that Aubrey Trevithick was an honest man. It was not that they thought him dishonest. It was that he held the Lantern's lease and the regatta purse and a number of other people's old debts, and a man who holds so much must surely have a manner where his scruples should be. I had been as bad as any. On the Friday I found him I had stood in my own press room looking at the rutter and thought: he borrowed my key, he promised to have something to say to me, and here is a book with its insides out and a collector beside it. I had made him a crook inside ten minutes. I had given him a partner, a quarrel, a hammer. It is such a comfortable shape, a crook and his accomplice. A crook is a man who has it coming. And when Garrow had said the same, in his raincoat, I had said "Well" and let him.

All the while Aubrey had been doing the one thing none of the rest of us did, which was to notice. He had held a book up to the light, seen what was wrong with it, and gone to considerable trouble not to say so until he was certain. He had put a stamp on a letter and not posted it, I thought, because he wanted me to see the book first. I was ashamed of every unkind word I had given him, from the hat to the cane, and there had been a good many.

And it turned the whole business round like a coat. If Aubrey was no partner, there had been no quarrel over spoils. Someone had made a false book, and Aubrey had found it out, and someone had learned that he had, and he had been stopped as one stops a sentence. The stamp on that envelope was as dry as the day he licked it. The words had never left the case. So the person who made the false book had learned of them some other way, between the Wednesday and the Thursday night, and I could not think how.

"I was there," said Imogen.

I looked up.

"At the Bindery. On the Thursday. I've been trying to say it to you since the fair and I keep saying something about the weather."

"I know."

She went quite still. "How?"

"Someone saw you go in at five past ten and come out at twenty past. Not the police. Someone with reasons for keeping out of it, whom I shan't name."

"Five past," she said. "Yes." Her mouth worked. "Then you know what I did to the wall."

"I know you did something to a wall."

"I kicked it. Twice. Mrs Pellow, I swear to you, I didn't touch him. I left him alive. He was alive and he was furious, he was in his shirtsleeves with that pencil behind his ear and the lamp on, looking like a boy with a frog, and he wouldn't be got at. I'd gone to have it out. The piano, all of it. I'd gone to stand in front of him until he said one thing that wasn't about accuracy."

"And did he?"

"He said, 'Immy, I can't do this tonight.' He said he had somebody coming at half past ten, and he meant to be kind to them, which was a good deal harder than being right, and he needed all the kindness he had." She was gripping the case so hard that her knuckles stood up. "And I said something I would like back. I said 'Daddy,' in the voice. The one he hated. As if it were a diagnosis. And he said, 'Tomorrow, then. I shall be accurate about the piano tomorrow,' and he shut the door."

In the yard the tearing had stopped. I did not turn my head.

Somebody coming at half past ten. Not a thief with a chisel, not a stranger out of the dark: an appointment, and one Aubrey had dressed himself in kindness for. I thought of a woman in a mac who had gone in without knocking. People do not knock when they have been told the door is open.

"Do you think I did it?" said Imogen.

"No."

"Garrow does, or he'd like to. He's been to the Lantern twice, asking who inherits as if he were asking the time. I do. Me. A house with a roof like a colander and a lease on a pub I'd give Jago tomorrow with a ribbon on." She breathed out through her nose. "He thinks Daddy was in it, you know. I heard him on the stairs: 'Trevithick's hands weren't clean.' That's what they'll put in the paper. That's what they'll say at the funeral, in the car park, with their hats on."

"When is the funeral?"

"Monday, if Garrow will let me have him. Mr Nance has promised a hymn Daddy would have corrected." She hunted for the rest. "I don't only mean the funeral. Everyone here was perfectly civil to him for thirty years and not one of them believed he'd lend them a pencil without wanting it back with interest. I want it said, out loud, in front of them, that he was the only one who noticed. That he was the honest one. I want to bury him as that."

"I am not a person who makes speeches."

"No. You're a person who looks at a thing before she says what it is. He said that too."

I said that I would do what I could. It was not a promise. It was a lid, and we both knew it, and she took it for what it was.

She put her hand inside the jacket and brought out one of the small brown envelopes the police use, and from it a ring. A gold signet, heavy, worn soft at the edges, with a swan cut into the stone and its wings half raised. I knew it as one knows the hand that has passed one change across a counter for a quarter of a century.

"They gave me his things in this, with a receipt in triplicate," she said. "I can't wear it. It goes on my thumb, and I'm not going about with my father on my thumb. I thought you should have it. Not to own. To keep, until it's done."

I took it to the paste shelf and set it down beside Ned's old toffee tin, the Silver Jubilee one, which these days held a great many bone folders and one burned corner of paper. Quire came down from her patch of sun, sniffed the ring, and sat beside it as if she had been posted there.

Imogen lit the cigarette on the step, in the lane, in the sun, with the care of a woman lighting the first candle in a church. Then she went down the hill to see Constable Kitto, and the bell closed behind her.

"You were listening," I said.

"I was tearing very quietly." Tamsin stood in the yard door with a strip of paper in each hand. "That one's with the grain. That one's across. The across one's rubbish, it fuzzes. So it isn't a rule. It's just what the paper does."

"That is the whole of binding."

"Right. So." She looked at the ring and not at me. "Who did he tell?"

"That's a different question."

"How?"

"He told no one. The letter's still got its stamp on. If he'd told someone, it would be someone who is now very careful not to say so." I heard myself, the way I sound when I am being accurate, and it was his voice I had caught. "The question is who knew."

I went to the drawer under the counter and took out Ned's last order book, the black one with the cloth spine he had rebacked himself. I have never been able to read his hand. I do not open it at the pages, and I would sooner be seen in the street without my cardigan. But the inside of the front cover is clean board and paper, and his hand does not reach there. I took the pencil from behind my own ear and wrote, in capitals, so that I should not be mistaken:

WHO KNEW AUBREY KNEW?

Then I underlined it twice, the second time hard enough to dent the board.
