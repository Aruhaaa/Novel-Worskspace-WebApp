# What Jago Couldn't Say

Jago Rowe ducked under the lintel of the police house at ten past midnight as he ducks under every lintel in Trevenna, but this time he did it as though the wood had said something personal.

Demelza had knocked at my door at twenty to twelve. I was standing at the sink with a cloth, wiping up a kettle's worth of water from the hob, and Tamsin was asleep upstairs in the box room under Ned's eiderdown with her boots on, because I had not had the heart to take them off. "I'm letting Jago out," said Demelza. "He's on your way home."

"I am home."

"Then he's on your way to Jago's. Get your cardigan."

We walked down together. Church Lane was empty and smelled of the Co-op's bins and somebody's late roses. She did not talk. At the chapel wall she said, without turning her head, "You've got something. It's on your face like paste."

"It's not something yet."

"Dot."

"Five o'clock tomorrow, in my kitchen," I said. "If I'm right I shall tell you first, before Garrow, before Kit's solicitor, before the Pope. If I'm wrong I would prefer to be wrong in front of nobody."

She considered it as far as the Co-op. "First," she said.

"First."

"Don't be wrong, then," said Demelza.

The police house is the front room of a semi-detached cottage at the foot of the lane, with a blue lamp over the door that has not been lit since 1981, and a cell behind a door that used to be the pantry's. The ficus was drooping. The Queen looked down from her photograph with the expression of a woman who has seen a great many men released and was prepared to wait for the next. Jago sat on the bench with his boots between his knees, pulling the newspaper out of them in long grey handfuls, and the room smelled of old sea and older newsprint.

"There's a form," said Demelza, who stood by the door with her notebook. "I've filled in the form. Everything we have against you, Mr Rowe, is that you got wet. A man you pulled out of the harbour is alive and will go on being alive, with his own money in his pocket and nobody's fist on his head. Being unwilling to say why you were there is not an offence in this country. So I'm not holding you. For lack of evidence. It isn't an apology."

"Constable," said Jago.

"I'd go home, Mr Rowe."

He stood up. The room got smaller. He put his coat on, and sat again to work his foot into the second boot, and it was then, as he bent, that the lamp caught the heel and I saw the painted figure on it, a white 12, slapped on with a brush and gone grey at the edges. Fishermen do it so their boots do not walk off the quay. A white twelve and a rim of salt.

I said nothing. I thought of six pale macs on six pegs, and under them, standing in a line like a polite congregation, a pair of black rubber sea-boots in a damp passage in a granite house.

"I'll walk you home," I said.

"I know the way, Dot."

"Yes. But I know the boots."

* * *

Jago lives at the harbour end of Net Loft Row, in a cottage so full of other people's lobster pots that the kitchen is a sort of wicker cathedral. They go up the walls in stacks, to the ceiling, with the laths in their ribs and the old green twine hanging off, because there is nowhere else in Trevenna to put them and he is not a man who says no to a neighbour. He lit the stove with a sheet of the same newspaper and filled the kettle and put it on the wrong ring, and moved like a big man who has been told off.

I had sat in that kitchen a hundred times. I had sat there with Ned, with a pint, on the night of the Jubilee. I looked at the wall by the dresser where a photograph hung in a plain frame, and I saw it, for the first time in twenty-seven years, instead of the picture of it that I had carried about. A girl of nineteen in a riding hat set at an angle, on a grey horse with its ears back, laughing at whoever held the camera. She had the Penhallow nose. She looked as if the camera belonged to her, which I imagine it did. I had always taken it for a calendar page.

"Leave it, Dot," said Jago, behind me.

"I did leave it," I said. "For eight days. I am told it is a fault."

He said nothing. The kettle began to tick.

"Size twelve boots with a white twelve on the heel," I said. "They stand in the back passage at Penhallow Court under six pale macs. Loveday said they were the gardener's. Loveday has not had a gardener since Suez."

He set down the caddy very carefully.

"That's the size of it," said Jago.

It came out of him as the tide comes up the Hard, slowly, and then all of it. "Thursdays," he said. "I leave Jory the key to the box and the plug on the jukebox at half past nine, and I tell them it's the books. It is the books, for ten minutes. I do the quarter's rents on her kitchen table. Then she makes tea, and we play piquet for coppers, and she cheats, and I do the buckets. There are eleven. They've gone up since March." He moved a hand the size of a frying pan. "Three years, since the winter Ned went. She came into the Lantern in her gumboots, which she hadn't done since 1956, and ordered a small sherry, and said I looked as if I could do with an evening."

"Nineteen fifty-six."

"Aye, you'd not know. You came in 'sixty-two." He looked at the photograph, not at me. "I was twenty-eight, on the pilchard boats. She was forty-one and the daughter of the house, and there was a hay barn, and it got out, because in Trevenna it does. The Chapel took it up. Her father sent me a fortnight's wages in an envelope and a berth on a coaster out of Falmouth, and I went, because I was twenty-eight and thought it was what you did. Three years. When I came back she was Miss Penhallow and I was Jago Rowe and we nodded in the street. Thirty years. Like two ships." He breathed out. "Ned never said one word. He bought me a pint. He was good that way."

"Nobody would mind now, Jago."

"Everybody would mind," said Jago, "and then they'd be kind to us, and I couldn't stand that." He took the kettle off. "She's seventy-four. Let a man keep one thing in this village. Garrow'd have her name in the Truro paper by Sunday with the old story dragged out behind it like a dead dog."

"On the thirteenth," I said.

"Half past nine till midnight. I left by the kitchen door and was home by half past, and nobody saw me, which was the idea."

"Did Morwenna Hext come to the house that night?"

He looked at me then. "Morwenna? No. She does Tuesdays."

"Loveday told me, on Tuesday, in front of Morwenna, that Morwenna sat up with her till midnight on the Thursday. Playing piquet."

Jago's hand stopped halfway to the shelf with the tea in it. "Piquet," he said.

"Yes."

"That was me. I lost sixty pence." He put the caddy down. "Nobody came to that door but me. The bell's the size of a bucket. I'd have heard it in the cellar." The big face under the bald dome worked, slowly, as a winch works, and he said, very quietly, "She put Morwenna in my chair."

"Her hearing aid squealed all through it. I thought it was the aid."

"It's not the aid," said Jago. "It's her. She'll do it lying in the dark with her mouth shut."

I did not say why I thought it mattered, and he did not ask. He is not a stupid man, only a silent one, and there is a way of being silent that is a form of listening. I got my cardigan from the back of the chair.

"It's half past twelve."

"She doesn't sleep. She told me so, in July, with the buckets."

"I'll come."

"You'd frighten her."

"She's not frightened of me. She's never been."

"Then you'd frighten me," I said. "You have been in a cell for twenty-two hours. I should like one of us to arrive."

He took a black rubber torch from a nail by the door, as long as my forearm, and put it in my hand. "She'll be in her gumboots," said Jago. "Don't say."

* * *

It is twenty minutes from the Hard to Penhallow Court if you take the cliff path, and in the dark it is twenty-five, with the sea going on at your left hand like a person breathing in the next room. There was no moon to speak of. The gorse gave out its coconut smell, and something in the hedge went away from me with a rustle. I kept the torch low. I did not rehearse. I have found that if I rehearse a conversation, the other party comes in with different lines, and I am left holding mine like a woman with the wrong key.

There was a light in the kitchen. When I banged the knocker it was not a minute before the bolts went back, and Loveday Penhallow stood in her own doorway with a hurricane lamp held up beside her face, in a flannel nightdress down to her shins, a man's cardigan, and gumboots.

They were on the wrong feet. Each toe pointed away from the other like the hands of a clock at ten to two. I had been told not to say, and I said nothing, and she watched me say nothing with her head on one side.

"Pellow," she said. "It is one o'clock in the morning, you have lost your senses, and I have no sherry."

"Jago is out."

The hearing aid in her ear gave a small, delighted sort of cheep. "Is he? Good. He is a fool, and I am obliged to him." She stood aside. "Come into the kitchen, then, I have the range lit. I do not sleep. Eleven buckets is a very good alarm."

It was the warmest room in the house and the only one with its lamps on. We sat across a scrubbed table with a pack of cards on it, a fan of them half-dealt, as if someone had been called away in the middle of a hand, and I did not look at them.

"He says he was here from half past nine until midnight on the thirteenth," I said. "He says he lost sixty pence at piquet. He says nobody rang the bell."

"He's a gentleman. He will say what is convenient."

The aid screamed. It went up through the scale and stopped. Loveday put a long hand, the beautiful one, flat across her ear, as one would to a child who has told a story at table, and for a time neither of us spoke.

"Oh, Pellow," she said.

"Who asked you?"

"On the Friday. Before nine in the morning, before Aubrey was out of your press room. She stood in my hall in her mac with a cake in a tin, and she said, 'Loveday, dear, if anyone should ask, I sat up with you last night.' She did not say why. I did not ask why." The long hand went down, and she looked at me, and I saw that she was frightened, and had been frightened for a week, in a way that made her look younger than Jago's photograph. "I am a coward, Pellow. You may tell the Chapel."

"Why did you say yes?"

"Because she reads to me. Eleven years on a Tuesday, in all weathers, with Ronald going the way he is. Friendship. And shame." She turned a card face up and turned it down again. "I have known for years that things go out of this house. A snuffbox. The plates from the Hakluyt. A Book of Hours I never liked. Small things. She would borrow them. She always said so. Sometimes they came back, and sometimes not, and I told myself it was her way of being useful, because she had so little else to be useful with. I did not look at it. If I said no to her now I should have to say I had known, and for how long, and that I had sat in my own house and watched the Penhallows go out of it by the teaspoon so as not to be disagreeable."

"Did you know about the Carne?"

"I know that Garrow said 'interfered with.' Nobody says that about a book that has merely been stolen." The aid said nothing at all. "I'm not a fool. I only practise."

I got up. It seemed discourteous to sit. The range ticked, and in the passage, through the open door, I could see the macs on their pegs, and the place where a pair of boots had stood.

"Is it Morwenna?" said Loveday.

"I don't know yet."

"You do, Pellow. Your face is doing what the Chapel's did in 1956." She had got up too, a thin old woman in a nightdress with her feet in the wrong boots, holding the lamp, so that her shadow went up the wall behind her and over the ceiling to meet the rest of the house. "Will they put her in prison?"

She did not wait. She turned and went away down the passage with the lamp, the boots clumping, left on right and right on left, and I was left in her kitchen with the cards and the question and nothing to put it in.
