# The Quarrel at the Lantern

The ceiling of the Drowned Lantern is hung with glass fishing floats, a hundred and forty of them in their nets, and in every one a small lamp burns. Jago's grandfather began it in 1880, one float for each man lost off Trevenna, and nobody since has had the heart to take one down. The effect, if you sit under it long enough, is of drinking at the bottom of a green sea with all the lights on. It is the only pub I know where the decorations have a casualty list.

I had not been in since the quiz. I stood in the doorway at seven that Friday evening with the smell of chips and old beer coming out to meet me, and a dart that had been on its way to the board seemed to stop in mid-air, consider me, and then arrive in the wall. The Lantern's darts team, five men in their working shirts, looked round together, as people look at someone they have been discussing.

"Mrs Pellow," said the nearest.

"Evening, Alan."

I walked the length of the room under the lamps, and the talk closed behind me like water. Jago Rowe was at the pumps with a cloth over his shoulder, as big as a door and as silent. He did not say good evening, which in twenty-seven years he had never failed to do. He drew me half a cider without being asked, put a mat under it, and slid it along the wood with the three fingers and thumb of his right hand, and went to the other end of the bar to attend to a customer who was not there.

I took the cider to the table under the window. Kit was not there; Garrow had borrowed him for the evening, I learned, and installed him in the vestry of St Petroc's with a pot of tea and a great many questions. Demelza was. She sat at the far end of the bar in her uniform with a lemonade she was not drinking, and she looked at me once and nodded, a short level nod, which I understood to mean that she was on duty and would pretend not to be listening, and that I was to say nothing she would be obliged to write down.

I had not been seated a minute before Bert Tregear, eighty years old and coxswain of the lifeboat until his knees, came across in his cap and sat down opposite without being asked.

"You'll want to know about Thursday," he said. He had been waiting for the question since I came in. "I'll tell you about Thursday."

It took an hour. The Lantern on a Friday night had, I understood, appointed itself my witness, and it gave its evidence in the manner of the place, which is all at once, with corrections. Alan Polglase the postman came over from the dartboard, and Elsie Hocking and her husband Len from the settle, and a couple of the Chapel men who disapprove of the Lantern and were passing through it on the way to the gents. Between them they reassembled the night as a woman reassembles a torn page, with paste and argument.

"It was a good night," said Elsie. "Till it wasn't."

"We came fourth," I said.

"We came fourth because the Regulars cheated."

"Elsie Hocking," said Alan, with dignity, "the Lantern Regulars came second on merit. You gave a Brahms for an Elgar."

"Len gave a Brahms for an Elgar."

"I gave," said Len, who is a quiet man, "a considered answer to a difficult question, and it was robbery."

"It was the Nimrod," I said. "It is always the Nimrod. Anyone who has been to a funeral has heard it."

"That's what I said!" said Len, and Elsie hit him with her handbag, quite gently, as one punctuates.

I will say it was a comfort. For about four minutes the whole of Trevenna was reduced to a dispute about a piece of music, and I was cross with the right people for the right reasons, and a man was not dead. Then Bert coughed, and the table remembered itself, and the green light settled back on our faces.

They did the room first. The Jam Tarts at the next table, third place, as they have come every Thursday since the Coronation, with Morwenna in charge of the pencil, as she is of everything. The noise. The chips, which Jago does on quiz nights in a pan the size of a bath. Quire Straits in the corner by the fruit machine, Elsie and Len and Kit and myself, and a chair for the Reverend Nance that stayed empty all night because he had promised us and did not come. We had been a man short, which in my view also accounted for fourth.

Then they did the quarrel.

"Twenty past eight, near enough," said Bert. "Right in the middle of the geography. She come in from the back, the daughter, in that coat."

"Leather," said Elsie.

"I know it's leather, Elsie. And Mr Trevithick was stood at the bar with his whisky, very upright, like he'd swallowed a ruler, and she said, 'You sold Mother's piano.'"

"She said, 'You sold Mother's piano and you kept the stool,'" said Alan.

"It was about money," said Elsie. "She wanted four thousand pounds for a workshop up in London and he said accuracy obliged him to ask for a business plan."

"It was about her mother's money. He said it was held in trust. She said it was held in a drawer."

"I heard the word Mother," said Alan, "and the word piano, and another word that I shall not repeat in front of Mrs Pellow."

"I'm fifty-eight, Alan."

"I'd still not repeat it."

They agreed on two things only, and I counted them on my fingers under the table as they went by: that the girl had said Mother, and that she had said money. They agreed also that she had gone out at five and twenty past eight like a door slammed in a gale, and that Aubrey had stood a moment looking at his glass, and had drunk it, and straightened his waistcoat, and said to the room at large, "I beg your pardon, ladies and gentlemen. A family matter." Which, said Elsie, was the most human thing she had heard him say in twenty years.

I had no pencil. Alan lent me the chalk from the dartboard, which was no use on a sheet of paper, and then, being a postman and a man of resource, produced a stub from behind his ear. I took a beer mat from the next table, one with a printed pasty on it, turned it over, and began to write.

*8.00 Quiz begins. 8.20 A. and Imogen. 8.25 Imogen out.*

By nine o'clock the room had thinned and taken a new colour. Demelza had gone at half past eight, her lemonade untouched. The Chapel men had gone. Bert's wife had sent for him by a child. I sat with the mat and the cider, and the lamps in their nets swung a little each time the door opened, and I watched Jago work the pumps with that large slow back, until there was no one near the bar but the Reverend Nance, who had come in at ten to nine with his bicycle clips on, and ordered, in a voice of the deepest apology, his one small sherry.

He took it to the end of the bar, to a stool under a float with a green lamp, and sat with it in both hands like something that might go off.

"Arthur."

"Mrs Pellow! Forgive me. I didn't see you there."

"Nobody does," I said. "You didn't come to the quiz on Thursday."

"No. No, I am afraid I was detained." The cough drop travelled from one cheek to the other. He looked at the sherry. "By the roof. There is a matter of the roof, which wants a man to look at it, and one cannot always, forgive me, predict."

"At half past nine?"

"At half past—" He stopped. His mild eyes, which are the colour of weak tea, came up to mine with an air of actual pain. "Was there a question about half past nine?"

"I'm only asking where everyone was, Arthur. Garrow will ask. I thought you'd rather be asked by someone who liked you."

"I walked," said the Reverend. "I do walk, you know, in the evening. I think about my sermon. I could not tell you which way, precisely. Up, I think, and down. It is a very small village." He laughed, rather high, and drank the sherry in one swallow, though he generally makes it last the evening, and put down a coin more than the price. "Forgive me, I am sure it is nothing."

Then he took his clips off, which he had not done, and put them on again, and went out, and the bell swung on the door for some while after.

I looked at the beer mat. I wrote *9.30 Nance?* and, after thought, left the question mark, because it was the only honest thing on the card.

At twenty to nine on the Thursday, Jago told me, when at last he came down the bar, Aubrey had used the payphone. He told it to the glass in his hand, with the voice that moves like a tide. It hangs on the wall at the back by the gents, behind the dartboard, he said, and the dartboard had been in use, and Aubrey had stood against the wall with his cane hooked on his arm and the receiver an inch from his mouth, as if he distrusted it.

"Who did he ring?"

"He didn't say."

"What did he say?"

"'Half ten, then.'" Jago wiped the glass. "That's all I heard. He put it down. He had a second whisky, which he never did, and he went out. Ten to nine. That's the size of it."

"Half ten where?"

"He didn't say."

"Half ten, then." I said it twice, to hear what it did. It was the hour the quiz ended, when the whole Lantern would be on its feet hunting for coats. A good hour, I thought, for a man to be somewhere else without being missed.

I wrote it on the mat under the rest, with the time, and then the rest of the night as the room had given it to me: Imogen out at 8.25. Aubrey at the bar, then the telephone, then gone by ten to nine. Nance a question. Quiz ended half past ten; the Jam Tarts, third; Quire Straits, fourth and robbed. I went out at ten to eleven, up the hill, and it was dark.

Then there was the key. I sat looking at it for some time before I wrote it, because I did not care for what it did to my feelings.

The door had been locked behind him, with the spare. A person who kills a man does not stop to go through the pockets looking for a key unless he knows there is one to find. Someone had known. I had known; I had handed it over. Aubrey had known, naturally, and could tell nobody anything now. And Kit had known, because he had told me so himself that afternoon at the trestle, between a Georgette Heyer and a Dornford Yates, with his face white and his thumb in his mouth. *He told me on Thursday he'd got your spare key, Auntie Dot. Sorry. He said you'd been kind.*

Three people. It was a short list, and I did not like the third name on it, and I wrote it down all the same.

"Jago."

"Dot."

Aubrey had held the lease of the Lantern for eleven years, and in all that time Jago had never said a word against him, which in a Cornishman is either a testimony or a confession. I did not say so. "Who would want to see Aubrey Trevithick at half past ten at night in my press room?"

He did not answer. He took up a glass, a tumbler, and began to wipe it with the cloth. Round and round the inside, with the thumb that had only three fingers to keep it company. He wiped it for a full minute by the clock over the fruit machine. I counted. Above his head one of the green floats turned slowly on its net, and the lamp inside it flickered, as a lamp does that has been listening.

"Leave it, Dot," he said.

"I don't think I shall."

"Ned'd have left it."

"Ned would have asked twice as many questions as I have, only with a pipe in his mouth, so that nobody noticed." I finished my cider. I did not leave it. I put the beer mat face down on the wood, and on the clean side of it, with Alan Polglase's stub, I began a second list, headed in my best capitals with the question that had been sitting in the room since seven o'clock, wearing a green lamp and waiting to be asked: *Who was going to meet him?*
