# Bunting and Bargains

The Reverend Arthur Nance opened the Trevenna Antiquarian Book Fair an hour late, by apologising to God and then, since nobody else seemed to be in the habit that day, to the dealers.

"Forgive me, I am sure it is nothing," he said, standing on the dais in St Petroc's hall with his bicycle clips still round his ankles and a cough drop travelling from one cheek to the other, "but our chairman cannot be with us. Let us ask a blessing on the stalls, and the floor, and the roof, which is not what it was, and on those of you who have come a long way and found us, which is not easy. And on Aubrey." His voice went on the name. A woman from Bristol with a box of Penguins put her hand over her mouth. "There will be no ribbon," said the Reverend, and sat down on the wrong chair.

Twenty-six trestles stood under Jago Rowe's regatta bunting, two hundred yards of it, red, white and blue and a yellow nobody could account for, strung across the rafters because Aubrey had said it would add tone and Jago had said it would add bunting. Beneath it the dealers stood among their boxes in shirtsleeves and tweed, speaking in the low reverent voices of people about to be rude about one another's prices. I sat at my trestle at the end, beside the tea urn, with a pot of Ned's paste, a bone folder, a card in a dead man's handwriting, and no customers.

I had not wanted to come. But Garrow had told me not to leave the village, and the village had seemed to me that morning very small, and there was nothing to do in the Bindery but listen to it. Also, though I did not say so to myself, a woman at a trestle with a paste pot is the best-placed observer in any room. People talk across her as they talk across a hat stand. At fifty-eight I had been wasting the talent for years.

"I wonder, my dear," said a man in a bow tie, "if you could tell me who is in charge of repairs?"

"I am," I said, and mended his Trollope.

He was a Mr Fosdyke, from Hay, and while the hinge dried he told me, unasked, that Aubrey Trevithick had once sent back one of his catalogues with eleven corrections in red ink, including a Latin ablative. "He was right about every one. I have never forgiven him." That was how people spoke of Aubrey, I found, all that afternoon: with exasperated care, as of a clock that struck the hour correctly and at the wrong people. Nobody in the village had believed him an honest man. They had thought him too tidy for it.

"Mrs Pellow."

A shadow came across the trestle, a large pale one. Pieter de Vries was from Rotterdam and dealt in maps, and he wore a linen jacket the colour of milk and round tortoiseshell glasses and a clipped grey beard, and he stood at my table with his hands behind him, as a man stands at the edge of a museum.

"I am sorry for your trouble. Very terrible, this."

"Thank you, Mr de Vries."

"The inspector has been to see me this morning. He asks me about paper." He took off the glasses and polished them on the jacket, which made both paler. "A paper with a lion in a fence, from the seventeen-sixties. Is it Dutch, he asks. I tell him, probably, yes. Such paper we have sold to the whole world, to your Navy, to your Bank of England. Every dealer in Europe has a drawer of it." He put the glasses back. "And while I say it, I hear how it sounds. A Dutchman, in a large white jacket, who tells the policeman the paper is Dutch."

"It sounds like information."

"It sounds like a confession with good manners." The smile was very small. "I came for the maps, Mrs Pellow. Only for the maps."

He went back to his stall and began, with the elaborate calm of a man carrying a full cup across a room, to straighten a chart of the Scheldt that had not moved.

I took my paste pot, because I could not think what else to take, and went to the far corner by the door, where Morwenna Hext had her honey.

She had a trestle to herself and a gingham cloth, and on it the honey of eight hives in jars with gingham lids, set in ranks like a small yellow army. Beside them lay the candles: rolled tapers in sheets of honey-coloured wax, tied in threes with raffia, and cakes of beeswax in muslin for polishing furniture and freeing sticking drawers. A card said ALL PROCEEDS TO THE LIFEBOAT in her upright hand. The whole stall smelled like a church that had been given a holiday.

"Dot, my handsome. Have you eaten? You've not. Take this." A jar landed in my free hand. "No, I won't hear it. You're skin and bone, and it's for the lifeboat, so you're doing a service."

"Morwenna, I only came to say—"

"You came for a look at the Book," she said, and tilted her head at the end of the trestle. "Go on. I'm minding it for Jago. It can't be left lying about with all these strangers, and it isn't safe in the hall safe, either, I'm told, since the chairman's key went with him."

It lay on a cushion of tissue paper, a fat ledger in half calf, brown with a green cloth side and a gilt label across the front: REGATTA, TREVENNA, WINNERS. I knew it as I know my own handwriting. Ned had bound it in the summer of 1964, two years after I came to Trevenna; I had watched him do it, and he had let me press the boards. Every winning crew since the war was written in it in the treasurer's hand, and every treasurer had been a small anxious person who kept it in the airing cupboard.

At the back joint someone had made a repair. I looked at it twice. The old calf had been lifted and re-pasted, with a strip of Japanese tissue let in beneath, and the work lay flat and clean, in no hurry, and very nearly as good as Ned's.

"Who did that?"

"I did." She was straightening candles. "It was going, Dot, and I couldn't have the Book come apart in Jago's hands at the finish. Paste and a bit of nerve. Ned left a tin of his tissue behind once, years ago, and I never gave it back."

"It's a good hinge."

"It's an honest hinge. Don't make a fuss, I'll go red."

She was already red. She was always a little red, in the cheeks, like a winter apple, and she looked at me with her small bright eyes as if to see whether I had said it to be kind. I had not. I put my thumb on the joint and felt it give not at all, and had the thought, which I put away, that it was a heavy book for a ledger.

There was a noise from the middle of the hall like a sheet being torn very slowly, and a hush.

Jago Rowe was on a stepladder under the rafters in his shirtsleeves, the largest man in Trevenna, bald as a harbour stone, with an anchor on his forearm so faded that it might have been a bruise, and a right hand with the little finger gone to a winch in 'fifty-eight. He was Ned's oldest friend and the only man I knew who could tie a knot with nine fingers and still be ashamed of it. He had a length of bunting in one fist and was leaning out over the aisle to reach a hook. Below him, in the aisle, in his raincoat, with his notebook open to take a statement from the Reverend, stood Detective Inspector Garrow.

The knot gave. The whole run of it came down from the beam in a long soft rattle of flags, red, white, blue and the yellow, and it landed on the Inspector from the crown of his head to his shoes, so that for a moment he was a pillar of pennants with a notebook sticking out of it.

Nobody laughed. I will say that for Trevenna. Nobody laughed for at least two seconds.

"Mr Rowe," came a muffled voice, "was that deliberate?"

"A knot, Inspector." Jago's voice is like a slow tide; one hears it arrive a good while before it reaches you. He looked down at the pillar with an expression of great gravity. "Not a good one."

"I see."

"That's the size of it."

It was at this point that Imogen Mayne came in at the door in a black leather jacket.

It was July and the hall was close; she wore it like a position. She had her father's height and none of his ease, a cropped head, a silver stud in the side of her nose, and a cigarette unlit behind one ear, and she walked past the bunting and the Inspector and the Reverend as if they were a wall she meant to get round. At the table by the door stood Kit, with the chairman's grey tin box held against his chest in both hands, where the dealers' stall fees went.

"I'll take that."

"Sorry, I was only minding it. Until someone—"

"It's mine now. It's his." She took the box from him and set it down and opened it, and counted the notes with a fast dry thumb. "Where's the float? Where are the receipts? Who has the catalogues, Kit?"

"They're, sorry, they're by the—"

"Sorry's not an answer. It's a noise." She did not look up. She had found something in the box, a stub of pencil, one of Aubrey's, chewed at one end, and she stood with it in her palm. "Daddy," she said to it, quietly, and then closed her hand and looked up, and her eyes went over the hall and found nobody they wanted, until they came to me.

"You're the bookbinder."

"Dot Pellow."

"He said you were the only person in the village who didn't flatter him."

"He said that?"

"As a complaint." The nose stud flashed. She put the pencil in her pocket, and the box under her arm, and went away to count somebody else's money.

I had not expected to feel hit. I sat back on my heels beside the honey and put the jar down carefully on the floor.

"Pellow!"

The hearing aid reached me first, a high thin whistle that crossed the hall ahead of its owner, and then Loveday Penhallow herself, in gumboots under a tweed skirt, seventy-four years old and as thin as a pen, with long beautiful hands on the head of a blackthorn stick. People moved out of her way as they would for a heron. She stopped in front of me and the whistle stopped, and she looked down.

"They have my Carne in a paper bag in Truro."

"Yes, Miss Penhallow. I'm sorry."

"Don't fuss, Pellow. I did not ask you to be sorry." Loveday barks; it is one of the pleasures of her. "They say it was damaged. Garrow says there's some question about the book. What question? You were in the room."

I thought of the ewe sheep at fourteen shillings and the lion in his fence. It is a hard thing to say to a woman of seventy-four in a village hall, between a honey stall and a man in a bow tie. "I think it will be looked at very carefully," I said.

"That's what people say when it won't." The hearing aid gave a short whistle, like a kettle thinking about it. "You found him. You have the nose for it. You found out what was wrong with my pump before the plumber did. Find out who did this."

"I'm a bookbinder, Miss Penhallow."

"Nobody looks at you," said Loveday. "It's what the job wants."

I have been paid worse compliments, though not many.

Kit had come across the hall with a folder of typed pages, holding it out to her in both hands like a man handing over a cheque. "Miss Penhallow. Sorry. This is the list, the valuation, what Mr Trevithick and I did up to Wednesday. It's, sorry, it's all in order. I'd thought you should have it."

She took it and turned the pages with her long fingers, and I saw over her arm the column of numbers going down the left in Kit's careful capitals, and the last line on the last page. *114. Rutter, MS, c.1631, in contemporary calf, rebacked 1971. The "Carne".*

"That's as far as you got."

"Yes. Sorry. We'd done the north wall, and the Carne's the, it's the last on it."

"Last on the list," said Loveday. She closed the folder. Her hand found my sleeve and held it, and the fingers were cold as a bell, and surprisingly strong. "Aubrey valued my library as far as the Carne, Pellow, and not one inch past it. The sale is in September. I have a roof."

She went. The whistle went with her, up the aisle and out through the door into the afternoon, and the hall began again at its ordinary murmur, and a woman asked if the person with the paste could do her Georgette Heyer.

I went back to my trestle and did her Heyer. From there, across the hall, I could see the honey stall, where Pieter de Vries had come to buy a candle. I could see him over the heads. He picked the cheapest, a single yellow taper, and put a note on the cloth, and Morwenna, small and round and unhurried, folded it into tissue paper and tied it with raffia and held it out to him. He took it as if it might still be lit. He did not look at her face, nor at her hands, nor at the jars. He looked at the tissue paper the whole time, as if it were being read aloud to him.
