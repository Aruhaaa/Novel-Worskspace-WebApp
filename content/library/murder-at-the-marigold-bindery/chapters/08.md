# Quire Gives Evidence

The cake was on my step at ten to six, under a tea towel with a faded lighthouse on it.

I had slept in my clothes and not well. Demelza had walked me up the twenty yards to my own door at two, and stood on the cobbles until she heard the bolt go home, and I had lain on top of the counterpane with my shoes on listening to the cottage tick. When I opened the door the light was the colour of weak tea, the gulls were already shouting about it, and there it sat on the granite: a saffron loaf the size of a hymn book, still faintly warm through its greaseproof, with a corner cut off to show it was meant to be eaten and not admired. Morwenna's, of course. Nobody else in Trevenna gets the crust that colour.

Nobody had knocked. Nobody ever does. In Trevenna, when there is a death or a flood or a husband gone off with a woman from Redruth, the cake appears on the step before the news is round the harbour, and the person who has baked it is never anywhere about. I had found one on my own step twice before in my life. The first was in 1962, when I was a bride nobody had asked for, and the second was March 1986.

I took it in. I stood at the kitchen table, in my shoes, and ate the corner and then another corner, and then I was crying with my mouth full, which is not a thing I recommend to anyone with a good set of teeth. I was furious as well. It is a peculiar sensation to be both at once; it leaves the face with nowhere to go. Somebody had come up the lane in the dark to leave me a cake, and in four days nobody had come to ask whether I was all right, except a constable who wished to know why I had not mentioned a cupboard. I ate standing up and I finished the slice, and I wiped my eyes on the lighthouse, and I thought: well. That's the first decent thing that has happened since Thursday, and it is flour.

* * *

Demelza was in the shop by half past eight, in uniform, with her helmet under her arm and her eyes ringed like a pair of pennies. The Truro man had been and gone. He had a case of brushes like a lady's dressing-table and left the press-room window the colour of a chimney-sweep's glove. The saucer and the spoon had gone in a bag. Quire sat in the middle of the counter with her back to the constable, as she had since Friday.

"You should have told me it existed," said Demelza.

"I have said it was remiss."

"It isn't remiss. It's the one thing in this whole business that points at a person and not at a piece of paper. Who taught whom. Who stood at that bench." She did not raise her voice. Demelza never does. She lowers it, until you have to lean in, which is a great deal worse. "I rang the Inspector at six. I told him I'd seen that notebook on Friday, during the search. I said I'd had it in my hand and judged it to be old repairs."

"Did you?"

"No. I didn't open the paste cupboard. I thought a paste cupboard was where you kept paste."

"It is."

"Thank you, Mrs Pellow." She looked at the ceiling for a moment, where the shop bell hung on its curl of brass. "So I told him I had read a book I'd never touched, and now it's gone, and any minute he's going to ask me about it in front of you, and I need you to nod."

"I don't lie to policemen."

"You'll be lying to a policeman, then, but only on my account." She said it quite flatly. "If I say I never knew there was a cupboard, I've searched the scene of a murder for four hours and missed it. If I say I knew and thought nothing, I'm a fool who can be forgiven. Either way I'd rather be the fool. Nod."

"Demelza—"

"He's turning in at the bottom of the lane. I can hear that raincoat."

You could, in fact. Detective Inspector Garrow wears his raincoat in all weathers, and it makes a noise on the steps like a man folding a tarpaulin. He came in under the bell with the sun full on his back, and stood looking about the shop as if he were trying to put a price on it.

"Mrs Pellow, dear," he said. "A burglary."

"Of a sort."

"Of a sort. Yes. I'd say that's about right." He lowered himself on to the customers' stool, which he had not been invited to do, and opened his notebook, which is bound in rexine. "A window opened with great delicacy. A cupboard opened without a mark on it. A till left with fourteen pounds sixty in it. And the single article taken is one that nobody in the kingdom would pay a penny for. Would you call that an ambitious thief, Constable?"

"No, sir."

"A careful one?"

"Yes, sir."

"A very careful one. Careful enough to leave a lady with a fright and a story." He turned to me, kindly, which was worse. "Mrs Pellow. A woman on her own. A husband three years gone. A business that, shall we say, is not what it was. Then a dead man in the back room, and half the village not knowing where to look. I've seen it before, my dear. A little disturbance. Something to be fussed over, something that makes people come round."

I let him finish. I find it saves time.

"Inspector, I have never in my life wished to be fussed over," I said. "It's such a messy thing to be given."

"Quite." He wrote something. "And the notebook, Constable. You saw it on Friday?"

"Yes, sir. A record of old jobs. I took it to be of no relevance to the scene."

"Of no relevance."

"At the time, sir."

"Mrs Pellow?"

I nodded. I have been nodding all my life, at weddings and sermons and the accounts, and it came to me quite easily, which was the worst of it.

The door bell went again. Kit came in backwards with a box of linen thread in his arms, saw the raincoat, and said "Sorry" to the bell.

"Mr Pellow." Garrow beamed. "Just the man. Saturday night."

"I was at the Lantern. Sorry. From about nine, I think. Darts."

"Darts."

"A match. A friendly."

"Against whom?"

"Porthcurno. Some of them." Kit shifted the box. "Or, no, I think they didn't come, I think it was the Seconds against, we played ourselves in the end."

"And who won?"

Kit looked at the ceiling as if the result might be written there. "Sorry. I think I did. Or Eddy Tonkin."

"And Mr Tonkin can tell me so?"

"He went home about ten to feed his ferrets."

"And from ten to whenever you left?"

"I was at the board. Sorry. It was full, the Lantern, on a Saturday, they must have, somebody would have seen me."

"Somebody will have seen you," said Garrow, in the voice of a man who has never believed it. He closed his notebook. "I'll ask. Don't leave Trevenna, either of you. The lady of the house will understand that." And he went out in a rustle of tarpaulin.

Demelza stood perfectly still with her eyes on the middle of the counter. Kit put the box down very slowly, as one puts down something that might go off. Neither of them looked at the other; the shop had more than enough to look at.

* * *

I went to church. I go to St Petroc's at Christmas and for funerals and on the rare Sunday when I wish to see how a thing is done, and that Sunday I went because the Inspector had gone, and I wanted to watch what he looked at.

He was in the back pew with his notebook propped on the hymn board. St Petroc's choir is nine people, of whom two are sure, and they sang "Eternal Father, strong to save" about a quarter-tone under the organ, with the particular courage of people who have buried a great many fishermen. The Reverend Nance preached on the lilies of the field and apologised twice during the sermon, once to the lilies. Morwenna was in her usual pew with her handbag on her lap. She did not turn round, but I saw her shoulders settle when I came in, as a hen's do.

I took the back of the hymn sheet, and a stub of pencil I keep for measuring, and wrote three words down the margin: *till, key, cat.*

The till first. Fourteen pounds sixty and a button, and a bag of coppers I would not have left either. Whoever it was had gone through the shop in the dark, past the cash and the good shears and Ned's brass dividers, which would fetch ten pounds in Truro, and gone straight to a small green book of other people's names. A thief who cared about a notebook more than a till.

The key next. Demelza thought a key had been taken from the hook. But the key had been on my chest since Saturday morning, and it was on my chest now, under the hymn sheet and my good blouse; and the lock was as unmarked as the day Ned fitted it. So there was another. Ned had been a man who bought two of every tool, because the first always walked, and I had never in thirty years seen or looked for a second key to that cupboard. Somebody had one. Somebody had been given it, once, by a man who gave out keys the way other men give out advice, which is to say when he was sure of you.

And the cat. I had it from Demelza, in the small hours, that Quire hissed at her on Friday, bit her on Saturday, and had that afternoon taken a piece out of the Truro man's cuff. I had seen her take the skin off a plumber. Whoever had carried Quire to that cupboard had done it without losing a drop of blood, and had then poured her milk, and laid a spoon.

Well. I could not make a person of that. I could make only an outline, like the pale shape on a pastedown where a print has hung and been taken down. Somebody who valued a list of names above a till, who held a key I did not know existed, who had been kind to a cat in the middle of a crime. I looked down the church for such a person, and found a great many cardigans.

* * *

By four the fair had been packed into its crates and the church hall smelled of damp card and old men's tweed. I took my trestle apart, and Kit took the other end, and we carried it out to the van without a word. I could do nothing for him. I took myself down to the Lantern.

Jago was in the cellar, as usual, which means that his voice came up through the trap in the bar like a ship's sounding. "Darts?" it said. "There's always darts, Dot. Whether it was a match or a lot of men throwing things at a board on a Saturday, I couldn't tell you."

"Was Kit there?"

"Saw him at nine. Saw him at eleven with his coat on. Between, I'd a barrel to change and a flood to mop. I was down here, the best part of an hour." The voice paused, and came up again with its owner under it, wiping his hands. "That's the size of it. Take the Dutchman this, will you? He's not eaten since breakfast. He's in the back."

He put a pasty on a plate, and the plate in my hand, and went down again.

Pieter was in the back room on his trunk. It stood by the dead dartboard with its straps done up and its brass corners polished, and he sat on it with his white jacket across his knees like a man on a railway platform in a country where the trains had been cancelled. His round glasses turned to me. He did not look surprised. I do not think anything surprises that man; he simply reorders.

"Mrs Pellow. You have come to arrest me?"

"I've brought you a pasty."

"Ah." He took it with two hands. "The Inspector says I stay. I say, I have a ferry on Tuesday. He says the ferry will wait. A ferry! It is not a man, a ferry, it does not wait." He ate a mouthful. "I said the paper was probably Dutch. Probably! In my trade this is a word you say like *bless you*. Now I am the man who knows about Dutch paper. It is a sad thing, I tell you, to be honest in a small place."

"Are you?"

He looked at me over the glasses, and I had the impression of a man weighing me, leaf by leaf, for chain lines. "I am what is usual in my trade, Mrs Pellow. I buy from people. I do not ask what is in the attic. Everyone in my trade is exactly this honest."

"And what's usual in this village is saying nothing."

"Yes! It is extraordinary. I ask a question and I am told, *that's the size of it*, or *proper job*, or *dreckly*. I do not know what any of these mean. I ask the landlord, who says, *that's the size of it*."

"That's the size of it," I said, and left him with his pasty and his strapped trunk, and he laughed, a single bark, the first sound of the sort I had heard from him.

* * *

It was dusk when I put the rubbish out. I pulled the dustbin from its place by the yard wall to empty the bucket, and behind it on the flags, in the dust, lay a flat tin with its lid rolled back on the key like a snail. It had been licked so clean it shone. Gulls make a mess of a tin; they leave it ruined and glittering on a roof. This had been done as a cat does it, patiently, to the corners.

I picked it up. I turned it over and read the Portuguese on the bottom. Then I stood a moment with it in my hand and an idea going round in me that would not come to a stop, like a coin on a table. I could not have said what it was. I put the tin in the bin, and the lid on the bin, and by the time I reached the shop door the thing had come out again like a splinter.

I sat down in the shop chair to take off my shoes. I intended to sit for the length of a boot-lace. The next I knew the lamp was lit and the window was black, and something heavy and warm and quietly vibrating lay across my knees: Quire, who has sat on my lap on perhaps six occasions, all of them when I held a sardine. She had her eyes shut. Her torn ear was twitching.

"You snore," said Demelza.

She sat on the customers' stool, where Garrow had sat, with her helmet in her lap and her notebook closed on the elastic band. I do not know how long she had been there. I do not think she did either.

"I'll tell you what I've not told the Inspector," she said, "because I don't know yet what it is. If you find something, you tell me first. Not Garrow. Not Kit. Me."

"That's a great deal to ask."

"I know." She got up and put the helmet on, and pulled the strap with two fingers, which is how she shows she has stopped being a person and become the force. "I'll ask it anyway."

I thought of the green notebook, and the other key, and the cake, and Quire's pulse going on my knee like a small engine. I have never promised anything lightly in my life.

"I promise," I said.
