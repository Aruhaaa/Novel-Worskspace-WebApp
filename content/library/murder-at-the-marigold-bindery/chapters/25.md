# High Water on the Slip

Ada Bolitho said afterwards that I left the tent like a woman who has seen her bus, and I daresay that is fair. I did not run. I have never run for anything but a kettle. But I handed Tamsin her red book and went down the benches between forty people who leaned out of my way as if I were a draught, and nobody spoke until the flap, where Demelza stood with her notebook shut in her fist and a face like a closed shop.

"Ten minutes," I said.

"Dot."

"She hasn't anywhere to go. The harbour mouth is the far end of Trevenna from the road, and the only other way out is wet."

Demelza looked over my head at the harbour. Behind us Garrow's voice had risen on the subject of sandwiches. Edna Chegwidden had put an egg-and-cress into his hand to stop him pointing, and was telling him it was rude to waste it.

"Whatever she says to you isn't evidence," said Demelza. "I want you to know that I know that."

"I know it too."

"I'll be at the top of the Slip. I'll see you and I shan't hear you. Ten minutes, and once he's finished that sandwich I can't promise." "It's the only favour I've got left, Dot. I'd like it back one day."

The Slip is a ramp of concrete laid in 1923 at an angle chosen by a man who had never walked down it in court shoes, with a galvanised rail along its eastern side and the lifeboat house at the head, its green doors shut. The shower that had turned the tea tent into a drum had gone through at three. The sun had come out low and yellow, and the tide had climbed the ramp until only the upper half showed, streaked with green weed, lapping the concrete with the small domestic sound of a cat at a saucer.

Morwenna sat on the rail, side-saddle, with the Regatta Book across her knees and her handbag at her hip, swinging one foot like a girl on a gate. Along the flank of the Slip below her the gig *Provident* lay afloat on two ropes, oars shipped, her crew all gone to the tea. As I started down, Jago Rowe went past me, hands hanging, without a word, and stepped into her and sat on the stern thwart like a man told to mind a boat. I did not take that in. I set it down because I had spent the afternoon taking in everything else.

"Mind the weed," said Morwenna, without turning her head. "Sit down, my handsome, you've been on your feet since half past two."

There was an iron mooring post at the foot of the rail, and I sat on it.

"Is Demelza going to arrest me?"

"When we've finished."

"Good. I should like to finish." She opened the handbag, took out the tin, and lifted the lid: half a saffron cake, yellow as a duckling, the horn-handled knife laid along the top. "Sit. Eat."

"No, thank you."

"Well." She looked at the cake and shut the lid on it. "That's the first time since 1962."

She meant it for a joke, and it was one, and neither of us laughed.

"You've told them all of it, Dot, bar what it was for," she said. "I find I'd like someone to have that right. Then I shall have said it once, and it's very restful, having said a thing once."

"Three hundred and eighty pounds a week." She said it as one gives the quantity of flour. "That's Gwithian House, with the garden and the nurse who doesn't raise her voice. The County would have him at Bodmin for nothing, in a day-room with twenty chairs. I went and stood in the door of it. He likes things in their drawers, Ronald. On a Tuesday he doesn't know me, and I'm the nurse. On a Sunday I'm his wife. I take the bus and take my chance."

"You could have told me."

"And you'd have found something out of the rent, and starved the cat. I am the one who brings the cake, Dot. It isn't a position you can reverse halfway through. I've counted the regatta's money since 1971, and you'll find every penny of it. It was only my own that ran out."

"I read to Loveday on Tuesdays and had the run of the shelves, as you saw. Small things first. A snuffbox. Three plates out of a Hakluyt. I called it borrowing, and I think she called it that for me; she's no fool, Loveday, only gifted at not asking. Then came the second winter of fees, and the Carne at the end of the long shelf in Ned's cover. Ned had told me what it was worth once, on this harbour wall, over a pasty."

"I took it home in March under the shortbread, and put the day-book in its cover the Tuesday before Easter. The 1763 estate day-book; nobody had opened it since the old Squire. I sewed it in the honey-house, on the second frame. Ned carried that frame up Church Lane himself in 1958, on a handcart under a blanket, and said Ronald wasn't to be told what it was for. Ronald never asked."

"It was the best sewing I have ever seen in a forgery," I said.

I had not meant to say it. Morwenna looked pleased. God forgive her, she looked pleased, and for a moment so did I, and neither of us referred to it again.

"I met Mr de Vries at the Lemon Street book sale in Truro. I said a lady I knew had some old leaves. He didn't ask where from. They don't, my handsome, if the price is right and the lady has a pleasant face. Forty, in three goes. Twenty I kept back, because I was frightened, and the safest place for anything you're frightened of is the one book in Trevenna nobody would think to open."

"The treasurer's."

"Ned made those boards too thin for what I wanted. So I split them and laid the leaves between with wheat paste and shut them up again. I've carried that book about the village for three months like a hen with a china egg."

I had held it in my two hands in the WI hall, found it heavy for a ledger, and put that down to Ned. Well.

"I want to tell you about Aubrey," said Morwenna, "because they will say he was in it, and he wasn't."

"I know he wasn't."

"Jago will have told you about the telephone in the passage. He spoke into it with his back to me, and nobody on the end. 'Half ten, then.' He couldn't say it to a woman's face. That was Aubrey, he took other people's shame and wore it like a spare coat. I said, 'Half ten, Aubrey,' to his back, and went to my table and wrote 8.40 at the foot of the first round in my best hand."

"I left at ten to ten, for my mac. Then I stood in the old pilchard cellar doorway at the foot of your lane and watched Imogen Mayne go up and come down again with her face wet, and I thought, well, he'll be softened. I walked up at half past. The door was on the latch. He was at the bench in his shirtsleeves with the lamp on and the rutter open, writing on the pastedowns in pencil. He did not turn round. He said, 'Mrs Hext, I am glad you came. I have thought what I should say, and I find it is the plain thing.'"

"He wanted them back, Dot. Every leaf. Then he would tell Loveday the book had been injured by an incompetent binder, and that would be the end of it. He gave me his word, and he was a man who kept it. I said I couldn't, they were sold, the money was Ronald. And he said, 'Then you must say so in the morning, to Loveday and to Mrs Pellow, and I shall stand beside you.'"

She was quiet.

"He would have stood beside me. I have been over it a good many times, and I think it is the most dreadful thing anyone has ever offered me."

"And then?"

"Then he turned back to the bench to write it down. 'Accuracy,' he said, 'is a form of courtesy,' and he put his pencil to the paper. The hammer was on its nail at my left shoulder; I'd put my hand on it when I came in, as you steady yourself on a gate. I should like to tell you I decided. I can't. I'd feel better either way."

"Once," I said.

"Once. He made no sound at all. I have heard a hive go quiet when somebody sets down a hat too close, and it was very like."

"You hung it back."

"My hand did. It hadn't held that hammer in thirty years and it went to the left-hand nail like a swallow to the same beam. Face out. Ned's way." Her mouth moved. "Caught by a nail, after all that."

"Then I put out the lamp. One does. I stood in the dark with Aubrey at my feet, and heard a step in the lane. You walk like somebody counting stairs, Dot; I'd know it in my sleep. You were humming, flat, the one from the music round that your team got wrong. You came up past the window, and I held my breath, and I thought: if she looks in, I shall tell her everything, and be glad. And you went by. You didn't look."

"No," I said.

It came out quite steady. I had carried that dark window for nine days, and I had expected the saying of it to weigh more.

"I was angry with you for about a minute, which wasn't reasonable. Then I put the strip on, because I wanted to see what I was doing, and I waited for it, flicker and stammer and light, and that's the headache. It's quite true. I only put it at Easter because it seemed the safer end of the year. I do try to tell the truth about small things. It saves effort."

"The stove," I said.

"I lit it with your own kindling and burned what he'd written. *Mrs P. in the mor.* He had put that down for you, so as not to forget. I couldn't burn the book. It's the one thing I'm not able to do. I took his key off the bench, your green wool, and locked up behind me."

"And the cupboard."

"Ned lent me that key for the Saturdays, in 1955. I expect he wrote *fetch back*. He never did fetch it back, and I never offered. I took it to my wedding in my coat pocket. In thirty-four years I never turned it once, until the Saturday you said to Kit, with the door wide open, that Ned had kept a notebook. You've never learned to lower your voice, Dot. You tell the whole lane everything in a whisper."

"Where is it?"

"In the honey-house, in the biscuit tin with the Christmas paper. I held it over the stove and I couldn't. I couldn't burn his hand either. You may have it back when they've done with it." She looked at me sideways. "It isn't a bad hand, you know. You only think it is."

"And Quire."

"Three months, in eighty-six. Sardines, because a sardine tin wants only a key, and the cat wasn't hard to please. On the back step at six, before you were up. You'd forgotten to feed anything, my dear, yourself included, and I thought, I can't mend that, but I can see to the cat. I'm sorry about the cupboard. I couldn't leave her loose with the window open and the harbour at the bottom of the lane. The milk was only manners."

"You might have said. In eighty-six."

"You'd have had to thank me."

The foot had stopped swinging. She looked down at the water.

"I loved him, Dot. It was no news to Ned. He was kind about it, which is worse than the other. He gave me the frame for a wedding present, like handing a woman a hat when she's asked for the sea. And you had him, and you didn't need him. The week he died you forgot to feed a cat, and by the Wednesday you had the shop open. I sat in the honey-house three days with the bees and a pot of tea going cold, and I couldn't open anything."

"I was not good at it," I said. "I was only upright."

"It looks very much the same from the pews."

I had no answer, and said so by saying nothing, which is not my habit.

"He told me about the dot, you know."

"The Pellow dot," I said.

"In my first week. A pin-prick at the foot of the last gathering of everything he finished, with the point of the awl. *So they know somebody was here.* He'd not have told you; it was a pupil's thing. When I had the last gathering of the Carne open on the frame in March, there it was. I could have cut that gathering out and sewn in a leaf of the day-book. I sat with my thumb on it for the best part of an afternoon." She looked at her thumb. "I left it. I thought somebody who knew would find it. I think I wanted somebody to."

"I had to sit down," I said.

"Yes. I thought you would."

"And Pieter," I said.

"He put a note in my handbag at the Lantern while the lemonades were being fetched. The quay, five to eleven; he should like to speak of the paper. The Tarts went home at a quarter to, and I went round by Net Loft Row. He had a leaf with him still, in his paperback, too beautiful to sell. He had worked out the lion paper and the honey, and he would write to the police from Rotterdam, as a courtesy, so that I might speak to them first. Another courteous man. I seem to have spent this summer among them."

"He was standing at the edge with the leaf in his hand, looking at the water. Low water twenty-five past nine, I thought, so a young flood over mud, and nobody about. A foreign gentleman, a drop of drink, a dark quay." The foot swung once. "I pushed him. I did not wait to see."

"He might have drowned."

"That was the idea, my dear."

She said it in the level voice, the voice of someone reading out her own minutes, and it was the level that I could not bear. I would have borne a shout. I found I had stood up and was shaking all down my front, and I sat down again on the mooring post, for the look of the thing.

"He's awake," I said. "He has been since yesterday, and this morning he spoke."

"I thought so. You told me he was asleep with your whole face. You go pink to the ears, Dot, you always have." Her shoulders came down about an inch, and I saw it. "I'm glad. I should like that written down somewhere, that I'm glad. It's the one thing in all this I would have back."

The weed at the edge of the Slip stood up straight in the water and leaned neither way.

"I've always liked the top of the tide," she said. "Well. That's the minutes." She slid down from the rail, small and round, with her sash straight and the Book in the crook of her left arm. "There's one more item."

"Morwenna."

"Ned would be so cross with me. Good calf."

She threw it left-handed, underarm, the way one tosses a posy at a wedding, out over the rail and high, and the Regatta Book turned once in the low sun with its red label flashing and came down in the green water a yard off the gig's bow.

It floated. A big book will, for a moment, on the air in its spine. Then the sea began to find the way in, and Jago Rowe came down the length of the *Provident* in three strides and said, "No. Not that, Morwenna," in his voice like a slow tide. He leaned out on his enormous arm, the one with the anchor gone to a blur, and took the Regatta Book out of the sea by its spine as a man takes a loaf from a hot oven, and set it streaming on the Slip at my feet.

"Oh, Jago," said Morwenna, quite gently, as one speaks to a dog that has brought back a stick one had not meant to throw.

There was a noise from the head of the Slip: a constable covering a hundred feet of wet concrete in boots. Garrow had come to the top behind her, with half an egg sandwich still in his hand. He looked at the sandwich, and at the woman by the rail, and said, in a voice I had not heard from him before, "Constable Kitto. If you would."

Demelza stopped in front of Morwenna with her plait come out from under her helmet, breathing hard. "Morwenna Hext," she said, "I am arresting you on suspicion of the murder of Aubrey Trevithick, and of the attempted murder of Pieter de Vries. You do not have to say anything unless you wish to do so, but what you say may be given in evidence."

"Yes, my handsome," said Morwenna. "I rather thought you might." She took the cake tin out of her bag and pressed it into my hands. Her fingers were warm and dry and perfectly steady. "Eat, Dot. You'll want it before the day's out. It's a day old, but it's a day old in the right way."

Demelza took her elbow, as one steers a person off a kerb, and they went up the Slip together, the small round figure in the sash and the short square one in the helmet, past Garrow, who stood aside and for once in his life said nothing at all.

I knelt on the concrete by the Book. For the first time since the tent nobody was looking at me, and I discovered that I did not mind. The sea had done what a week of suspicion had not: it had got into the glue. Wheat paste will hold for ten years if you keep it dry, but it has never learned to refuse water. That is why we use it. It lets go, for those who know how to ask.

I slid the bone folder from my bag into the seam of the back board, where the water had gone in ahead of me, and eased. The board opened like the lid of a pie. Between the two skins of millboard, flat and brown and cold, lay the leaves.

I lifted them out one at a time on the flat of my left thumb and laid them in a row on the warm concrete, where the sun had come round the lifeboat house and found them. The ink had not run. It does not, on old rag, if you are quick: the iron has had three hundred and fifty years to make up its mind. A pilot's careful brown hand, a mile at a time, telling a stranger which rocks to keep well open. Twenty of them lay along the Slip in the sun, spread like a hand of cards put down by a player with nothing left to hide, and the tide stood at the top and did not move, in no hurry whatever.
