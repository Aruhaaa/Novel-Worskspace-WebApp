# The Music Round

Jago Rowe kept the Thursday answer sheets in a biscuit tin, and until that night I had supposed he did it out of sentiment.

By twenty to eleven the Lantern had shut its eyes and loosened its collar. The curtains had been drawn across the harbour windows, which was the whole of the ceremony, and forty people who had behaved themselves all evening began, behind a bolted door, to behave less carefully. Wilf Hosken had taken off his boots. The Seconds had resumed their darts at the far end, in the dim, with the exaggerated solemnity of men who cannot be fined. Nance sat in the inglenook with a lemon barley, appalled and wholly delighted, like a man who has been taken to a nightclub by his own churchwarden. Tamsin, on her fifth lemonade, lay along the settle with her boots on the cushion and her eyes shut, and announced to nobody that this was the best night of her life and that she would deny it.

Jago put the tin down on the bar in front of me with the sound of a coffin lid settling. Huntley & Palmers, the lid painted with a stagecoach in a snowstorm, the corners rusted brown and the sides ringed with forty-one weeks of other people's beer.

"You've wanted this since Friday," he said.

"I haven't asked."

"No. You haven't. That's how I know." He rubbed the top of his head with the hand that has four fingers and a thumb. "I read that out tonight, Dot. His words, in his order. I told you to leave it, the first night, and I was wrong, and I'd be wrong again if I held my tongue. He told me for a year to file them properly. You're good with paper."

"Jago..."

"Take what you like. Put them back in order." He lifted his coat from its nail. "I've a barrel to see to."

He went out through the back, and the door sighed to behind him. It was a full minute before it struck me that a landlord who is keeping a lock-in does not as a rule see to his barrels in his coat.

I do not know what other people do with a heap of paper. My hands do one thing, which is to square it. I tipped the sheets out on the bar and knocked the edges true against the wood, and they fell into their weeks like dominoes: forty-one Thursdays since the first of October. You could read the weather in them, stiff in the cold months and limp and fingered in July. The Regulars' sheets carried remarks in the margins, WE WUZ ROBBED in capitals, eleven times. The Seconds' were a drift of scoring and thumbprints. Ours were as neat as a church notice, and had finished fourth on thirty-one occasions, which Jago had preserved like a parish register of a very consistent sin.

Jory, the potman, had been missing for a quarter of an hour, which nobody had remarked, a potman's absence being the natural condition of a pub. He came in now from the yard, wiping his hands on his apron, with a five-pound note between his teeth like a retriever with a duck.

"Where've you been?" said Wilf Hosken.

"Quay steps. Dutchman's trunk." Jory took the note out of his mouth to be accurate. "Strapped up like a cow for market. He said he'd be along for it himself."

"And he paid you?"

"A fiver."

"Is it a Dutch fiver?"

"It's got the Queen on."

"Then he's more English than he looks," said Wilf, and went back to his boots.

Kit slid on to the stool beside me with his pint. He did not say "Sorry." He looked at the sheets and then at me, and I think he knew what I was after before I did.

"Last week's," he said. "It's on top. Jago never put anything in order in his life."

It was. The Jam Tarts' sheet for the thirteenth of July, folded once down the middle and stained with a ring of something brown. At the head, in a hand I should have known on the far side of the world, because it had written me eleven lines on the day Ned was buried: *JAM TARTS. Capt. M. Hext.*

I turned back through the weeks first, I do not know why, and the hand ran through them all, quick and upright and small, the hand of a woman who has never crossed a *t* twice. Then I came back to the thirteenth. The rounds went down the page one after another with the times at the foot of each in Miss Hocking's fashion: *8.38*, *9.10*, *9.31*. At the foot of the fourth, Music, stood *9.50*, and underneath it the writing changed. It was round and backward-leaning and dotted its *i*'s with small circles, and I knew that too, because Doris Rosevear had once signed the Chapel's Harvest returns with a smiling face.

*Capt. changed 9.50. M.H. gone up to Loveday's. D.R. now holding pencil. (J.R.'s rule.)*

The rest of the page was in Doris's circles to the bottom. The captain's neat hand did not come back.

Kit leaned over. "Ten to ten," he said, without being asked. "Jago pulled the plug at the wall. Eddy said it was robbery."

"You remember the time."

"I always remember the time. I catalogue." He looked into his pint. "It's about all I'm good for."

"That is untrue," I said, "and we shall return to it."

I should say what I knew. Everyone in Trevenna agreed that Morwenna had gone up to Loveday's after the quiz, in the way that everyone agrees on the date of the Armistice, without having been asked and without a doubt in the world. I had agreed myself. I had nothing against her in my hands but forty minutes and a woman called Doris.

The Jam Tarts were getting their coats. The Institute does not hold with lock-ins; Mrs Chegwidden had said so twice and had then looked longingly at her gin. Jory was to draw the bolt for them when they were ready. Morwenna sat a little apart with her big handbag open on her knee and the tin out of it, as it always is, and a greaseproof parcel unfolded beside her: saffron cake, cut in fingers. At the end of the bar, Pieter de Vries had not moved from his stool. He had a lemonade he had not touched, and since he came in he had followed Morwenna across the room with his head, the way a dog follows a plate.

"Sit," she said, when she saw me coming. "Eat. You look like a dishcloth that's been wrung by a strong man."

I sat. I had meant to be clever, and found that I was not going to be. "I'm trying to put last Thursday in order, Morwenna, and I keep losing the middle. I wondered if you'd help me."

"Whatever you like, my handsome." She cut a finger of cake with the little horn-handled knife and gave it to me on the greaseproof.

"When did you go up to Loveday's?"

"Half past ten." She did not hesitate. "As soon as the last answer was in. She'd been after me for a hand of piquet all week and the damp gets into her chest. Mind the crumbs on Doris."

"It's only that I have your sheet."

"Have you?"

"Jago's tin. Doris has written the time on it, in your fashion, under the music round. *M.H. gone up to Loveday's.* Ten to ten. And Kit says that's when the jukebox went off."

The lid of the tin went down on her knee, slowly, with a small click. She looked at the finger of cake in her hand with the attention of a woman choosing between two windows. Then she smiled, and it was the whole of her smile, with the cheeks in it.

"Then it was ten to ten," she said, "and I've been telling myself half past for a week. There. Isn't it dreadful, what the mind does." She tapped her temple with a knuckle. "Ronald asked me on Sunday whether I was the new nurse. I said no, I was the old one. I put his tablets in the wrong pocket and found them in the lift. I'd have sworn on the Book it was half past. It's no good, Dot, I'm sixty-three and I've mislaid whole months. That Doris. She wrote *Alleluia* on the Chapel quiz when she meant Armenia."

"Well," I said.

"Eat your cake."

It was saffron cake, the true yellow kind with the currants, which I have eaten at every crisis of my life in this village, and it did what it has always done: it made a smaller thing of whatever I had been holding. I told myself that forty minutes is not a lie but a mislaid piece of an evening. I have mislaid whole years of mine. I put the sheet in my cardigan pocket, where it joined a pencil, a loupe and a lemon sherbet, and I ate the cake, and I should like it recorded that I knew perfectly well what I was doing.

At a quarter to eleven Jory drew the bolt back a hand's breadth, and the Institute went out in a body, like a hen coop being moved. Morwenna was last. She stooped and kissed my cheek, smelling of woodsmoke and wax. "Sleep," she said. "Promise me." Her voice went off up Net Loft Row with the others', saying something about Edna's knees, and Jory shot the bolt behind them.

Demelza had come across for her coat. She did it as a person does who has been wanting to for an hour, standing too close to the bar and buttoning her cardigan over the pocket with Kit's margin in it. She did not look at me. She looked at the pump handles.

"You said first," she said. "If there's anything."

The sheet lay in my cardigan an inch from my hand, with its ten to ten and its Doris and my friend's quick neat writing going out at the music round like a candle. I thought of the tin on Morwenna's knee. I thought: it is forty minutes, and it is Morwenna, and I have been given a perfectly good explanation by the lady herself.

"Not yet," I said.

"Right." She nodded at the pump handles. "I'm off duty. I haven't seen a lock-in. I've spent the evening in a tent." Jory let her out like a cat, and the smell of the harbour came in and went out again behind her, and I understood that I had now kept four things from her, and that the fourth had come easier than the third.

Five minutes after that, at ten to eleven by the clock over the till, Pieter de Vries got down from his stool. He turned up the collar of the linen jacket, in July, in a bolted room, and said something low to Jory. Jory shot the bolt a hand's breadth and let him out into the dark. The window was uncurtained at one corner, and I saw the pale shape of the jacket pass the glass and go downhill, towards the Steps and the quay, with the quick short walk of a man who has made an appointment and is afraid of being early.

I did not follow him. I will say that plainly, because I have been asked since, and by more than one person. I sat with the tin of Thursdays and squared another week, and then another, and the sheets would not stay squared. Tamsin snored. Nance, in the inglenook, had begun on a hymn under his breath and been told by the Seconds to carry on, as it was better than the darts. At five to eleven I got up, because the room had become unbearably warm and the saffron cake lay in me like a stone, and I asked Jory to let me out for air.

The Lantern's door opens on the head of Pilchard Steps. Beyond the lamp over the step the dark began at once, and in it the whole of the harbour breathed, the flood making over the mud with that sullen thick lapping a young tide has, a rope creaking against a ring somewhere below, a boat knocking its fender on the wall. The air smelled of weed and diesel and, from the far side of the quay, a bonfire somebody should not have lit. I stood with my hand on the doorpost and the Jam Tarts' sheet in my pocket and thought of nothing whatever, which I have noticed is the condition in which the worst news finds a person.

From the quay, below me in the dark, a man cried out. It was short, and it was cut off, as though a hand had been put across it. Then there was a splash, heavier than any fish.
