# The Tea Tent

The sandwiches were cleared at ten past three. That was the first thing, and it nearly ruined everything.

By then the tent was as full as the Chapel at its anniversary tea. The crews had come up from the Hard in their white jerseys, steaming, and sat along the benches with the cox, a stout twelve-year-old called Kerensa Rosevear in a lifejacket two sizes too large, holding the Institute's pen in both hands like a candle. The Chapel had come, and the Lantern, and the Co-op, and Ada Bolitho in the raspberry hat she had taken back from the otter for the occasion. Gwen Trewhella sat at the front with her hand cupped behind her ear. I counted forty-odd faces, and the canvas over them dripped slowly, like an old clock, on the long tables. Imogen stood at the dressed table with the silver cup in her hands and her chin up, looking more like her father than she knew. Demelza was against the back pole. Beside her, behind a trestle, with an expression of the deepest forbearance, stood Garrow.

Edna Chegwidden had begun to clear. The Institute clears the sandwiches at ten past three; it has done so every regatta since 1971 and does not mean to change for murder. She took up a tray of egg and cress, and Doris Rosevear took up the tongs, and Garrow came off the pole like a man stung.

"Nobody is to move anything!"

"I'm moving the sandwiches, Inspector," said Edna, "not leaving the country."

"Nothing is to be removed from this tent."

"They've been out since two. They'll turn."

"Madam, I am not concerned for the sandwiches."

"Somebody had better be," said Doris, and for a moment the whole Institute stood with its trays like a row of waitresses at a siege, and forty people laughed, and in the laugh I walked up the length of the tent to the table at the end.

My hands had begun to shake, a fine tremor like a spring in a clock. At the third trestle I put them on the table's edge and held on, the way I do at the bench when a gathering resists, and found it steadier than I had expected.

"Morwenna," I said.

She was standing behind the white cloth with her hands folded on the sash, the apple cheeks pink, the pearl studs in. The Regatta Book lay open before her at the last written page, where the winners' names go in the treasurer's hand: *Trevenna, Provident, 1988*, and under it a blank. She smiled at me with great warmth. "My handsome. Has it come to you to say a few words? I didn't think you'd the heart. Sit down, I've saved you a slice."

"May I look at the Book?"

"The Book?"

"The hinge," I said. "The one you mended. I said on Tuesday I couldn't find the join. I should like to look at it properly before the cox signs, if nobody minds. It's an old woman's whim."

There was a rustle down the tent, a polite settling, of people deciding that this was the sort of thing I would do. I felt them lose interest in me. It is a sensation I have known all my life, and for the first time I put it to use. Morwenna looked at me for the length of one breath. Then she lifted the Regatta Book in both hands and held it out across the table, smiling, and laid it in my arms like a child at a christening.

"Of course," she said. "Mind the corners. And you'll have a bit of cake, Dot, before you start, you're white as a sheet."

"Afterwards."

"Mind you do."

It was heavier than a ledger. I held it against my chest and my heart went, once, hard, against its boards.

"I want to tell you about a hammer," I said.

I did not raise my voice. I have found that when you have never been listened to in a room, the one thing you must not do is shout, because they have a rehearsed answer to a woman who shouts. I spoke as I speak to a customer over the counter about the condition of a spine, and the tent, being a tent full of people who had come for sandwiches and found themselves at the end of a sentence, bent forward to hear.

"On the morning Aubrey Trevithick was found," I said, "the backing hammer in my press room hung face-out on its nail. That is how my husband hung it. Ned was left-handed, and it falls to his hand that way. I have hung it face to the wall for twenty-seven years, and so does every right-handed person who ever worked at that bench. Whoever hung it after Aubrey was dead was somebody who had watched Ned, or been taught by him, so long ago that the habit had gone into the wrist."

"Dot, my handsome," said Morwenna gently, "half the village watched Ned hang that hammer. I came in and out with the buns for thirty years. A person sees a hammer."

"Quite right," I said. "A person does."

"Whoever sewed the Carne Rutter this spring," I went on, "turned the kettle stitch to the left. Ned's stitch. He taught it to his pupils because he never thought there was another way. It was sewn fast and even, by a hand that did not stop to think, and Kit, who learned it on a hassock at ten years old, has sewn like a wet Tuesday ever since."

A noise went through the tent at "wet Tuesday", something between a laugh and a sob, and Demelza at the back looked at the roof.

"You'll have to forgive Ned his sayings," said Morwenna.

"The paste on the hinge was Ned's paste. Wheat starch, a pinch of alum, a drop of clove oil. It smells of Christmas. Only Kit, Jago and I ever made it, and Ned."

"My dear, I've put cloves in my plum cake for forty years. Half the lane has had a jar of that paste off Ned for their wallpaper."

"There was beeswax on the thread. I use tallow; so did Ned. Beeswax is a hobbyist's choice."

"I sewed the Institute's banner with beeswax," said Morwenna, "and you told me so yourself, and you said it was a lovely job."

"I did." My hands were beginning to shake against the cloth of the Book, and I laid them flat on the red label, to feel the boards. "On the Saturday night someone broke into the Bindery and took Ned's notebook out of the paste cupboard. They forced the window. They did not force the cupboard. And they shut my cat in it with a saucer of milk, one of my own blue-rimmed saucers, and laid a teaspoon beside it with the handle to the door."

Somebody breathed in. It was Ada Bolitho.

"Quire bites everyone," I said. "She has bitten the police and me when I was late. She was carried to the cupboard and fed without a mark on anyone. That was done by a person she knew."

"She is a lovely cat," said Morwenna, "when she's not hungry."

"And the key." I had not known I was going to come to it. "Ned's notebook, which a girl of sixteen copied two pages of in May, says there was a key to that cupboard on a hook in the press room, lent on Saturdays to a pupil, and that it was to be fetched back. The hook has been bare as long as I have known it. A key like that stays in the pocket of a coat for thirty years and more, because nobody ever asks for it."

"Ned gave keys to half the lane," said Morwenna. "He'd have given away his boots if you'd stood still."

There was not an unkind note in her voice. That was what I could not bear. She answered every point with courtesy, as one answers a mistaken but dear person at a meeting, and the more courteous she was the more of that large wet tent turned its face towards her, on her side. Garrow's hand had crept to the trestle in front of him and lay flat there, quite still.

"On Tuesday evening at the Institute, with the urn running, you told the committee that the strip light over my nipping press gave you a headache. You had stuck your head in at Easter." I made myself look at her. "That light was fitted in October. It comes on in three stages: a flicker, a stammer, a light. A person has to stand there and wait. On the morning Aubrey was found you told me, and Demelza, and Inspector Garrow, that you had not set foot in that room since my husband died."

"I might have looked in at the shop door with the Easter buns," said Morwenna. "A figure of speech, Dot. I couldn't tell you now." For the first time, a very small line appeared between the grey brows, as a pencil mark appears when you press a little too hard. "I do see how it looks."

"And lastly there is the K." I turned. Demelza, at the back pole, without a word, lifted a small clear bag by one corner so that the front benches could see it: a charred scrap with a silver trace of pencil that the light just found. "That was under the grate of the press-room stove. It is in her hands today, and in the Inspector's, because I kept it five days when I ought not to have, and any blame for those days is mine. Aubrey wrote on the pastedowns of the Carne before they were burned. K., cat. 114. That is Kit's catalogue number for the rutter. Aubrey noted that Kit had flagged the paper. It was a footnote, and nobody ever meant it as an accusation. The line under it says *Mrs P. in the mor*, because he was waiting for me in the morning. And at eleven the night before I walked past his window and did not look in."

My voice did something at the end of that, and I let it. At the back pole Garrow said nothing at all, which in him was a kind of speech.

"Now I should like to measure the Book," I said.

Nobody said anything. The canvas dripped.

I laid the Regatta Book on the white cloth, open at the back board, and took the dividers from my bag. I opened them across the edge of the board until the two points just kissed the front and the back of the thickness, and held them up, and carried them to the brass gauge Tamsin held out flat on her palm, and read the figure aloud. "Three and a half." I did the same for the front board. "Three and a half." I took Tamsin's red book. "In 1964 my husband wrote in his job book, 'Boards two millimetres and not a hair more.' I measured them on Tuesday night with this gauge, as a joke, and you have all just watched me measure them again."

Garrow had come off his trestle. He stood at the third table, not quite beside me, and his eyes were on the dividers.

"Ned bound them thick," said Morwenna.

"He wrote two."

"He was always one for changing his mind, my dear. I recall him saying, after, that he'd gone up a touch because of the wind."

"I do not think so." I turned the pages of Tamsin's book to the first, and I read aloud, as steadily as I could, with a hand on the table's edge to hold it, the line that she had copied in her clear upright script from my husband's green notebook. "'M. Hext. Left-handed, forced right at school. Nineteen fifty-five to fifty-eight. Best I've had.'"

"WHAT'S SHE SAYING?" said Gwen Trewhella.

"That Morwenna can bind," whispered Doris Rosevear, loudly.

"OF COURSE SHE CAN," said Gwen, with great contentment. "SHE DID THE BANNER."

Nobody laughed. It was a different silence from the other, and it went up through the tent like water up a wick. Forty people looked at me. I had not been looked at by so many people at once since Ned's funeral, and then I had been looked at as a widow, an object of tact. This was the other thing. They were looking at me with the faces of people who have been shown that the page is not blank, and Ada Bolitho's hand had gone to her hat.

"I loved you, Morwenna," I said.

I said it in an ordinary voice. It was the hardest sentence of my life, and it took no longer to say than 'pass the teapot'.

"I think I still do. You held my hand through 'Abide With Me.' You have put saffron cake on my step at every bad time since 1962. I would have given you anything you asked, and you never asked. I only wanted you to know that before the rest."

Morwenna's face did not change. The mild blue eyes met mine across the table, level and warm, and the lines about them gathered, a little, as if at a joke she was too well-mannered to share. "That is very kind of you, Dot," she said. "And you've done a great deal of thinking, and you haven't eaten. You must sit."

She sat down herself, in the folding chair behind the white cloth, as one sits at the end of a long committee, with her hands in her lap.

That was when Quire jumped.

I do not know how she came to the tent. I had left her in the kitchen at noon, among the dividers, with the door shut. Perhaps she had gone through the window; perhaps she had walked down behind the crowd at the heel of the gig race, as she does, in the unconcern of a person for whom the world has been arranged. She came across the grass from the Slip end where the canvas lifted, black and white and one-eared, past forty pairs of feet, and went up onto Morwenna's knees in a single movement, turned round once, and settled, with her bib to the roof and her eyes half shut, like a bun into a tin.

Nobody moved. In the back of my head, as clear as a bell in a cold room, I heard myself say it on a Friday morning in July to nobody at all: *Quire, who hisses at everyone.*

Morwenna looked down at the cat. She put out her left hand and stroked the torn ear, once, with a finger, as one touches a thing to check it is still there. Then she lifted Quire in both hands, very gently, and set her down on the grass.

She stood up. She took the Regatta Book from the white cloth, with the dividers lying open across the boards where I had left them, and the dividers slid on to the cloth with a small bright sound. She tucked the Book against the sash, as a woman puts her handbag under her arm to leave a meeting at the end of the agenda. Then she walked down the length of the tent, between the benches, nodding to Edna and to Mrs Trewhella, to the open flap, where Jago Rowe stood against the daylight with his big hands hanging. He stepped aside. He held the canvas back for her, as a man does for a lady, and she went out into the sun.

Nobody stopped her. I have gone over that a good many times, and the nearest I can come to it is that nobody present could think what the form of words was.
