# Ash in the Stove

Nobody rakes out a stove in July, which is why I did.

The press room had been given back to me on Saturday and taken away again on Sunday, and on Monday morning at half past seven it was mine once more, with a board nailed over the broken sash and a fine grey powder over everything the Truro man had thought worth touching. He had been thorough with the stove. The black iron lid wore a coat of fingerprint dust like mildew on a loaf, and the fire door, the damper and the little brass knob of the oven were furred with it. I could not lay paste on a bench that had that in the room. I put on Ned's old gardening gloves, filled a bucket in the yard, and started at the top.

It is a squat, cross-tempered stove, older than I am, with a rocking grate and an ash pan the size of a bread tin, and I had lit it last on Wednesday for the paste and let it die by supper. That is the thing I kept coming back to as I scrubbed. On Friday morning, when I put my palm flat on the lid beside a dead man, the iron had been warm. A stove that has gone out on Wednesday night does not hold its heat till Friday. It had been fed since, and fed late, and nobody had said so; and nobody had said so because it was July, and nobody lights a stove in July, and a policeman who sees cold grey ash in a firebox in a summer month looks at it exactly as long as he looks at the weather.

Looking at it myself, I realised, I had not seen it either. I had been looking at the floor.

The kindling box beside the stove, a fish crate of split pine, was down by a good double handful. If you had asked me on Thursday I should not have been able to tell you how many sticks were in it. But I had filled it myself on Wednesday, and I knew the look of a full box, and this was not one.

I opened the fire door and the smell came out to meet me: cold ash, flat and grey and slightly sweet, the smell of the morning after bonfire night, quite wrong in a room with the sun coming down through the skylight and a bumblebee working at the glass. I put the shovel in. The ash was fine as flour and white as a miller's hat. I shook the grate, and the rest of it dropped, and I drew out the pan and carried it into the yard and set it on a sheet of newspaper, and went in for the sieve from the shop.

A spoonful at a time I sieved it, like a woman making a very poor cake. There were clinkers the size of walnuts. There were the pale curls of burnt kindling, and a black shred of something that had been cloth. And on the third pass, when the pan was nearly bare, I looked into the dark of the firebox with the torch, down past the grate bars to the iron floor where the draught pulls things, and something lay curled there like a dead leaf.

Touching it was out of the question. I went to the shop, and came back with a sheet of blotting paper, and slid it under the thing as Ned taught me to lift a dried flower, an inch at a time, holding my breath so as not to blow it to pieces.

It was a corner of paper about the size of a playing card. It had been laid paper once, buff coloured, with the faint ribbed pattern you can see against the light; the burn had taken the rest of it and left this, black and brittle, the edges rolled and glittering. On the back clung grey fibres, and I knew them at once. They came off a board. This had been pasted down to the inside of a cover.

On the flat of my two hands I carried it to the bench. I clamped the lamp, and swung its arm down, and turned the shade until the light came in low across the char instead of down on it, a trick I use for reading the ghosts of old blind-tooling on a leather spine. And the black woke, and showed me silver.

Pencil. A soft one, a 2B, in a small upright hand with the sevens crossed in the continental way. Graphite is only carbon, but it holds a polish that soot has not, and under that slanting light the lines stood up off the burn like writing in frost. There were two of them, and the second was cut off by the char in the middle of a word.

*K. – cat. 114 –*

And lower down, further in:

*Mrs P. in the mor*

I sat down on the cutting-board stool. I know that hand. I have seen it on every invoice and every note that ever came down Fish Cellar Lane from Aubrey Trevithick, signed *yrs, A.T.*, with a full stop after each initial as if the letters might otherwise run off. Booksellers pencil their notes on the endpaper; they always have. A man holds a book as the buyer, and he writes down what he finds, small and light so that it can be rubbed out. He had written on the endpapers of the Carne Rutter while he sat at my bench on Thursday night with his cane across his knee, and someone had torn those endpapers out and burned them in my stove.

The bumblebee banged at the glass for a while, and I let it.

*Mrs P. in the mor.* It did not take a binder to finish the word. He had said it to me himself, at a quarter to six on Thursday, in the shop doorway with his hat on, while I was handing him a brass key on a green wool loop and wanting only to be rid of him. *Tomorrow I shall have something to say to you, Mrs Pellow. I would rather be accurate than prompt.* And I had said something about the buns. I had come at ten to seven, on Friday, with the buns in a paper bag and a pot of paste to set, as I had told him I would. And he had been there, or had been there for eight hours, with the book open on the bench and his notes ready.

Mrs P. in the morning. I was Mrs P. He had written it down so as not to forget, and then he had waited.

* * *

I make a poor detective and a worse liar, but I can reason from a bench. Whoever burned those pages had not burned the book. It lay on the bench on Friday morning with its thread hanging out of it like roots, and it was worth, as Kit had told Loveday, a figure with a comma in it. A stolen book is money; a pencilled note is a rope. Anyone with a head would burn the rope and keep the cash. They had taken the notes out of the covers and fed them to my stove and left the rutter to whoever came next, and what that told me, sitting on the stool with the light on the scrap, was that the person who did it had cared about being named a good deal more than about being rich.

Nothing about that pleased me. It made them neater than a thief. It also made them somebody who had known what to look for, which was a thing Aubrey had written on a page of a book that only Aubrey, and perhaps Kit, had been reading.

I turned the lamp a little. *K.* A letter with a full stop; a man's initial or a woman's. *Cat. 114* was a number I knew. Kit had given Loveday his typed valuation on the Friday of the fair, a good clean list of her library, ending at lot 114, the Carne Rutter, *as far as the Carne and not one inch past it.* K. for Kit. Kit's catalogue. I told myself that was all it was, a clerk's reference. A man pencils the cataloguer's number beside his note about a book. It is what you do.

Several times I told myself so, and each time it sounded more like a thing one tells oneself.

Well. I had promised. I got up, and went into the shop, and rang the police house.

* * *

Demelza came in eight minutes, in uniform, her plait pinned up under her helmet, and I took her through to the bench without a word and swung the lamp. She bent over the blotter with her hands behind her back. I watched her read it. I saw her get to the K and I saw her go still, the way Quire does at a sound in the wall.

"Kit," she said. "Or Kitto."

"It's Kit's catalogue number. He numbered the Carne lot one-fourteen. It's the number on the list."

"It's a K and a number, and one of the two Ks in this village is me." She said it quite pleasantly. "He didn't know me from a hole in the road, Mr Trevithick. But he might have, by the end of the week." She straightened. "And the other one?"

"Mrs Pellow. In the morning. He told me he'd have something to say on Friday. I came at ten to seven."

She did not answer that. She took out her notebook, took off the elastic band and put it on again, and put the notebook away without writing in it. In the quiet I could hear the bumblebee still working at the skylight.

"I ought to bag that," she said. "I ought to put it in a bag with a label and take it to Truro and give it to Inspector Garrow, and by lunchtime he'd have Kit in the back of a car."

"On a pencilled K."

"On a pencilled K, a hammer from Ned's nail, a man who was at the Bindery twice that week and a cataloguer's name on the thing that was stolen." She pushed her helmet back an inch. "I'd like a day. I'd like to find out who K. is before somebody else decides. It stays here. In your hands, in whatever you've got. I'll come with a bag when I know what I'm going to say and to whom."

"That isn't procedure."

"No. It's Monday."

The shop bell rang.

I had the blotter folded into my apron pocket before I knew I had moved. Kit stood in the doorway between the shop and the press room with a pint of milk in each hand, as he does every morning, because I will not drink tea made with milk that has stood. He looked at the uniform, then at a spot politely beside it.

"Sorry. Morning."

"Mr Pellow."

"It's Kit." He said it to the milk. "It was Kit, anyway. When I last, sorry."

"I'm on duty."

"I know. I only wanted to say. About Saturday. The darts. I was at the board, sorry, I was, and I know it's not, it isn't much of, I didn't mean to put you in the middle."

"You were at the board," said Demelza, "with a man and his ferrets."

"Sorry."

"That's the thing, isn't it." Her voice had not risen. It had gone small and level and extremely clear. "I told you in March last year that I'd been passed over for sergeant, and you said sorry. As if you'd done it."

"I wished I had, so there'd be somebody to shout at."

"I didn't want somebody to shout at. I wanted you to shout. I wanted a plate thrown."

"Not at my paste pots," I said, from the corner.

Neither looked at me. Kit said, very low, "You didn't ask me to stay. You said you were going to Plymouth for a course and I said, sorry, I said I thought."

"You thought. You think a lot, Kit. You stand there thinking in a pair of boots." She had her helmet strap in two fingers and she was pulling it so hard it must have hurt. "Fourteen months, and the first word you have for me in a doorway is sorry. It's a weather report. It isn't an answer."

There was a silence in which nobody breathed in the right order.

"Don't be kind to me," Demelza said. "Anyone but you. I can't do the job if you're kind."

She went out past him, not touching, and the bell took the whole quarrel and shook it out into the lane. Kit stood with the milk in his hands. After a while he said "Sorry," to nobody, and took both bottles into the shop, and I heard him fill the kettle, carefully, like a man with a limb in plaster.

* * *

When the kettle began to sing I took the blotter out of my pocket. I stood at the shelf where Ned's things stay, and took down his tin of bone folders, a toffee tin with the Silver Jubilee on the lid, gone brown at the corners, in which his tools still lie in the order he left them: the long folder with the chipped tip, the little one for the hollows, the curved one that he said was a whale's rib and I always knew was a shin of beef. I eased the scrap into the cloth lining and set the lid on the top. It closed with a sound like a held breath.

In three years I do not think I had said a word aloud to the empty room. I said it to the tin, and the stove, and the board over the window, and the silver on the black that was no longer there to be seen.

"He was waiting for me."
