# Smelled Like Church

The third step of the net-loft stair has creaked under every foot since 1962, and at ten past five on Monday morning it had creaked under one I could not account for.

I heard it from my bed, twenty yards up the lane, with the window open and the harbour as quiet as it ever gets: a short dry complaint, a long pause, then a second one, softer, as of someone going up a ladder in her stockinged feet. It might have been a gull. A gull can make a surprising noise on slate. But I had lain awake four nights listening to that lane and I knew what a gull sounds like on a roof, and this was not one.

So at half past two that afternoon, with the stove clean and the Silver Jubilee tin shut on its secret, I put the last of Saturday's saffron buns in a paper bag, for no reason I could have defended, and climbed the steps.

There are eleven of them, granite, up the outside wall to a door under the eaves, with an iron rail that Ned painted black every Easter and that I have not painted since. The padlock hung open on its staple. It has not truly locked since the winter of 1979, when the screws gave up the wood, and Ned used to say it was there to be a conscience. I pushed the door with the flat of my hand and went in.

The net loft runs the length of the building under the slates, one long room with a floor of broad boards worn silver, and from the rafters three seine nets still hang as they have hung since before the pilchards went: tarred and stiff with salt, grey as old sails that have forgotten the wind. Nobody has fished with them since 1953. I keep the stock I cannot sell up here, broken boards and spoiled paper and a press with a cracked screw, and the smell is tar and hemp and slate and ancient fish, which is the smell of the whole village if you take the people out of it.

Under the far gable, beneath the little window that looks down the lane, somebody had made a bed.

It was a sleeping bag, army green, zipped to the chin of nobody, with a folded anorak for a pillow. Beside it stood a milk crate holding six library books stamped with Truro's blue date, a jam jar of pencils, and a rubber torch of the square-headed sort that takes a battery the size of a brick. I looked for a candle. A candle in a loft hung with tarred twine is the kind of thing that gets onto the front of the *Cornishman*, and I was glad to find none. Four exercise books were stacked on the crate, edges squared, blue covers, with the Truro school crest on the front.

I opened the top one. I am not proud of that.

It was full of spines. Page after page of them, drawn in biro on the feint-ruled paper, each in its own tall oblong: spines with raised bands, spines with gilt panels worked up in the fine crosshatch of a girl who has measured how a real gilt line catches the light, spines with the tails of the headbands curled just so. She had given them titles. *The Tides of Trevenna, Vol. III. A Short History of Mackerel. Letters from a Pilot's Widow.* There were forty to a page, and the books on the shelves of the Bindery have never looked as good. Near the back there was a full-page drawing, in blue, of a sewing frame, upright, with the cords strung and the tapes laid and the needle caught in the thread, and notes down the margin in brackets, in a hand much better than mine. *(tapes: five, linen.) (kettle stitch, head and tail.) (this is the bit that matters).*

"Right," said a voice behind me. "So."

I did not jump. I would like that recorded. I closed the exercise book and put it back on its stack, squared, as she had left it, and turned round.

She stood at the head of the stairs with her hands behind her back and her jaw stuck out. The bleached stripe had fallen across her left eye, the way a flag drops in no wind. I had not heard the third step. I realised that she had stepped over it.

"It's private," said Tamsin Bray. "Up here."

"The loft belongs to the Bindery. Which on a bad month is me."

"I haven't broken anything."

"I never said you had."

"You've got a face on."

"It's the only one I have."

She sulked as other people paint: with depth, in layers, over several sessions. She sat on the milk crate with her knees drawn up and her chin on them, and said nothing for a minute and a half, and then said "Right," and nothing again. I took the bun out of its bag, to give my hands something to do. Her eyes went to it as a cat's go to a mouse that has dared to move.

"That's two days old."

"I don't mind."

I held it out. She took it and ate it with the concentration of a communicant, and half-way through, in a different voice, she said, "Mum doesn't know."

"About the loft."

"I've been up here since the exams. There's five of us in two rooms, and Terry's on lates, and the dog sleeps on my feet." She brushed crumbs off her knees. "Up here it's quiet. Nobody wants anything. You can see the whole lane from that window, and your door, and the corner of the press room." She looked at the little square of glass as at an old friend. "Nothing happens in Trevenna, so you have to watch it quite hard."

I let that one stand. I have been watched hard in my time and I know what it is worth.

"Thursday," I said.

She did not pretend. I will give her that. A grown woman would have had a dozen expressions to choose from, and she used none of them. "I was up here," she said. "I'd got my torch and a book. I didn't see anything till about half past nine."

"Tell me what you saw."

"Right." She put the last of the bun in her mouth, swallowed, and wiped her hand on her jumper, the great grey one that has been through two brothers. "Half past nine, the Reverend. Mr Nance. In his bicycle clips. He'd left the bike at the bottom, I saw him push it, and he came up and knocked, and Mr Trevithick opened the door, and the Reverend started on at him. I couldn't hear all of it. Something about the roof. And, 'you know what it's worth, Aubrey.'"

"And what did Aubrey say?"

"He said, 'Not tonight, Arthur.' Quite kind. Then he shut the door." She frowned. "Very quietly. Like when you shut a door on a baby. And the Reverend stood there and said sorry to it."

"He would."

"Then he went down the lane pushing the bike, and he was coughing." She counted on her fingers, which were inky to the second knuckle. "Five past ten, the lady with the nose stud. The one in the leather. The daughter. She didn't knock, she banged. She was in there fifteen minutes. I couldn't hear the words but I could hear the shape of it, and the shape was, shouting, and then not shouting, and then quite a lot of nothing."

"And she left?"

"Twenty past. I've got a watch, it was Dad's." She showed me: a man's steel wristwatch on a strap punched with new holes. "She came out with her face all, like this." She dragged the heel of her hand across both eyes. "And then she stood in the lane and kicked the wall. Twice. I think she hurt her foot."

I thought of Imogen Mayne on Saturday night, counting a hundred and thirty-five pounds for an otter with a cigarette going, and saying *well, that's that, then*, in a voice that was not about the money, and found I had stopped being able to dislike her.

"And after that?"

"After that nobody for ten minutes. Then half past ten." Tamsin pulled her knees in. "A lady came up from the bottom of the lane. In a mac, the pale sort with a belt, and the hood up. Not a scarf, an actual hood. She'd a big bag on her arm. She didn't knock. She put her hand flat on the door, like that, and it went in."

"It wasn't locked."

"It must have been on the latch. He'd have been expecting her."

I did not ask her how she knew he was expecting anyone. "Who was she?"

"I couldn't see. It was from above, and she had her head down, and everyone's short from above." She considered. "Not tall. Not fat. She walked like somebody carrying something that mustn't spill."

"Is that all?"

"No." She hesitated, and for the first time looked like what she was, which is a girl of sixteen who has been on her own in a loft with a very bad piece of knowledge for four days. "I had the window open for the air. She went right under it, to the door. And I smelled her."

"Smelled her."

"Like church," said Tamsin. "Polish and candles. Like the vestry on a Tuesday when Mum goes in to do the brass."

I stood in a room of tarred nets with the afternoon coming in grey through the glass and understood that I was being given something very much heavier than a bun.

Church. In Trevenna, church is a quarter of the population by Sunday and a tenth by weekday, and every woman in that tenth had done the brass or the flowers or the linen at one time or another, and every man had been in the vestry to sign something. The Reverend Nance himself smells of his own vestry: Brasso and hymn-book dust and mouse and cough drop. But Nance had been at the door at half past nine and gone home down the hill wheezing, and Tamsin was clear it had been a woman, by the walk. I own a mac the colour of porridge. So does every woman I know, and a hood, and a bag on her arm. A woman in a mac with the hood up is the most invisible article in the English countryside; I have been that article myself for thirty years, and not on Thursday only. If I had gone up that lane to commit a crime I could not have chosen better.

"Did you see her come out?"

"No. I got cold. I went down in the bag." She did not meet my eye. "I woke up when the church clock did twelve and there wasn't anybody."

"And me."

I had not meant to say it. It came out like a tooth.

Tamsin looked at the floor. "You went by about eleven," she said. "You were humming. You looked at the shop window and the front door, and you didn't look in at the press room. The light was off by then."

"Yes," I said. I had told nobody that. I had told it, in the end, to a girl in a sleeping bag. "No. I didn't."

"I'm not going to say," said Tamsin. "If you don't tell Mum."

I sat down on a tea chest. For a moment neither of us said anything. The torch lay on the crate with its square head turned up at the slates, and I reached across and switched it on, to be doing something, and the beam went up through the hanging nets and laid a great scale-pattern of knots and shadows on the underside of the roof, like the belly of a fish. In the middle of it the blue exercise book on the stack showed up, with the sewing frame drawn on the open back page. Someone had written under it: *(Ned's? ask).*

"You are a witness," I said. "A good one. You have the times and the smell and a direction of travel. You must tell Constable Kitto."

"No."

"Tamsin."

"No, they tell your mum. They have to. It's the law." She said it with the heartbreaking logic of the sixteen-year-old. "And Mum'll say I've been sleeping out with all sorts, and she'll send me to Auntie Jean's in Redruth. Auntie Jean's got a launderette and a dog that hates me. I'd rather be stabbed."

"That is not a remark you should make in this particular building."

"I'd rather," she said stubbornly, "be told off by Mr Nance." She pushed her hair out of her eye. "If you tell, I'll say I never. I'll say I made it up for a laugh."

"You would not."

"I would. I'm good at it. Ask Mr Pascoe."

I looked at her, at the jam jar of pencils and the library books and the careful, fierce, sixteen-year-old face, and I saw that the child would do it. She would deny every word, and a poor witness is no use to anyone. I know about stubbornness. I have kept the Bindery open on the strength of it.

"Very well," I said. "A bargain. I will speak to your mother myself. Before anyone else does. I will tell her something true."

"What true?"

"I'll think of it on the way."

"That's what I'm scared of." But she let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "And you won't tell the constable?"

"I will tell her what I must when I must, and I shall do it so that your name is on the last line of the page and not the first." I rose, because my knees were beginning to say things. "You are going to be sensible, in the meantime. No candles. No stairs in the dark; the third step is bad."

"I know. I step over it."

"I noticed."

At the door I turned. She had not moved from the crate. She was sitting very upright, with the grey jumper pulled over her knees, and the torch-light was in her face, and for once she did not seem to have anything ready.

"Mrs Pellow." It came out quickly, as if she had been carrying it all afternoon. "Can I watch you do corners next?"

I opened my mouth to say something about the Education Authority. What I said was, "Yes."

I had said it before I had thought, which is not a thing I do. I went down the eleven steps in the grey light with the rail wet under my hand, and I did not step on the third.
