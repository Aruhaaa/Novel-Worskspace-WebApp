# Measuring the Boards

The bunting went up in the night. I watched the last of it from my kitchen window as the light came: forty yards along the harbour wall, from the corner of the Lantern to the lifeboat house, hung by men who had been up since three and did not wish to be thanked. Red and white, a yellow that was meant for Cornwall, a run of blue triangles borrowed from some signal locker, all in the order Jago likes and no other. He had been at it since he left his own kitchen. I did not need to be told. When a big man cannot sleep he finds something with a rope on it.

By five o'clock there were four cups on my table with the tea going cold in them, in the order they had been poured.

Imogen Mayne had come because my note said her father's name and nothing else. It was in Tamsin's hand, since mine would have taken longer to read, and Tamsin had pushed it under the door of Room 3 at the Lantern at half past four and come back up the lane at a run. Imogen followed in the leather jacket, her cropped head bare, smelling of cigarettes and the Lantern's carpet, and sat down at my table without being asked. "If this is about the piano," she said, "I've said all I'm going to say." It was not about the piano.

Demelza had arrived ten minutes before, from the police house, with her tunic buttoned one hole out. Whatever she had done with Jago's cell and her conscience in the interval she did not offer, and I did not ask. Tamsin sat on the dresser stool with the exercise book on her knee and Quire beside her like a bookend, and watched the three of us as a girl watches a lesson for which she has already done the homework.

I told it in order. If you tell a thing in order to tired people, it is believed as a matter of rhythm. Tamsin's copy; the pupil list; Jago's boots, and Loveday's piquet, and a cake in a tin on a Friday morning in a hall. I gave it flatly, the way I would give the dimensions of a book, and while I talked Demelza read the two pages with her finger under the lines like a child, and Imogen smoked.

"The woman with the cake," said Imogen, when I came to the name. "The one who put her arm round me at the bar last Thursday." She laughed once, down her nose. "She said Daddy was the most exact man she'd ever met, and that exactness was a kind of love. I was so grateful I said thank you to her sleeve."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It was a good line. I'd have used it myself." She tapped ash into the saucer I had given her. I had never in my life given anybody a saucer for that purpose, and Ned, who smoked his pipe in the yard like a man doing penance, would have fainted.

"Right," said Demelza, and shut the book. "Facts." She said them to the table. "A schoolgirl's copy of a notebook that's been stolen, so it's a copy of nothing I can produce. A dead man's scrap with half a name on it. A pensioner's hearing aid. A landlord I let out last night on my own say-so, who'll tell you where he was only in a dark kitchen. And a cat."

"And the beeswax," said Tamsin.

"And the Jam Tarts' sheet," I said.

"Is the light any use?" said Imogen.

We looked at her. She had her chin in her hand and the cigarette burning down between two fingers, and she was looking at nothing, like a person who has been trying to remember something for several minutes and has at last decided to say it badly. "At your meeting, on Tuesday. The bunting, the sandwiches. I was trying to be invisible with an egg roll. She went on about a headache. She said the light at your place had given her one. In your workroom, over the big iron press. She'd stuck her head in at Easter."

The kitchen did not change. Down on the harbour a halyard knocked against a mast. "Which light?" I said.

"A long one. A tube. It flickers."

"It was fitted last October," I said. "It comes on in three stages: a flicker, a stammer, then the light. A person would have to stand under it and wait for the third to be given a headache. And on the morning Aubrey was found, with a teapot in her hand and half the lane listening, she told Demelza and Garrow and me that she had not set foot in that room since Ned died."

"March 'eighty-six," said Demelza. She wrote it down and underlined it twice, and I saw a muscle move at the hinge of her jaw.

"I did not sleep," I said. "At three o'clock I got Pieter's leaf out from under the Concordance and measured it with my gauge. Laid rag, sized, a hair under a sixth of a millimetre. Say point one five." I took the little brass thing out of my cardigan and set it on the table among the cups. "On Tuesday night at the Institute, to be funny, I put this on the front board of the Regatta Book while Jago and Mr Nance quarrelled about who should carry it to the finish. Three and a half."

Tamsin did not need to be asked. She opened the exercise book to the second page and put her finger on the line. "Two," she said. "'And not a hair more.'"

"I picked it up that night and thought it too heavy for a ledger. I put it down to Ned. I put everything down to Ned that week; it's a habit." I looked at my hands. "He wrote two millimetres because he wanted a book an old man could hold up in a wind. And the one I weighed in the hall could have been used to prop a door."

Tamsin had found a pen and was writing on the back of her left hand, in the clear upright script, with her tongue between her teeth. "Three and a half take off two," she said. "One and a half. That's one board. There's two boards, so three." She looked up at the ceiling and her lips moved. "Three divided by nought point one five."

We waited. In the grate behind her a cinder settled.

"Twenty," said Tamsin.

Nobody said anything. Imogen put out her cigarette in the saucer, slowly, and went on pressing it after it was out.

"Twenty what?" she said.

"Leaves." I heard my voice come out level, and was ashamed of how well I had learned it. "A millboard can be split down its thickness, like a sandwich, and something flat and old laid in, and the layers pasted back round it. A good hand does it so that you couldn't find the join with a thumbnail. Then you cover it in calf and marbled paper, and put a red label on the front, and offer to take the Institute's treasurer's job."

"Sixty leaves are missing," said Demelza.

"Four are in the false book. Sixty are gone. A dealer has some, I should think; that is what a dealer is for, and the Dutchman's one folded leaf says he does. But twenty, I say, are in a ledger that has been carried round Trevenna for a month in a flour sack, being admired."

Demelza got up. She is not a woman who walks about when she thinks, but she went the length of my kitchen, which is eleven feet, and back, with her hands behind her like a constable on a beat.

"I can't get a warrant on thick boards," she said. "I'd put it to the justices at Truro with Garrow's name or mine, and they'd ask who measured, and I'd say the aunt of the man we're holding for the murder, and the clerk would look at me the way you look at a woman who tells you the bus is late because of the Pope. Garrow won't hear it. He has a thumbprint on a lamp, a Dutchman, and a theory with a train timetable in it. And I can't so much as mention the leaf without giving him you and me together." She stopped. "And if I walk up to the president of the Institute on regatta morning and ask for the club ledger, she says, 'Of course, my handsome, I'll bring it round after the cup,' and she doesn't."

"No," I said. "You don't ask in private."

"Dot."

"She has never in her life refused anyone anything in public. She would hand over her handbag first. If I ask her in front of forty people, with the cup on the table, to let me look at a hinge I admired in the Institute's hall on Tuesday, she will say yes and smile, because the alternative would be to be seen to say no."

"The prize-giving," said Tamsin.

"The prize-giving. She'll lay the Book open on the table for the cox to sign. I shall ask to see it. And I shall have my dividers."

It was a good plan. I mention that because it was also the worst plan I have ever made, and I watched the three of them find that out in the order in which their faces permitted it. Imogen was first.

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I shall have measured the Institute's ledger with a pair of dividers in a tea tent, in front of the whole parish, and accused its president of murder over a plate of egg sandwiches." The words were quite steady, as if they belonged to someone I had met at a funeral. "I shall not be asked to mend so much as a library ticket in this county again. Well."

"That isn't how it's done," said Demelza. "If she takes fright. If she puts it in the harbour."

"It floats, mostly," said Tamsin. "In 1931 it took a boy in a punt." Everyone looked at her. "It's in the notebook."

"Ten past three," I said.

"Ten past," Tamsin agreed, and she crossed her ankles on the stool and became, for a moment, a woman of forty. "Not before the race, or she'll say she's bringing it to you after. Not after the prize-giving, because the Book goes back in its sack and she goes home. You do it when the cox is waiting to sign, with the pen out and the cup on the cloth. That's ten past three if Trevenna time holds, and it will. Twenty minutes late is what it does."

"I am to stand where?" said Demelza.

"At the back."

"I'm not agreeing to anything."

"No."

"I want that heard."

"It's heard."

It was Imogen who broke it. She reached down beside her chair for the leather bag, a big creased thing with a broken buckle, and brought out a buff folder, and put it on the table without opening it. "I don't know why I brought this," she said. "It was in the case with the letter. I thought, it's in his hand, and I don't know what to do with a thing that's in his hand."

The folder held Kit's typed schedule of the Penhallow shelves, forty-one sheets, with a pencil going up and down the margins like a man walking a cliff. I knew the pencil before I saw the words. Two B, soft, and every seven crossed in the continental fashion, as I had seen them in silver on a scrap in a stove. Against lot 31, a Hakluyt: M.H. to mend, L.P. consents, Feb. Against lot 57, a Book of Hours: M.H., mending. Not on shelf, Apr. Against two almanacs and a snuffbox in a drawer: M.H. again, and a question mark, and on the last sheet in the bottom corner, small, as an old man notes a thing for himself and does not mean the world to see it, Mrs H. is kind, & careful. Cf. all of the above. Ask Mrs P.

Demelza read the last line twice. Then she turned the sheet to the window, where the light was coming up the colour of weak tea, and breathed out through her nose. "That," she said, "Garrow can't ignore."

"It doesn't say she's a thief."

"It says she was handed seven things by a house where a murdered man was sent to value the contents, and that he wrote her initials against every one and ended with a note to you. It's dated. It's in the hand of a man whose letter Garrow has had in his own pocket for three days. He'll hate it." She put the sheet down on the table very gently, as a person lays something hot on a cloth. "I can put this in his hand before the cup."

I took the scrap out of my cardigan, in its fold of blotting paper, and put it into her palm. She looked at it. Then she took an evidence bag from inside her tunic, where it seemed she had been carrying one for some days, and sealed the scrap into it herself and signed the label on her knee. "That's the first thing I've done by the book all week," she said, "and I'd like it noted that it doesn't feel any better."

"Put down that I held it five days."

"I'll put down what happened."

"Then put down that too."

"And at the back?"

"I'll be at the back. At the back where I can see the Book and the door." She buttoned her tunic correctly for the first time that morning. "If you're wrong I was at the lavatories."

"The Institute's lavatory," said Tamsin, "is a bucket behind a hessian screen."

"Then I shall be very discreet."

They went, one after another, as the light climbed the dresser. Imogen went to find a clean shirt and a face to put on her father's cup. Tamsin went to find the quay and the programme and the exact minute of everything, which she can do with one look at a blackboard. Demelza went, I suppose, to find whichever of her several consciences was senior.

When they had gone I took the tool roll off the dresser and untied it on the table. I laid out my dividers, my second-best bone folder, and the little brass gauge, squared to the edge of the table with the last cold cup, like a woman setting a table for a guest who will not leave. Quire got up and sat among them. She does not usually care for dividers.

Outside, the harbour had filled with flags.
