# Tamsin's Exercise Book

"It got a B plus," said Tamsin, and laid the exercise book on my kitchen table the way a woman lays down a hand of cards she knows is better than yours.

It was a quarter past ten. I had put the kettle on, because it is what one does, and moved Quire to the dresser among the blue-rimmed plates, which takes five minutes and a certain amount of language. The single bulb over the table made the room look as though it were being interviewed. Through the window above the sink the riding lights of the moored boats lay along the harbour in a short row, yellow as commas.

The book was red, school issue, with a label stuck on the front in a hand I knew from the shop order forms: TREVENNA, VANISHING TRADES. T. BRAY, 4B. Underneath, in green ink, someone had written Neat. More sea next time. L.

"That's Mr Lanyon," said Tamsin. "He set it in May. A trade that has gone or is going in your own parish. Everybody else did pilchard curing. Tommy Rosevear did pilchard curing twice and got a C. So I did bookbinding, because it was in the lane and I could watch."

"You didn't tell me."

"You were busy being cross about the electricity bill." She turned a page without looking at it. "You lent me the notebook on a Saturday. You said mind it, and you said you couldn't read it yourself because you couldn't stand to see his hand. I sat at the shop desk and you did the ledger. I'd done two pages and you said that'll do, I've got to lock up, so I did two."

I did remember. I remembered a green notebook going across the counter in my own hand, and thinking what a relief it was to have it out of the cupboard, and that I should never have to think of it again.

"I copied them exact," said Tamsin. "The words, I mean. I tidied the rest."

She had. I should like to say that I was too much moved to notice, but I am a bookbinder, and what I noticed was the hand. Ned wrote like a spider walking home from a wedding. Tamsin had taken what he wrote and set it down in a clear upright script like a row of well-sewn stitches, every seven crossed, every loop closed, with notes in square brackets in the margin where she had been unable to read him and said so. [pen changes here, green to blue-black.] [crossed out, can't read, begins with H?] It was a better hand than mine. She watched me see that, and she did not look modest.

"You've tidied his sevens."

"Everyone's sevens. Mr Lanyon said it was an A for the handwriting and it didn't count."

"It counts with me."

"Right," said Tamsin. "So." She flattened the book with the side of her hand, and I put on my glasses and read the first page.

> PUPILS. Saturdays, and any evening I can stand it.

> J.R., from 1951. Holds the paste. Will not sew. Says his fingers are for rope.

> M. Hext (left-handed, forced right at school). 1955 to 58. Best I've had. Hook key to her for Saturdays. Fetch back.

> Second frame to M.H., wedding present, 1958. She will use it.

> C.J.P., my brother's boy, 1965, aged ten. Sews like a wet Tuesday. Will come.

I laughed at the last. I could not help it; I had watched Kit sew, and Ned was exact. Then I read the third line, and the laugh went out of me like air from a bellows, and I read it again.

"Hext is a common name," I said.

"In Trevenna?"

"There is Hedley Hext on the Chapel side. He is eighty-one and blind."

"Was he a girl in 1955?"

"She wasn't a Hext in 1955. She married Ronald in the spring of 'fifty-eight."

"The ink changes there. I put it in brackets." Tamsin leaned over and put a finger on the margin. "I thought he'd written the list out fair afterwards. At the wedding, maybe. When he gave the frame."

"I never knew there was a second frame."

"Why would you? You didn't read it."

She was right, and she said it without any cruelty, which was worse. I took off my glasses and put them on again. The kettle had begun to tick on the ring behind me.

"Page two," said Tamsin, and turned it for me.

> 1964. REGATTA BOOK for the committee. Foolscap ledger, 200 leaves, sewn on five cords, half calf, marbled sides, red label. Boards 2 mm millboard and not a hair more. It is carried down the quay in a wind by the chairman, and in 1931 Hocking's father dropped the old one in the harbour and it took a boy in a punt, the tide table and most of an afternoon to get it back. Light enough to drop.

> Charge: nine pounds and a pint (J.R.).

"Ned and his two millimetres," I said, and something in the figure caught, as a nail catches in a cardigan sleeve. I could not see what. It was ten at night and I had been in a state of siege since half past three, and I let it go. "He always did know exactly what he wanted a book to weigh."

"It's the one she showed round at the WI," said Tamsin. "Mum says. The one with the hinge she mended."

"Yes."

"Dot."

"I know what you're going to say."

"I'm going to say it anyway."

"Then I am going to say that it is an old man's list, written in a good mood. Morwenna cleaned his brushes. She brought him pasties. She was a girl in a bright coat in a workshop and he was kind to her." My voice had gone up, I noticed, into the register in which I speak to men from the gas board. "Morwenna Hext cannot bind a hymnbook. She has never mended anything in her life but a hem, and she did that badly."

"She mended the Regatta Book's back joint," said Tamsin. "You said yourself you couldn't see the join."

I had no answer to that. I got up and made the tea, which was a thing to do with my hands, and filled the kettle again and set it back on the flame for a second pot, and sat down.

The funeral was a Tuesday in March, and the wind took the hymn sheets out of the porch and sent them over the churchyard wall like gulls. I had forgotten my hat. I stood in the front pew of St Petroc's with my gloved hands folded and everyone's good opinion of Ned coming at me from behind, a lovely man, a lovely man, and at "Abide With Me" someone took hold of my left hand. I had thought it would be Kit. It was Morwenna, with a glove on one hand and not the other, singing the alto line in her small steady voice and holding on through "change and decay" so hard that my wedding ring left a mark on the next finger for two days. When we sat down she did not let go, and neither did I. In the porch afterwards, when people were saying lovely, she was the only one who said, "He was a careful one, Dot." It was the best thing that was said that day. I had kept it in my pocket for three years like a stone with a hole in it.

"She held my hand," I said. "Through the hymn."

"I know. Mum told me. She said she was lovely to you."

"She was."

"I'm not saying she wasn't." Tamsin's voice was careful, and it came to me that she was sixteen and had never had to say this to anyone. "I'm saying write it all down and see where it goes. Mr Lanyon says that about essays."

"I am not writing a case against Morwenna Hext in my kitchen at half past ten at night."

"Then I will." She held up her fingers, the blue ones, and began to turn them down. "Right. So. One. The woman in the mac smelled of church. Polish and candles. I thought vestry, I thought Mr Nance, so I went and sniffed things on Wednesday, in the hall. Everything churchy's got beeswax. The polish and the tapers. And the only person in Trevenna who smells of both at once is the one that sells the tapers and does the brass."

"Half the Institute does the brass on Wednesdays."

"Half the Institute doesn't keep eight hives."

I thought of a golden tail of thread lying in a seam of my cutting board, and an envelope in my drawer, and I said nothing about it, which Tamsin noticed.

"Two. She pours with her left. At the fair, with the honey, and at the quiz when she did the tea for our table. She cuts cake with her left. And she signs the lifeboat tin with her right, I watched, and she writes the answers with her right, and I thought: people do both. But they don't. Not like that. Not smooth."

"I saw her do it at Penhallow Court," I said. "She poured with her left and then saw me looking and changed hands. I took it for nerves."

"Three." Tamsin turned her head and looked at the dresser, where Quire sat among the plates with her bib very white and her ear very torn, watching us both with contempt. "That one hisses at everybody. At the police, at you when you're late, at Kit, and he's fed her for three years. She went into Morwenna's lap like she'd been doing it all her life. I was on the loft steps. I saw."

"A cat is not a witness."

"She's the only one of us with no reason to be polite."

The bulb hummed. Down on the harbour a halyard was knocking against a mast, slowly, as if somebody were being asked to come in.

"She left the quiz at ten to ten," I said. I had not meant to say it. "The Thursday. I found the sheet in Jago's biscuit tin, in two hands, with the time written at the foot of the music round. I asked her at the Lantern, lightly, and she said half past ten. I showed her the sheet, and she said her memory wasn't what it was, and Ronald had asked her if she was the new nurse, and she offered me cake out of her bag." I stopped. "I let it go."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted her to be forgetful." It was the truest thing I had said to anyone that week, and I said it to the tablecloth. "And Loveday Penhallow told me, unasked, in front of both of us, that Morwenna had sat up with her till midnight that night playing piquet. Her hearing aid squealed all the way through. She has that aid, Tamsin, it whistles when she tells a lie, and in the whole of that afternoon nobody noticed how often."

"Two lies," said Tamsin, "from two women who aren't liars."

"Yes."

I sat down again and pulled the exercise book towards me, and I did something I had not done in three years, which was to read my husband's words as though he were in the room and I were obliged to listen. Not the list. Not yet. I looked instead at the drawing on the facing page, which Tamsin had put in because Mr Lanyon liked diagrams: a sewing frame, Ned's, the oak one in the press room with its four pegs and its worn cross-bar, in pencil, every peg labelled. [oak, one peg newer wood.] And down the side, in her round-backed brackets: [there were TWO of these? Ned says 2 in the book. Where is the other one?]

I had been asking the wrong question for a week. I had asked who, and refused the answer, and gone on asking it. But the question was not who. Somebody had been in my shop, Demelza had said, on the first morning, and I had taken it for a thief with a key, because I had needed it to be. It was the other way about. The shop had never been used. It was the book that had been brought to the cover. Whoever it was had needed a frame, a month of evenings, a bench, a press of some sort and a pot of clove paste, in a village of six hundred where a woman cannot buy a stamp without the Post Office knowing what she means to write, and had done it all without anyone smelling Christmas in July.

"How," I said. "Not who. How. Where in Trevenna would you sew sixty leaves of a seventeenth-century pilot's book, left-handed, on a frame nobody knows you have?"

Tamsin said nothing. For once she had the sense to say nothing.

I pulled the notebook copy round and put my finger under the third line. "M. Hext," I read aloud. "Left-handed, forced right at school. Best I've had." I heard it as Ned would have said it, to the back of the stove, not looking up, in the voice in which he gave out his highest praise, which was a sort of grunt. I read it a second time, more quietly. "Best I've had."

He had never written that about anyone. I knew it as I knew the stairs. He had said of me, in his kindest mood, that I would do, and I had treasured it for twenty-seven years.

"Morwenna Hext was a better binder than I am," I said, and behind me the kettle, which I had set back on the flame for a second pot and forgotten, boiled over onto the hob with a noise like an audience.
