# Regatta Morning

Nobody looked at me on the morning of the regatta, and I have never been so grateful.

By eight the harbour looked like a quilt dropped from a height. The tide had gone out by then in its slow way and left the moorings leaning on the mud at all angles, each hull with a flag at its mast, so that the whole basin was a mosaic of triangles, reds and blues and Jago's one yellow, and the water in the channel the colour of pewter. Stalls ran the length of the quay: pasties, crab sandwiches, a coconut shy, the lifeboat tin rattling on its string. Ada Bolitho had a table by the Post Office wall for her raffle, and on it, in the raspberry felt hat, with its amber glass eyes blazing at the sea, stood the otter. Nobody dared to ask her whether it was the prize.

The five gigs had been brought round overnight and lay on the Hard in their long black-and-yellow hulls like sleeping pike, with six oars each laid inboard along the thwarts and a crew in white jerseys sitting about them eating pasties with the particular calm of men who are going to be sick later. The Trevenna boat was the *Provident*. Wilf Hosken, who had rowed her stroke thirty years before, stood at her stem with his hand on the wood, saying nothing, which for Wilf is a speech.

The band was tuning up on the quay. I stood at the back of the crowd where the wall steps down to the Slip and let it carry me, in my second-best cardigan, with the tool roll in my canvas bag. The Trevenna Silver Band tunes up as other bodies of men go to war: everybody at once, in every key, in the conviction that the others will see sense.

"Mrs Pellow!" Jory the potman was at the Lantern door with a cloth over his shoulder and his hands cupped. "Phone! It's the constable!"

I should say that the Lantern's payphone hangs in an alcove at the end of the bar, between the dartboard and the gents, and that I had not been near it since Aubrey stood there on the thirteenth with his back to the room and said, *Half ten, then.* The floats overhead had their lamps lit, 140 of them, a ceiling of small green moons. Jago's regatta breakfasts were in progress, four crews at the long table and a smell of bacon. Jago himself was at the back with his sleeves rolled, lifting a tray, and when he saw me he put it down, and I knew from the way that he did it that he was not going to be in the room for what came next.

"Dot," said Demelza's voice, very small and a long way off, with a hospital's echo behind it. "I'm at Treliske. I came at seven. Nobody stopped me. I'd the uniform."

"Is he awake?"

"He's been awake since yesterday. He didn't speak till half an hour ago. The sister says he looked at the ceiling for a time and said it, and then said it again, as if to check. Twice." She stopped. I could hear her breathing, and the squeak of a trolley. "'The lady with honey.'"

I held on to the receiver. At my elbow, on the bar, between a bowl of pickled eggs and a dish of dry roast peanuts, someone had set a jar of heather and gorse honey for the lifeboat tin, with a handwritten label in a neat upright hand: HONEY FOR THE LIFEBOAT. M.H. It was the jar Pieter de Vries had looked at from the door on the Thursday night, standing as if undecided, with his collar not yet turned up. I put my hand over the label as one covers a child's ears.

"What else did he say?"

"He asked what day it was. He asked for his glasses, and the sister says he can't have them till the doctor's been." A pause. "It's a man with a bang on the head, Dot. It's one phrase. It's not a name."

"I know."

"I thought you'd want to hear it first."

"First," I said. "Thank you."

When I had put the receiver back I stood for some time with my hand on the jar. I had known it since the kitchen, but knowing is for kitchens. This was a phrase with weather in it, spoken by a man who had floated face-down in the dark, and it came to me not as an idea but as a taste. I did not look round. Behind me in the Lantern someone had begun to sing, and four crews joined in with their mouths full.

* * *

Loveday Penhallow arrived at half past ten in a bath chair.

It was a wicker chair on iron-tyred wheels that had stood in the Court's coach house since her mother's last illness, and Reverend Nance was pushing it down the lane from the church in his bicycle clips, with a rug over the old lady's knees and the face of a man steering a hearse. Every cobble produced from him a small cry. "Forgive me," he said. "Forgive me. I'm so sorry. It is nothing."

"Do not apologise to the cobbles, Arthur," said Loveday. "They were laid in 1740 and will outlast the pair of us."

She wore her good tweed and a hat with a pheasant feather, and she had her blackthorn across her lap like a lance. When the chair drew level with me she lifted one gumbooted foot a quarter of an inch from the footplate and put it down again.

"Pellow. Right feet. Say nothing."

"I wouldn't dream."

"Hm." She looked at me with her whole narrow face, and the hearing aid in her ear gave a thin whistle, though I had not said a word that could be called untrue. "I've come to see whatever it is you're going to do. The aid is on. It will tell me."

"I haven't told you I'm going to do anything."

"You have a face like the Chapel in 1956," said Loveday. "Take me to the tent, Arthur."

The marquee stood at the head of the quay, a great white affair that had been put up at six in a sea mist and was still steaming faintly in the sun like a horse. Inside it was cool, and smelled of damp canvas and urn. The Institute's long tables ran down the middle with their cloths, set for the regatta tea; at the far end, where the canvas gave on to the Slip and the harbour mouth, a smaller table had been dressed in white, with a silver cup, a pen on a saucer, a bowl of orange montbretia from some hedge, and in the centre, laid flat on a folded cloth, with its red label turned to the roof, the Regatta Book.

Morwenna was squaring it to the edge of the table with two fingers of her left hand.

She wore the Institute's green sash over her best blue dress, and her pearl studs, and the silver perm was newly set. She saw the chair before she saw me and came across with her arms out, and the apple cheeks were flushed with pleasure. "Loveday, my dear! Out in the air! Whatever will Dr Tregellas say?" She stooped and kissed the old woman's cheek. "How lovely to see you so well."

The aid screamed. It was not a whistle; it was a shriek that went up the scale and cracked the air like a dropped tray, and half the tent turned to look.

"That wants a new battery, my handsome," said Morwenna comfortably.

"It wants nothing of the kind," said Loveday.

Morwenna laughed, a warm small laugh. She straightened and turned that bright, kind face on me, and I saw her take in the cardigan, and the shadows under my eyes, and the bag over my shoulder, all in a second. "And you look like a wet week. You've not slept. Sit. Eat." A saffron bun came out of the bag on her arm in a paper napkin, and was in my hand before I had formed an opinion. "They've let Jago go, I hear. Edna's sister at the Co-op. I am glad. I never thought it of him, not for a minute." Her left hand went back to the Book, and smoothed the cloth beneath it. "And that poor Dutchman. Is he mending?"

I have thought about what I said next a good many times. I could have said that he was awake. I could have said he had spoken. There was a woman a yard from me whose hands I had known for thirty years, smelling of woodsmoke and wax, and in a moment I should have to say it to her face, and it seemed the least of what I owed her to say it first.

"Still asleep," I said. "They say he may be days."

"Poor man," said Morwenna.

I ate the bun. I will say that. It was a very good bun. I ate it because to refuse it would have been information, and I stood in the tent among the damp canvas and the montbretia with the yellow crumbs on my fingers, eating her cake, with my eyes on the red label on the Book, which did not seem to me to be very much heavier than a ledger. Behind me Reverend Nance had stopped saying sorry. I noticed that. It was the first time in my life I had known him silent.

* * *

Garrow was at the foot of the lifeboat house steps at a quarter to twelve, in the raincoat, in full sun, with the face of a man who has been told he must enjoy himself. Demelza stood a yard behind his left shoulder with the notebook against her chest and her eyes on the middle distance. She had given him the folder, I could see. His hand was resting on it, on top of the raincoat's pocket, as one rests a hand on a dog one doesn't trust.

"Mrs Pellow, dear."

"Inspector. I should like to ask you to be in the tea tent at three."

"And why would that be?"

"Because the constable has given you something to read, and I am going to ask a question in public, and you will want to be somewhere you can hear the answer." I did not say any more. I had an idea that if I said more, the idea would come to pieces in my hands.

He was silent a moment. A gull walked along the rail beside us with an air of conducting an inspection. "I have read Mr Trevithick's list," said Garrow. "I have also been handed a scrap of burned paper which I shall have something to say about on Monday, to more than one person. I do not say the list means anything. I say it is a catalogue of books that a lady was kind enough to repair. I do not say that you are right. If you are wrong, Mrs Pellow, I shall find it hard to be gentle."

"I should not expect it."

"I'll stand at the back," said Garrow.

"That is all I ask."

"It is all I shall do." He took out a handkerchief and mopped his forehead, and for the second time in two days he looked, briefly, very tired.

At noon Jago fired the gun.

He stood on the harbour wall above the Slip in his shirtsleeves with the starter's pistol raised in his right hand, the one with nine fingers, and the whole quay hushed, the band halfway through a bar and the otter staring at the sea. The five gigs lay across the harbour mouth in line abreast, oars up like the legs of a row of beetles, on a young flood that had been making for an hour and twenty minutes. Jago looked at the water, and at the men, and at nothing, and I thought: he has not slept, and he has hung forty yards of flags, and he is doing his job. He lowered his arm and the gun cracked and the gulls went up off every roof in Trevenna like thrown paper.

The race went out past the Point to the buoy and back, a mile each way, and the crowd ran along the quay wall as the boats shrank, and we saw them only as a flicker of oars. They turned at the buoy in a scrum. The *Provident* came out of it second. Tamsin, beside me, had hold of my arm in a grip that would leave bruises, and she did not breathe, and Wilf Hosken on the wall above us shouted advice in a voice I had not heard from him before, a rusted old pilot's-boy voice that cracked at the high notes. Halfway home they went into the lead. A length. Two lengths. Then the band struck up.

It struck up three tunes at once. The cornets began "Trelawny"; the trombones, who had not been told, began "The Floral Dance"; and a single euphonium, somewhere at the back, struck up "Eternal Father" in the manner of a man who has been given the wrong music and is determined to see it through. They met in the middle of the second verse of "Trelawny" as a family meets a quarrel at Christmas, and settled it, and ended on it, and were cheered as if they had meant to.

The Trevenna gig crossed the line a length clear, with the white jerseys heaving and the oars shipped in a single movement as clean as a page turning, and Jago fired the gun a second time, and the whole of the harbour roared. The boats leaned on their moorings. The otter looked on. I had never seen anything so loud come out of so many people who were so seldom permitted to shout.

I turned from the water, for no reason I can name, and looked at the tent.

It stood at the head of the quay with its flap tied back. Reverend Nance had wheeled Loveday's chair to the open side to watch, and at his elbow, in her green sash, a tray of egg sandwiches balanced on one arm, was Morwenna, who was pressing one on the Reverend with her free hand. He said he really couldn't. She said, "Sit. Eat," and he sat on a folded chair, as people do, and ate. And in the moment in which the second gun went off and the crowd rose and every face in Trevenna turned to the water, I looked at the tent, and Morwenna Hext, over the head of the man she was feeding, was already looking back at me.
