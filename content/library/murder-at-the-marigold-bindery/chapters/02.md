# Saffron Cake and Sympathy

"Keys," said Constable Kitto. "How many, and who's got them?"

She had been in my shop eleven minutes. In that time she had looked at Aubrey Trevithick for exactly as long as duty required, shut the press-room door on him with the flat of her hand, and strung blue-and-white tape across the top and bottom of Fish Cellar Lane from a roll in her bicycle bag. Now she stood on the far side of my counter with her helmet upside down beside the till like a bowl put out for the cat. She was short and wide in the shoulder, with a plait pinned so tight beneath the helmet that it had left a groove in her hair, and a notebook with an elastic band round it. She held the band the way another woman might hold a rosary. Quire had hissed at her on the way in, and she had taken it as a formal caution.

"Two," I said. "Mine, which has not left my cardigan since I put it on, and the spare, which I lent to Mr Trevithick at a quarter to six yesterday evening."

"Lent."

"Gave. I'd say I was bullied into it, but one should not speak ill of the dead, and it would be untrue. I gave it to him to be rid of him."

Her pencil stopped. "What did he want it for?"

"A bench, a lamp and a door that locked. He wouldn't say what for. He said he'd something to tell me in the morning, and that he preferred to be accurate."

"About what?"

"About whatever he was being accurate about, Demelza. He was a man who made a speech out of the time of day." I heard how that sounded and moderated it. "I took it to be the price of a trestle. We'd had words about the trestle."

She wrote that down. She writes everything down; I have never seen anyone take such trouble over a sentence she did not quite believe. "Door was locked when you got here?"

"Yes."

"Locked, with him inside it, and the spare not on him, not in the lock, not on the bench."

"I looked at the lock. I did not look in his pockets. I am a bookbinder, not a magpie."

"Right," said Demelza, which she says the way other people say amen.

I did not mention eleven o'clock. I had meant to. There was a gap where I might have, and she was looking at her notebook, and the sentence went past like a bus I had no real wish to catch.

The shop bell went, a tall shape filled the doorway, and the top of it hit the frame.

"Sorry," said Kit, to the frame.

He is my late husband's nephew, thirty-four years old, and the longest, reddest, most apologetic man in west Cornwall: ginger hair standing up on the side he had slept on, a chin gone rusty with a night's stubble, the left thumbnail bitten to the quick. He had run up the lane. He gripped the doorpost like a man on a boat. Quire, on the sill, hissed at him, and he apologised to her too.

"Sorry. Auntie Dot. Elsie Hocking said, and I said she'd got it wrong, sorry." He saw who was behind the counter. "Constable."

"Mr Pellow." Her voice came out as flat as a ruler. "You'll have to stay on the step, please. The press room is closed."

"Is it true? Only he was going to be at the hall at nine to see the stalls go in. I rang the Court, and Miss Penhallow said he hadn't, and I thought, sorry, I thought—"

"Kit," I said.

"Sorry."

"Inspector Garrow is coming from Truro," Demelza said. She had turned a page of the notebook with the briskness of a woman making a bed in a hurry. "He'll want a word. Stay in the lane. Don't go anywhere."

"No. Right. Sorry."

He went out backwards, which with Kit is not a figure of speech, and the bell shook for a long time after. Demelza watched the door and I watched Demelza. As she turned the page her sleeve rode up on the left wrist, and below the regulation navy showed a cuff of grey wool, darned at the thumb-hole in a thread that did not quite match. I knew that darn. I had done it myself in the winter of 1986, on a boy who went through thumbs. I said nothing. It is one of the few advantages of being furniture.

Beyond the window and the tape a girl was sitting on the net-loft steps with her knees drawn up. I knew her by sight and a little by hand: Sheila Bray's daughter, sixteen, whose mother cleans at Penhallow Court, and who had spent most of May at my counter with a school project on dying trades and a pen she chewed in place of a plait. Black hair cut to the jaw, a stripe of it bleached at the front, ink to the second knuckle. She was not watching the constable or the tape or the people beginning to collect at the foot of the lane. She was watching my window and, I thought, me. When I raised a hand she was not there. The third step gave one creak, as if it had been told off, and the steps were empty.

Detective Inspector Garrow came up the lane at twenty past eight in a belted raincoat, although the sun had been up three hours, with the look of a man bound in rexine: easy to wipe, and giving nothing back. He stopped on my step and took in the shop, the tape, the constable and me, in that order.

"And this is?"

"Mrs Pellow, sir," said Demelza. "She found him."

"Ah. The lady of the house."

"Mrs Pellow will do."

"Quite. If the lady of the house would show me through to—"

"The constable has shut the door and I would hate to undo her work. And the kettle's on."

"I shan't trouble you for tea, Mrs Pellow."

"It's no trouble. It's boiling."

I have a gift for waiting; I have been practising since 1962. He waited too, with his hands in the pockets of the raincoat, until the kettle had finished and the pot had stood its three minutes, and I will say for him that he did not once look at his watch. Quire had come off the windowsill to inspect him. She looked him up and down, torn ear cocked, and hissed with a thoroughness I had not heard from her since the vicar's wife came in with a spaniel.

"Does it bite?"

"Only the deserving."

I had not known that a man could be carried out through one's shop under a grey blanket, past the counter and the paste pots and the cat. They were very good about the step. There were two ambulance men from Truro and a doctor with a bag, who said "Mrs Pellow" as if I might be a patient, and on the blanket, over the chest, a hat. In the lane perhaps thirty people who had been ostentatiously on their way elsewhere took off their caps, those who had caps. Elsie Hocking put her apron to her mouth. Quire, on the sill, stood up and put her back into a hoop.

It was into this, at twenty past nine, that Morwenna Hext came up the lane like a small round tug through a regatta.

She had the silver perm, the pearl studs and the large handbag on her arm, and in her other hand, in a checked cloth, a saffron cake. Nobody has ever known Morwenna reach a crisis without one. Trevenna has been got through on her cakes: the lifeboat year, the chapel fire, the winter the harbour wall went, and the morning Ned died, when she was on my step at half past seven with a loaf still hot, and said nothing at all but "Sit. Eat." She walked under Demelza's tape as though it were a washing line, and Demelza, who had been ready to stop the Chief Constable, held it up for her.

"Dot, my handsome." She took my face in both hands, quickly, as if testing a loaf. "Sit. Constable, you've toast on your chin. Inspector, I don't know you, and you may stand where you like so long as it isn't on my cloth."

"Madam—"

"Hext. Women's Institute and the regatta purse. I'll thank you not to lay that coat on the cake." She took the brown pot out of my hands, which I had not known I was holding, tipped out the tea I had made without a word on what she thought of it, and began again.

I will not say it was a relief to sit down. But I sat. The shop was small for five people and a cat, and Kit had been let in to stand against the paper rack, apologising to it, and the saffron was yellow as the door and still warm, and I ate a slice with my eyes on the floor. Morwenna cut and poured and passed. She gave the Inspector the biggest slice and his tea sweet before he had said he took sugar, and he took it, and sat down on my stool, which I had not offered.

Quire had by then hissed at the constable, the inspector and my nephew, in that order, and retired to the top of the paper rack to consider the rest of us. She came down when Morwenna sat. I want that on the record. She came down the front of the rack like water down a slate, crossed the counter, the floor and the whole width of the shop, and climbed into Morwenna's lap and turned round once and settled, like a bun into a tin.

"Well," said Garrow.

"She doesn't do that," said Demelza.

"She doesn't do that for anyone," I said. "She's never done it for me, and I feed her."

"Cats know who's brought cake," said Morwenna comfortably, and put a hand on the black back. Quire shut her eyes. "She was always a sensible animal. How long's she been yours, Dot?"

"She turned up in March of 'eighty-six. The month Ned died. She came in fat as a pudding, and I'd forgotten to feed myself that spring, never mind a cat. Somebody did. I never found out who."

"A cat will always find somebody."

"So I've been told."

Garrow had his notebook out and his tea untouched, and was looking round the room with the particular patience of a man who has decided to be patient. "I shall want elimination prints from everyone who has been in the press room recently. Mrs Pellow, yourself. Mr Pellow."

"Sorry," said Kit. "Most days, sorry. I bring the post through."

"Mrs Hext?"

"Not since Ned died, Inspector, God rest him." She said it to the cat. "It was his room, you see. I never went in without Ned there to ask me, and after he'd gone I hadn't the heart. I hardly come up the lane now. My knees. Dot comes to me, and we meet at the hall, and that's very nearly all the conversation I get."

"Very good," said Garrow, and wrote it down. I watched his pencil. It wrote Morwenna as it had written me, and Kit, and the cat, if he had thought of it: a name and a few words, to be forgotten by lunch.

Garrow went through to the press room again, and Demelza after him, and Kit fled to the lane to be told off by nobody. When they had gone the shop was very quiet. The kettle ticked on its ring. Morwenna went on stroking the cat with her thumb behind the torn ear, where I had never been allowed to put a finger.

"Was it quick, do you think?"

"I don't know. I'm not allowed to think."

"You were always the one for thinking. Ned used to say you'd read a bus timetable for the plot." She cut a second slice and put it on my knee without asking. "Eat."

"He said he had something to tell me this morning," I said. "He came in at a quarter to six, wanting my bench, and I was short with him. He said he'd rather be accurate than prompt. I told him closing was at six. Those were very nearly the last words I ever said to Aubrey Trevithick, Morwenna, about closing."

"That's Aubrey. Never so much as the time of day without a lecture on being right about it." She looked at the slice on my knee until I picked it up. Her hand on the cat did not stop. "He always did choose the worst places to be right."

I thought she meant the Bindery, and the stove, and the floor. I thought she meant church halls and funerals and committees, all the places Aubrey had stood up to correct a date. I thought she meant nothing at all, which is what people say at such times. I did not ask. I ate the saffron cake, which was very good, and I kept the sentence in my head the whole of that day, the way you keep a button from a coat you no longer own, without knowing why.
