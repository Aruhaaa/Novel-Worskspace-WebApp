# Paste, Thread and Wax

Ned's paste is made in a dented enamel pan that is used for nothing else and has a handle mended with a bootlace. Wheat starch, never flour, mixed cold to a cream with a wooden spoon; then boiling water poured in a thin stream, stirring, until the whole turns from milk to glass in the space of a breath. A pinch of alum. And off the heat, from the brown bottle with the cork in the cupboard, one drop of oil of clove, no more, which Ned counted aloud in a whisper like a man at a christening.

Demelza had taken down the tape at half past seven on Saturday morning with a face like a shut door, told me the press room was mine again, and left. The aluminium powder still lay grey along the stove door. I found I could not lay a hand on that stove. So I made the paste on the gas ring in the shop, in the small pan, as I had the winter of 'seventy-nine when the flue fell in, and I had Kit stir, because his arm is longer than mine and he needed something to do with it.

He had come when I rang, at eight, in yesterday's shirt, unshaven, and stood in the press-room door a full minute before he would come in. He looked at the boards where Aubrey had been. Somebody, and I think it was Demelza, had put a sheet of newspaper over the patch and weighted its corners with four bone folders from Ned's tin. Kit stepped round it the way you step round a sleeping dog.

"Sorry. Is it, I mean, are you sure about doing this today?"

"Paste goes off. There is a fair on. I have forty hinges promised, Kit, and a man from Bristol who wants a Surtees back by Sunday." I unlocked the cupboard and took out the brown bottle, which stands on the second shelf behind the alum, beside Ned's green notebook with its rubber band. "Stir. Slowly. As though it had done you a kindness."

The starch cleared. I held the bottle over the pan, counted one in a whisper, and the smell came up out of the steam like a door opened on a lit room: oranges, nutmeg, string, the week before Christmas.

I stood very still with the cork in my fingers.

It was the same. I do not mean similar. I had put my nose to the hinge of the Carne Rutter at ten o'clock on Friday, with a policeman's back turned to me, and what I smelled now was that exactly, down to the faint sharp edge underneath it, the spike like a pin in a cushion that clove oil gives when it has been in the cupboard a month or two. Whoever had pasted that board had used this bottle's cousin. They had used Ned's recipe, in Ned's quantities, and counted, I would have said, aloud.

"That's Uncle Ned's," said Kit, working the spoon. "That's just, sorry, that's what it smells like in here."

"It is. Who else makes it?"

"You. Me." He stopped stirring. "Sorry. Nobody else, Auntie Dot. It's not written anywhere. You'd have to have stood where I'm standing."

"Yes," I said. "You would."

We let it cool. I told him the rest, because somebody had to hear it who knew what a signature was: that the middle of the Carne was a farmer's accounts on Dutch-looking paper, and that the cover and the ends were genuine. I did not tell him about the pin-prick. I had not yet found a way to say that to a living person. Kit listened with his head down, and his bitten thumb went up to his mouth and was removed again, and a good deal of colour went out of his freckles.

"Garrow thinks it was Mr Trevithick," I said. "That he was the forger, and fell out with a partner."

"Sorry. No."

"No?"

"He wouldn't have." He said it to the pan. "He was, sorry. He was very particular."

"Yes, Kit, I know." I looked at him, but he was looking at the paste, and I put the matter away on the shelf with the other things I did not understand that week.

When it was cool I carried the pan through to the press room and set it on the bench, and ran my hand along the cutting board for the bone folder I wanted. The board is a slab of beech the size of a tea tray, and thirty years of knives have scarred it into a country of rivers and roads, with Ned's long diagonal cut across the middle like a border. In one of the cuts, down by the edge of the lying press, something brown caught my thumbnail.

It was a tail of thread, three inches, wedged in the groove where the policeman's tweezers had missed it. Kit found me my glasses, which had slid under the bench. I took the loupe from my pocket and laid the thread across my palm in the light from the skylight.

The thread was sound, linen, twisted three-ply, the colour of a toasted crust. I had expected tallow and paraffin, which is what I use, and what Ned used, and what leaves a thread slightly grey and stiff and smelling faintly of a stable. This was golden. There was a bloom on it as fine as the bloom on a plum, and when I rubbed it between my finger and thumb it warmed, and gave off an odour, thin and sweet and entirely out of place in a room that smelled of a dead man and a policeman: honey.

"Beeswax," I said.

"Sorry?"

"The thread's been waxed with beeswax. I've never used it in my life." I held it to his nose. "Nor did Ned. He called it a wax for ladies who sew their own buttons. It's a hobbyist's choice; it's kinder to the thread and it costs three times as much."

"I use yours."

"I know you do."

He sniffed the thread and took his head away, and I put the thing in an envelope from the shop drawer and sealed it with a bit of Ned's paste, for reasons I cannot defend. Then I took a breath. I told him about the hammer, because it had been in my head since the doorway on Friday and I had reached the point of needing to say it aloud to anyone who would not write it down.

"It was on Ned's nail. Face-out. I've not hung it there since the funeral." I nodded at the wall, where both nails stood bare, the police having taken the hammer away in a paper sack with a label on it, and I had not asked what the label said. "Ned was left-handed, you'll remember, and he hung it to suit himself, round side to the room and the handle to his left. I could find it in the dark on my nail. Whoever was in this room put it on his, in his way. It isn't a thing you do by accident. You do it if you have watched a man reach for it ten thousand times."

"Sorry." Kit's voice had gone a long way off. "Who'd have watched Uncle Ned reach for it ten thousand times?"

"You. Me." I counted on my fingers, as I had under the table at the Lantern. "Jago, I suppose, in the early days, before the boat took up his time. Nobody else ever worked at that bench beside him. He was a private man, Ned, about his work."

"Only me and you and Jago."

"I did not say—"

"Only us three." He put the bone folder down, rather he did not put it down, he let it go; it fell with a small clean sound on the boards and lay there between his shoes. He looked at it as though it belonged to a stranger. What colour he had went out of his face and left the freckles standing on it like type on a page. "Sorry. That's, sorry. That's all of us, then."

"Kit."

"No, sorry, I only. Sorry."

"Kit, listen to me. You turn your kettle to the right. I have unpicked enough of your head and tail stitches to know it blindfold. Ned gave up on you at eleven and said the boy had hands as God made them." I made myself say it lightly. "I am only saying who might have stood beside him. I am not saying who."

He nodded, three or four times, and bent for the bone folder, and missed it, and bent again. I took an end of the thread out of the envelope and tried not to look at his bitten thumb.

"I can see your shoes under the door," I said, rather loudly, to the shop.

There was a silence in which somebody inhaled. Then the door to the street, which stood ajar, moved a few inches, and the girl came out from behind it as if she had been slid on a tray: Sheila Bray's daughter, with her jaw-length hair and the bleached stripe, ink to the second knuckle, a pen in the corner of her mouth.

"Right," she said. "So."

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Not long." That was certainly untrue. She looked past me at the press room, at the newspaper on the boards and the four bone folders, with an expression I could not read, not curiosity quite, and not the hunger of the village. She looked at it like someone seeing a church. "I could sweep."

"No," I said.

"I'm quick. I don't touch anything. I'd do the yard and the front step and not the bench."

"No, thank you. I've no money to pay anyone and I've enough on my conscience without the Education Authority."

She stood her ground. She is a thin small person in a big jumper, and she did not argue; she merely went on standing there, with her hands behind her and the pen in the corner of her mouth, looking at the Bindery as if it were the last warm room in the county. I thought of the Fish Cellar Lane steps, and the third that creaks, and the girl on them the morning before.

"The yard," I said. "And the step. And I'll not be paying you."

"Right. So." She vanished, and a minute later I heard the broom begin in the yard, a brisk skilled whisper, the sound of a girl who had swept before and liked the work.

I went back to the cupboard for the alum, and it was Ned's notebook on the second shelf that I looked at. A small green cloth book with a rubber band round it, much handled, which had lived in that cupboard since before I was married. Every job he ever did was in it, and every boy who ever held a needle for him, in his own tiny horrid hand. I had lent it to Sheila Bray's girl for an afternoon in May, for a school project on dying trades, and she had brought it back in a Co-op bag with a thank-you note in a far better hand than mine, and I had locked it away again without opening the cover. I cannot read Ned's writing. It is like being shouted at by a man in a gale.

"Kit," I said. "Ned kept every pupil he ever had written down. In that book. Every job and every name, with the dates." I took the alum from the shelf. "If someone learned his way of sewing, he'd have put them in it. It might be that there's a name."

"Have you read it?"

"I cannot bear his hand. I've never read it. It's been in that cupboard for—"

"Dot, my handsome! Are you in?"

It came from the shop, bright and unmistakable, and the bell over the door, which jangles when the door is pushed open, jangled a moment after her voice. Morwenna was standing at the counter with a tin open in her hands and her large bag over her arm and the face of a woman who has arrived that very instant. In the tin lay saffron buns, a dozen, yellow as the door.

"I heard they'd given you your room back, and I thought, she'll not have eaten. No cake this morning, the oven let me down, so it's buns. Kit, you look dreadful. Sit down before you fall."

"Morwenna, you're a saint."

"I'm a woman with a tin. Eat."

We ate. She stayed ten minutes, and talked about the bunting committee and whether the lifeboat would get a new tractor, and admired the paste, and said she wouldn't set foot in the press room, if I didn't mind, not today; and then she took her tin and her bag and went down the lane without looking back, as she always does, a small round person moving through the morning like a well-run errand.

When she had gone I took Ned's notebook out of the cupboard, and weighed it in my hand, and put it back. I turned the little brass key. It was Ned's key, a flat thing with a bow like a clover, that had hung on the third hook of the board in the shop between the yard bolt and the loft padlock since his day, and I had turned it in that lock out of habit and never out of fear. I looked at it on my palm. I threaded it on the bootlace that holds my glasses, and put the lace over my head, and felt it settle against my breastbone, cold, with a small weight like a coin.

I felt a fool, doing it, and not nearly enough of one.
