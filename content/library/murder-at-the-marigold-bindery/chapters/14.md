# Question Time

The quiz at the Drowned Lantern began at eight, which is to say at twenty past, because the tide at Trevenna is the only thing that has ever been on time and nobody sees why the pub should compete. Low water was at twenty-five past nine. Through the open door the harbour smelled of mud and weed and old rope, and the gulls stood about on the Hard in the last of the light with the air of people waiting for the doors of a theatre.

Jago had put out forty packets of crisps for the teams, cheese and onion, salt and vinegar, ready salted, in drifts down the middle of the tables, so that the three leading sides faced one another over a sea of them like fleets. Quire Straits had the table by the dartboard: myself, Kit, who held the pencil because his handwriting looked like a catalogue and he had never been argued with about it, Tamsin Bray, who had been issued a lemonade by a landlord who chose not to ask her age, and the Reverend Nance, with his single sherry and his bicycle clips still on. The Jam Tarts had the long table under the window, five Institute women in cardigans arranged round Morwenna like petals. The Lantern Regulars held the bar end, led by Wilf Hosken, who was seventy-one and had been a pilot's boy before the war, and Eddy Tonkin of the ferrets, who looked as innocent as a man can who is sitting on a book.

Demelza had come as a spectator. She sat alone at the next table with a half of shandy and her notebook shut in front of her, in the grey jumper under a cardigan, and she looked at nothing so firmly that it was plain she was looking at Kit.

Jago climbed on to his stool behind the bar with the cards in one great hand and his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead like a second pair of eyes. He rang the lifeboat bell over the till. The room went quiet by degrees, as a shingle beach does when the wave pulls out.

"Round one," he said. "General knowledge. Question one. Which saint is patron of Cornwall?"

"Piran," breathed Nance, "although there are those who would argue for Petroc, and of course Michael has a claim on the, forgive me. Piran."

Kit wrote it down. Question two was the name of the Tottenham midfielder known as Gazza, and Nance looked at the ceiling and said he supposed it was a Sicilian martyr.

"Gascoigne," said Tamsin, not moving her lips. "Paul."

"Is that how it's spelled?" said Nance, with real interest.

By question six he had given us a saint who was boiled, one who was roasted and one who was merely sat upon, and Tamsin had given us a goalkeeper, a Grand National winner and the Prime Minister's maiden name, and I realised that I was watching an alliance form. They did not look at one another. They passed the pencil under the table with the delicacy of two spies at a station. When Tamsin noticed, from the corner of her eye, that Eddy Tonkin's right hand had been below the level of the table for some minutes, she kicked the Reverend's ankle once.

"Forgive me," said Nance, rising, pink to the collar. "I am sure it is nothing. But I believe Mr Tonkin is reading under the table."

It was Pears' Cyclopaedia, the 1962 edition. Jago confiscated it in his enormous hand with the expression of a man removing a fish from a child, and deducted five points, and the Regulars arose in a body to say that a vicar had no business knowing what a cyclopaedia looked like from underneath.

"Round two," said Jago, when the shouting was done. "Books."

He stopped. He took the glasses down off his forehead, which I had never seen him do in public, and put them on his nose. From the inside of his shirt he drew a long brown envelope, a good deal handled, with writing on the front.

"Mr Trevithick gave me this a fortnight ago," he said. "It says, *Jago, for Thursday the twentieth, in order.* I haven't opened it before now. I'm going to read it as it's written, and anyone who wants to say a word about that can say it to me after."

Nobody said anything. At the end of the bar, by herself, Imogen Mayne sat with her hands round a glass and her face turned very slightly away.

I will not pretend it was a great round. It was what he would have set: elegant, a little vain and fastidiously exact. *Who wrote* Persuasion, *and in what year? Both, please. The title page says 1818 and the book appeared in December 1817; any team that gives the former will receive a mark for pedantry and none for the answer.* The room laughed at that. It laughed again at the third, which asked for the oldest printed book in the county and added, in Aubrey's voice through Jago's mouth, that he knew perfectly well it was in his own possession and that this was why he had not asked for it to be brought. I watched the laugh go round the room as one watches a wave go down a harbour wall, and I noted three people who took it late: Nance, who had missed the joke; Jago, who laughed at the end of his own sentence as though somebody had reminded him; and Morwenna, a full beat after the Jam Tarts, a beat in which she looked at the table and then smiled. It meant nothing. A woman at a quiz is generally thinking about something else.

They marked the sheets across the tables, as they always did, so that nobody should mark their own. Quire Straits had the Jam Tarts' sheet, and Tamsin took the pencil and ran it down the right-hand column like a customs officer.

Morwenna's hand was a pleasure to look at: quick, upright, small, every letter standing on the line with its feet together. At the foot of each round she had written the time, *8.40*, *9.12*, because Miss Hocking had decreed it, after a quarrel with the Regulars in 1987, to prevent disputes about who had been given longer.

"Question seven," said Jago, reading from Aubrey's second sheet. "What does a binder call a folded sheet, or a group of sheets, forming one section of a book? Gathering. Also signature. Also quire. All three are acceptable, and I will not hear a bookbinder insulted by a pedant." He coughed. "Underneath he's put, *Accept 'quire' from one team in particular, who have been waiting three years to be right.*"

The room turned and looked at us. Nance went the colour of a boiled ham. I would have liked to say something, and found that I could only smile at the table, which I did very hard.

"It says *signature*," said Tamsin. She was holding up the Jam Tarts' answer to the light. "Here. That's not what's on the card."

"It is, my handsome," said Morwenna, across the aisle. "Signature. It's the proper word."

"It says gathering."

"It says gathering, signature or quire," said Jago, "and I've just read it out."

"A signature's the mark at the foot of the sheet," said Tamsin, with the stubborn gleam of the sixteen-year-old who has read the whole of one book thoroughly. "It's not the sheet itself."

"It was the sheet itself in 1780," said Morwenna comfortably. "And it's the sheet itself in the trade, today. And it's also the mark. Both. I'd be surprised if you'd been told otherwise."

They looked at each other across the aisle, the girl with the bleached stripe and the woman with the silver perm, and each, I could see, was perfectly certain, and each was right. Jago allowed the point. Tamsin gave Morwenna her mark with the expression of someone surrendering a fort and keeping the flag.

"Good girl," said Morwenna, and winked.

Round two ended with the whole of Jago's voice gone to gravel. The last note on the sheet was not an answer. He read it all the same, very slowly, as though each word were a plank he had to test before he put his weight on it. *Jago: please read the answers slowly. People are generally cleverer than they look, and they like to be given time to find out.*

Then he took the glasses off and laid them down, and there was no sound in the room at all. Wilf Hosken, who has stood for nothing since VE Day, put his hands on the table and pushed himself up. Chairs scraped. It went round the room like a wave in the other direction, the Seconds and the Regulars and the Jam Tarts one by one, and Nance with his sherry, and Tamsin, who did not look as though she knew why, and Kit, and I. Demelza stood. At the end of the bar Imogen did not move, and I saw Morwenna cross the floor and lay a hand upon her shoulder, and stay.

Sheets were exchanged for the other rounds, which I scarcely remember, except that the Regulars marked ours and passed it round the table before it went back, and that I saw Wilf's lips moving as he came to the margin. I had not looked at the margin myself. Kit, who had meant to write the answers neatly and had then lost his place every time a grey cardigan moved at the next table, had begun by answering one question and finished by addressing another.

"Question nineteen," read Wilf, with enormous gravity. "*Which planet is nearest the sun? Mercury. Demelza, you have been at the next table for two hours with my jumper on, and I should say that you are not allowed to read this, because my handwriting has always looked like a ransom note. Question twenty: I am sorry about March last year. I know you didn't want me to say it, so I haven't said it, and it's in the margin, which is not saying.*"

You could have heard a crisp drop. Demelza was pink to the ears. She rose, crossed the three feet of floor, took the sheet from Wilf Hosken, read it right through to the end without moving her face, folded it in four, and put it in the breast pocket of her cardigan, where the police keep evidence.

"Is that a tick or a cross?" said Wilf.

"It's a matter for the Crown Prosecution Service," said Demelza, and sat down, and did not look at Kit again, or only twice.

The music round was Jago's own. He fed coins to the Wurlitzer, whose cracked amber dome looks as though it had been through the war, and gave us ten seconds of ten records, and the Jam Tarts took the first six like a scythe. Morwenna did not confer. She heard four bars, wrote, passed the pencil, like a woman shelling peas. Then Jago reached the newer records, and the Institute went down in a heap. The seventh was a young man declaiming over a drum machine, and Mrs Chegwidden wrote *Dance Band*, and Miss Hocking wrote *Gentleman's lament*, and Gwen Trewhella, who was deaf and knew all the tunes by the beat through the floor, was overruled four times in a row. At ten to ten Jago pulled the plug at the wall. Eddy Tonkin said it was robbery.

* * *

At twenty-five past ten the three sides were level on seventy-one, and Jago had a tiebreak card in his hand that was not Aubrey's, because Aubrey had not written one. "Closest answer wins," he said. "This is mine."

The door opened as he began to read. It let in the smell of the flooding tide and a man in a pale linen jacket, who stood on the mat as if he had been told to wait in a room and had come to suspect that the room was the difficulty. Pieter de Vries had the round tortoiseshell glasses and the clipped grey beard, and he looked the Lantern over, floats and faces and the cold sea of crisps, and did not seem to see any of it. On the bar by the pickled eggs stood a one-pound jar of Morwenna's heather and gorse honey with the lifeboat tin beside it, and his eyes went there and stayed, with an expression I cannot describe except to say that I have seen it on people looking at a witness.

"Tiebreak," said Jago, when he had finished staring at the Dutchman. "Name the Trevenna lifeboat lost in 1910."

Nance began: "There was one, I am almost certain, forgive me, I believe it was..." and trailed off. The Jam Tarts put their heads together and came up shrugging. "My grandfather's," said Wilf Hosken, "and he never said." Nobody wrote anything. Jago turned the card over, as if the answer might be on the back, and it was not.

"Then nobody leaves till somebody knows," he said. "Jory, bolt the door. It's a lock-in."

A cheer went up. Pieter de Vries, behind the bolt, moved to a stool at the far end of the bar and sat on it with his hands folded. Above us the hundred and forty glass floats burned in their nets, a drowned lantern for every drowned man, and not one of them would give us the name.
