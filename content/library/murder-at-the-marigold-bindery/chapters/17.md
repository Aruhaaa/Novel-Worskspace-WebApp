# Wet Boots

At ten past five on Friday morning I sat up in bed and said "Loveday" aloud, to Quire, who had spent the night on the foot of the bed for the first time since Ned died and did not consider the remark addressed to her.

I had not slept, exactly. I had lain with my eyes shut while the evening went past me in the wrong order, the sheet and the cake and the splash and the weed lines on the steps, and somewhere towards four the line from the leaf had come and stood beside another thing I knew, as two people who have not met come to stand together at a funeral. *From Cape Cornwall to the Longships, two leagues and a half, keeping the Brisons well open to the west.* Loveday had said it in her library, with the buckets under every third shelf, in her bark, with her eyes shut, and I had thought it a beautiful sentence and put it away.

A pilot heads his pages. A man who copies out directions for two leagues of bad water takes several leaves to do it, and he puts the stretch he is writing at the top of each, so that by a lantern in a gale, with his thumb on the line, he can find his place. The first two leaves of the Carne Rutter were in a plastic bag in Garrow's keeping, wearing their Cape Cornwall like a hat. This was the third.

Kit's list had given sixty-four leaves; I had read the line in the church hall. Four were in the false book. That left sixty gone to wherever it was they had gone, and one of them had come out of a Dutchman's paperback, out of a Dutchman's pocket, out of the harbour. I took it from under the Concordance and held it to the grey window, and it was what it had been at midnight. It lay on my palm and weighed nothing and had been going about the world for three hundred and fifty-eight years. I distrusted how easily the arithmetic went into a sentence. A Dutchman had held a real leaf. That was all I knew, and it is a very different thing from knowing why.

Kit was on my step at half past seven with his hands hanging, and "Sorry" was out of him before I had the door open.

"They've kept Jago."

"Who has?"

"Garrow. He's in the cell at Demelza's. On suspicion of the attack on the Dutchman, they're saying. Sorry. It's all over the Co-op." He swallowed. "The Dutchman's at Treliske. They say he'll live. They say it the way they say it about March."

"Jago pulled him out of the water."

"That's what everybody's said. Mrs Bolitho says it's the same thing seen from the other side."

I gave him tea, and he drank it standing, holding the cup in both hands, and looked at the clock three times. I left him in the shop with Tamsin in the yard and told her that if he said "Sorry" more than twice an hour she was to put a sheet of Davey board over his head.

The police station at Trevenna is the front room of the police house at the bottom of Church Lane, between the chapel and the Co-op, and it has the dignity of a bus shelter. There is a counter. There is a wall clock whose tick can be heard from the lane. There is a filing cabinet with its bottom drawer open for ever, a plastic ficus dusted quite recently with a certain loneliness, and a photograph of the Queen hung well away from the damp. At the back a door stood open the width of a hand on the cell, a whitewashed cupboard which had last held a drunk in 1984 and a pig in 1971. Through the gap I saw a bunk and a large grey back turned to the wall. A pair of sea-boots stood on end against the radiator pipe with a newspaper stuffed in each, to draw the water. He did not turn. I think he knew my step.

Demelza sat behind the counter in yesterday's uniform with a mug in both hands and an expression like wet slate. She had been up for twenty-six hours. I had counted.

"Facts," she said, without any hope.

I took the leaf out of my cardigan, in its blotting paper, and laid it on the counter between the ficus and the stapler. "I have something. I found it this morning. On the quay steps, caught in the weed, folded up, just about where he went in. I thought it might have come out of his pocket."

I had worked the lie out in the night, and it was a good one in the sense that every word of it was true but one. It had a hole in it you could have driven the ambulance through. I had worked out, too, what the truth would do to her. A leaf taken from the pocket of a man who might die, before the Inspector came, with the constable in charge of the scene on her knees not four feet off: Garrow would have the badge off her by Monday, and I should have put it in his hand.

Demelza looked at me for as long as it takes a kettle to make up its mind. Then she looked away, at the Queen, and said, "Right. Found on the quay steps. Time?"

"About seven."

She wrote it in the notebook with the elastic band. It was the worst thing she could have done. She believed me at once. She had been up all night and I was the nearest thing she had to a friend in the parish, and she set down my lie in her careful hand because she trusted me, and I stood on the other side of the counter and saw exactly what it was going to cost, and paid it anyway.

"Don't touch it," she said, and then, since I plainly had, "Never mind. What is it?"

"I think it's the Carne. I want to look at the first leaf."

"Garrow left the Carne in the safe." She said it slowly. "For the man from the county record office, Thursday week. He said old paper was in the wrong place wherever it was."

She took a key on a chain from inside her tunic and went down on one knee to a green safe in the corner behind the ficus, and when she stood up she had a flat evidence bag in her arms and a face that had decided something. She set it under the swan-neck lamp on the desk. Through the plastic I could see the rutter lying open, the cover back, the first leaf on top. I laid mine on the blotter beside it, took the loupe out of my cardigan, and said aloud what I found, because there are things one must say in order to believe them.

"The chain lines are the same distance apart. A quarter of an inch wider than the day-book's, you can see it, there and there. The same rag, with the same flecks in it, like pepper. The same ink, brown, gone to tea in the thin strokes, with a bite in the downstroke where the quill dug in. The same hand." I moved the loupe. "Look at the long *s*, how it leans, like a man walking into a wind. And that is the day-book." I lowered the glass to the middle of the bag. "There's the lion in his fence. This leaf has no lion. It's not that. It's the book."

Demelza looked from one to the other. She said nothing for some time, and the ficus listened. Then she said, quite softly, "If I put this in the book, it's the Dutchman's."

"Yes."

"Garrow gets it, and he writes the whole case round Pieter de Vries and stops looking. I don't say he's wrong. I say I'd like another day."

"You can't do that."

"I'm not doing anything. I'm having a cup of tea with a member of the public who has brought in an old bit of paper, and I'm giving it back to her." She slid it across the counter. "Not the Bindery. He'll be through it by tomorrow. And not your cardigan."

"It's where I keep most things."

"I'd noticed," said Demelza, and for a second the corner of her mouth moved.

The door opened at five to twelve without a knock, and the weather changed.

Garrow had not slept either. His raincoat looked as though it had been slept in by a larger man. He had Pascoe, his detective constable, behind him like a hat-stand, and he took in the room, the counter, the ficus, Demelza standing rather suddenly, and me, who had just put a folded square of blotting paper into my cardigan with the air of a woman posting a letter.

"Mrs Pellow, dear. It's as well you're here. You'll have it from the Co-op by dinner, and I'd like it said correctly."

I sat on the one chair that was not a Windsor, and he stood before the Queen with his hands behind him.

"A Rotterdam dealer comes to Cornwall in June," said Garrow. "He's known to the Dutch police, I learned this morning, in a small way, for the tax. He spends a fortnight in a village that has a fine manuscript. He buys nothing at the fair. And when a false book turns up, who tells me, unprompted, that the paper in it is probably Dutch?"

"He was being helpful."

"People who volunteer are people who are afraid you'll find it out yourself. Mr de Vries then has a trunk strapped on the quay at eleven at night and a berth on Wilf Hosken's boat, the *Margery Jane*, for five in the morning, paid for in cash, after I told him in so many words to stay. That's a man who made the false book, or paid for it, and was leaving, and quarrelled with his partner on the way out."

"What partner?"

"Rowe," said Garrow. "Or your nephew."

"Kit?"

"He catalogued the book, Mrs Pellow, dear. He knows what it should look like and what it shouldn't. He has no alibi for Saturday night but a darts match nobody remembers. His uncle taught him to sew. Mr Rowe was Ned Pellow's closest friend, he has keys to half the village, he held no love for the dead man's hold on his lease, and he was soaked to the chest at ten to eleven last night on a quay he won't say why he was on. I have charged men on less."

"Jago pulled him out."

"A man who pushes another into a harbour and then pulls him out has excellent grounds for being wet. We see it in drunks. A change of heart; a witness on the Hard." He glanced at the gap in the cell door and the sea-boots stuffed with newspaper. "Wet boots, Mrs Pellow. Oldest evidence in the book."

"And the cut behind his ear?"

"The boat."

"Or," said Demelza.

"Or," said Garrow, and nodded as though she had said something perfectly sensible about the weather. "I'm for Truro. The Dutchman's hotel room, his bank, and the magistrates, who sit at two. When I've done I shall have something to say to somebody, and I'd be grateful, Constable, if you'd keep Mr Rowe comfortable. Mrs Pellow. Dear."

He went. Pascoe went after him, nodding to the ficus on the way out as though it were senior.

The worst of it was that it was good. It had a Dutchman in it, and Dutch paper, and a trunk, and a berth, and a cut head, and wet boots, and a landlord who would not speak. It had two men for the part of the partner. It lacked nothing but the truth, which is a thing inspectors seldom miss. And in the pocket of my cardigan lay the one piece of evidence that would have made it perfect, the single leaf that turned Pieter de Vries from a man who knew about a forgery into a man who held the proceeds. I was sitting on it. I did not believe the theory. I had no better. It is a poor place to stand.

The telephone on the counter rang while Demelza was still looking at the door.

"Trevenna police," she said. And then, with a change in her back: "Miss Penhallow."

Even where I sat I could hear it: the faint bark of a woman who has never in her life trusted a telephone to carry her voice, and who sends it to the end of the wire at full strength. "No," said Demelza. "He's helping us with our enquiries." The bark rose. "I see. Since when?" She wrote something down and underlined it. "Your man of business. Yes. Mr Rowe keeps the estate's books. And on Thursday night he was on his way to you. At eleven. With..." She stopped. "The quarter's rents. Across the quay, in the dark." She pulled the receiver an inch from her ear. "No, I'm not suggesting anything, Miss Penhallow. I'm asking. Accounts are best done at night, yes. Because nobody interrupts. I'll write that down."

Jago Rowe could not, to the certain knowledge of the village, add up a bar bill without using his fingers, of which he had nine. Loveday had been telling people for some years that he kept her books, and the village had received it as it receives a bishop's remarks about his housekeeper. It had never believed a word.

From the earpiece, tiny and furious, a high thin whistle began, like a kettle coming to the boil: the hearing aid, a mile of wire away and at full cry. Demelza held the receiver at arm's length and let it sing. Then she covered the mouthpiece with her palm and looked at me across the counter, and I had never seen so reasonable a person look so uncertain.

"Is she lying," said Demelza, "or is she in love?"
