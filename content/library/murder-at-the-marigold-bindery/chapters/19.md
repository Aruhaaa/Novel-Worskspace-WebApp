# Nothing Left on the Bench

It turned out that the good tin was the one with the fish on the label. I learned this by opening both and setting them on the step side by side, like a woman laying two hats on a bed, and letting Quire choose. She sniffed the tin with the cat in a basket on it, which I had bought, and gave me a look I shall carry to my grave. Then she ate the pilchards with her back to me, steadily, as though the afternoon had been arranged for her convenience.

It was a quarter past six. The constable with the face like a shut gate had allowed me to fetch the tins from under the shop counter on condition that he looked in my bag, and he had looked in it with an interest that was not unkind. "Cat food, Mrs Pellow," he said. I said yes. He wrote it down.

Garrow's Rover came up the lane at half past, in second gear, and he got out of it like a man who has spent three hours in the company of the county's idea of a sandwich. He had left Kit in Truro, he said, with people who were very good at it. He did not say at what, and I did not ask.

"We shall want a second sweep, Mrs Pellow, dear. The gentleman from Scenes of Crime will be here at eight tomorrow, and he doesn't care to find things moved."

"I haven't moved anything."

"No," said Garrow. "And you won't."

Pascoe had a roll of white gummed tape, a rubber stamp, and the face of a boy given the best job at the school fête. He laid a strip across the join of my yellow door and its frame at the height of my hip and smoothed it with the side of his thumb, as I should have smoothed a hinge. He did it well. I told him so, which I regretted, because he went pink and said thank you, ma'am. Then he did the sash window, and then he went round the back and did the yard door, and I heard him whistling through his teeth among the bins.

"I have a pan of paste on the gas ring," I said.

"It was turned off at ten to five."

"Then it is rubber by now."

"I'm sorry for it," said Garrow, and the dreadful thing is that I think he was.

"I should like my bone folder."

"I'm afraid everything on the premises is an exhibit."

"It is a piece of cow, Inspector. I have had it thirty years."

"Then it will have a very full history," said Garrow, "and be returned to you with a label."

"There is the Chapel's pulpit Bible on the bench. It has come away at Leviticus."

"It will be perfectly safe."

"It is a Methodist Bible, Inspector. It was safer with less company."

He pretended not to hear. He touched the brim of his hat to me, which no policeman had ever done, and I had the impression that somebody had told him to. The Rover reversed down the lane with a great deal of noise, and a young constable I did not know was left on an upturned fish crate beside Mrs Bolitho's geraniums, with the look of a boy waiting for a bus that has been abolished.

The seal was a narrow strip of paper with a red stamp. Any child could have peeled it off in a minute, and it kept nothing in and nothing out. But it said that someone had noticed, as a plaster says so on a graze, and the door beneath it, which is Marigold yellow for Ned's mother's favourite flower, looked like something that had been hurt. Since I came to Trevenna in 1962 there had not been a bench within twenty yards of my bed that I was forbidden to stand at. I stood at the foot of the step for some time, to see how it felt, and it felt like standing at the foot of a step.

* * *

I had a trestle in St Petroc's hall and a canvas tool roll under it, left behind on Sunday when the fair broke up, and nobody had told me I might not fetch them. I went because it was somewhere to go with my hands empty.

The fair had ended on Sunday, officially. Three dealers had stayed on all week in the hall, because the chairman had held the float and the chairman's cash box was in the vestry under his daughter's eye and a bank manager's, and nobody could be paid until the one and the other agreed. That afternoon they had agreed. When I came in at seven the last stalls were coming down. A man who sold sea charts was rolling his stock into tubes. A woman with cookery books and lace bobbins was folding a banner into smaller and smaller squares. At the far end, where Pieter de Vries had kept his maps, a dust sheet hung over the trestles with a card pinned to it in his firm square hand, BACK SHORTLY, and nobody had had the heart to take it down.

The Institute was there as well, having come to borrow the long tables for the regatta tea. The hall did what a roomful of people does when the woman they have been discussing walks in: it became extremely busy. Doris Rosevear carried a chair to the far wall, where there were already eleven. Edna Chegwidden measured a trestle with a tape, twice. Miss Hocking counted teaspoons aloud, in tens, with the fixed attention of a woman defusing something.

Only Gwen Trewhella, who is deaf, looked up with her whole face and said at the pitch of a woman hailing a boat across a bay, "DOT! IS IT TRUE THEY'VE TOOK YOUR KIT FOR IT?"

A teaspoon fell. Miss Hocking lost her place and began again from nought.

"Ned's nephew," I said.

"WHAT?"

"Yes, Gwen."

"WELL, I NEVER. AND HIM SUCH A POLITE BOY."

It was the kindest thing anyone said to me that day, and it made matters worse. Doris steered her off by the elbow towards the urn, and over her shoulder, not quite to me and not quite to the urn, said that Morwenna had been asking after me, and was thinking of me, and would have come up with something hot only for her knees. I said that was kind of her. It was kind. I turned it over later, with the rest.

My trestle stood where I had left it by the tea urn. Somebody had wiped it down. The roll lay underneath, tied the way I tie it, and I knelt and went over it by touch, as you go through a child's pockets: two folders, the dividers, the awl, a lump of tallow wax in a twist of paper, the needle book, the little brass gauge with the worn thumbwheel. Nothing the police wanted, because the police had not known it was there. It was not much. I held it against my chest like a woman on a station platform.

Reverend Nance was in the porch with an armful of hymn books, bicycle clips on, a cough drop travelling from one cheek to the other. He stood aside to let me out. "Forgive me, Mrs Pellow," he said, "if there is anything..." His arms were so full that he could not offer me his hands, and the sentence had nowhere to go, so he shut his mouth on the cough drop. I said thank you, Mr Nance. I think we were both relieved.

* * *

Demelza was waiting at the foot of Fish Cellar Lane under the one lamp that works, in uniform, with her helmet under her arm, like a woman who has walked as far as the gate and found she cannot go through. The grey cuff of Kit's jumper showed at her wrist.

"He's had his tea," she said, in case I was going to ask. "Sausage and chips from the canteen. He ate the chips."

"And the sausage?"

"Said he'd leave it for the next man." She made a noise through her nose that was not a laugh. "They've given him a blanket, Dot. It's grey. It's got a stripe."

I said nothing, which was the right thing, and we stood there.

"I've been spoken to," she said. "Garrow did it in the corridor at Truro with a sergeant going past for the effect. 'Constable Kitto's judgement in this matter has been compromised by an undeclared personal association.' Fourteen months, and he calls it an association. It goes on my file on Monday."

"Everyone in Trevenna knows about you and Kit. The gulls know."

"The gulls aren't Truro." She turned the helmet round in her hands. "I'm to mind the police house. I'm to mind Jago Rowe in the front room. I'm to keep out of the Bindery and away from civilians connected with the inquiry, which is you. He put you last, I noticed, as if you were the easy one."

"Then we shan't discuss it."

"No."

We both looked up the lane at the door with its strip across it. "That's Pascoe's," said Demelza. "He did seals at the Academy with a ruler."

"He did it nicely."

"He would."

She unbuttoned the breast pocket of her tunic and took out the black leather wallet with her warrant card in it. "He said that if I felt I couldn't keep to the rules of the service I knew where to put this. On his desk, in the morning." She opened it under the lamp. The photograph showed a girl of twenty-six with her plait pinned up for the occasion and the expression of someone who has been told to stop smiling. We looked at it between us. On the chimney opposite a gull said something rude.

Then she shut the wallet, put it back, buttoned the flap and patted it flat, the way you put back a coin you have decided not to spend.

"Jago Rowe has said eleven words since two o'clock this morning," she said. "Nine of them were 'tea, please'. I'm going back to hear the other two."

"Demelza."

The scrap was in my cardigan pocket, with the loupe and the string, and for a moment it felt as warm as the stove had on Friday morning. I had promised to tell her first. I had told her first, on Monday, and she had said she would come with a bag, and had not. There was nothing I could give her that night but my fear, and she had enough of her own.

"Nothing," I said. "Eat something."

"Right," said Demelza. "Facts." She went down the lane, a short, wide woman with a helmet under her arm and her back very straight, and did not look round.

* * *

The search had put my paste pots out on the step. There were eight of them in a row with their lids on the ground beside them, because the constable who wanted the counter clear had not known where else a paste pot goes, and they looked like patients in a corridor. I sat down among them. Quire got on my knee, which she does not do, thought better of it, and moved to the pot with the least paste in it.

I counted what I had, as you count the till on a bad Friday. A pin-prick at the foot of a gathering, which proved that my husband had been fond of something he never mentioned. A charred corner of paper that said K. and Mrs P., which a man who wished to could read as Kit and Mrs Pellow, two people on one step. A leaf of a pilot's book lying between blotters under a concordance. A cat. I noticed that I was holding the cat in particular, as though she were evidence.

Then I counted the other things, which are the ones that wake you. The Michaelmas rent, ninety-two pounds to the Harbour Trustees, who had let the building to the Pellows since 1951 and had never once been paid late. The electricity, with the new strip light in it. Takings since the fair, eleven pounds forty. Nine jobs pinned to the cork board behind the seal, each with a name and a date, and each name belonging to somebody who would hear by Sunday that the Bindery had a police strip across its door. In a village of six hundred a bookbinder is not a woman you send your grandmother's hymnal to if she has been shut out of her own bench on suspicion of murder. One need not be unkind to be careful.

The good tin was forty-two pence. I worked that out too, sitting there, and it was the most cheerful sum of the evening.

Down on the Hard the children were playing sardines. One child hides, and everybody else hunts, and whoever finds her squeezes in beside her without a word, until the last seeker is left alone, hunting for a hiding place packed with giggling children. They had chosen the old lifeboat shed, whose door has not shut since Suez. I could see it from the step. The last seeker, a small boy in a pullover, went past the door with his hands out in front of him, the way you feel for a switch in the dark, while behind it nine of his friends held their breath. Then he went past it again.

I had done that.

The church clock was striking eleven as I reached the corner of the old pilchard cellar on Thursday night, and I counted the strokes, as one does, and by the last of them I was level with the press-room window. I was humming something from the music round, I could not tell you now what. The window was a square of black slate. I thought: he has gone home with my key in his pocket, and tomorrow he will bring it back with an accurate remark, and I shall be obliged to say thank you. I thought of my bed. I did not put my hand on the sash and say his name, and I did not so much as turn my head.

The doctor from Truro had said between ten and midnight, which is a doctor's way of saying he would rather not. If it was before eleven, I had walked past Aubrey in the dark. If it was after, I had walked past whoever did it, and been walked past in return. I have never been able to decide which I should rather have. Garrow would say that if I had gone in there would have been two of us on the boards, and he would be right, and I have said as much to myself in the voice of a sensible woman at three in the morning a great many times. It makes no difference whatever. He was on one side of a pane of glass and I was on the other, thinking about my bed.

* * *

It was quite dark when Tamsin came. I had not heard her on the lane because she had come down from my cottage, with my brown teapot in its knitted cosy held in both hands like an offering, two mugs hooked on her fingers and the bag of sugar still under her arm. She had been carrying it about since ten past three. She had stood at the corner of the cellar for the whole of the warrant, she told me afterwards, and then gone to her mother's for her tea so as not to be asked, and the policemen had taken her sleeping bag out of the loft and put it back with the zip the wrong way round, which she took personally.

At the time she said nothing at all. She sat down on the step below mine, among the pots, and poured, and put two sugars in my mug without asking, which is how I take it and which I had not known she knew. She gave it to me. We drank.

I know how long it was because the church clock struck the quarter and then the half. The children came up from the Hard in a straggle, and fathers whistled them in from doorways. The tide went on out under the quay, leaving its weed and its smell. Tamsin's fingers were blue to the second knuckle, from some pen. She held the mug in both hands and looked at the harbour like a person reading small print. Quire left her pot and sat between us with her back to the pair of us, which is as near as she comes to a blessing.

Nobody had said a word to me for twenty minutes, and it was the most useful twenty minutes of the day. Everyone else that evening had talked to me from somewhere else, from the far side of a trestle or a lamp or a rule. Tamsin did it from the same step.

When the last of the tea was gone she set her mug down on a paste pot, wiped her hands on her jeans, and said, like a girl reading from a list in her head, "I've got a copy of Ned's notebook. Some of it."
