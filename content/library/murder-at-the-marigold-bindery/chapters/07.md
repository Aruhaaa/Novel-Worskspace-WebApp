# Somebody Else's Key

The otter came up as lot forty-one, and the hall lights found both its eyes at once.

It stood on its hind legs in a glass case a yard high, on a riverbank of green baize and cotton wool, with a pewter-coloured trout clamped in its jaws and one paw raised, as if it had been about to ask a question and been interrupted. Someone had stuffed it around 1890 and never forgiven it. The eyes were amber glass, enormous, and under the new tube lights of St Petroc's hall they blazed like a pair of headlamps on a wet road. The front row, which was entirely Women's Institute, leaned back together.

"Lot forty-one," said Jago Rowe. "An otter."

The Saturday auction had been Aubrey's idea and Aubrey's gavel, and with its owner lying in a drawer in Truro the whole business had fallen, as things in Trevenna do, to the nearest large man. Jago stood at the lectern in a waistcoat last buttoned in about 1974, the gavel lost in his right hand (the one with four fingers), and ran the sale in the voice of a tide coming up the Slip: slow, unhurried and certain to arrive. He had not looked at me once all evening. I had been sitting at my trestle by the tea urn for three hours, mending the hinges of other people's Bibles, and I was used to being looked past. It was only that he did it so carefully.

"Who'll start me at five pounds?"

Kit, who had carried the case up onto the table in both arms like a man bringing in a drowned relative, said "Sorry" to the otter and stepped back.

"Five," said Mrs Bolitho.

Ada Bolitho keeps the post office and has not been outbid on anything since the Coronation. She wore a raspberry felt hat that needed both hands to remove, and she had already turned round in her chair to tell the row behind what the otter was for. "Hats on the paw," she said, "umbrella through the trout. I've the very spot in the hall. Twenty years I've been looking for a thing to put in that spot."

"Seven," said a voice from the back.

Heads turned. Pieter de Vries sat at the end of the last row in a folding chair that did not contain him, his white jacket spread over its edges like a tablecloth, his round glasses holding two small otters. He had raised one finger perhaps half an inch from the catalogue on his knee. He was looking at nothing at all.

"Seven pounds, from the gentleman at the back," said Jago, as if announcing a drowning. "Eight?"

"Ten," said Mrs Bolitho.

"Twelve." The finger went up half an inch, and came down.

It was a good deal like watching a heron and a terrier dispute a ditch. At twenty-five the room sat up. At forty it began to enjoy itself, and somebody at the back had to be told to stop whistling. Mrs Bolitho bid with her whole body, rising on the balls of her feet to do it, hat tilted like a mainsail, and Pieter bid with nothing but the finger, in a stillness so complete that by sixty pounds I had begun to find it ghastly. He did not look at the otter, or at her, or at the roof. At seventy, Imogen Mayne, behind the cash table in her leather jacket, put down her pencil. At ninety, Mrs Chegwidden said "Disgraceful" in a voice of deep contentment.

"I've had that hall in my head since nineteen sixty-one," said Mrs Bolitho to the room at large, "and I'll not be beaten by a map."

"One hundred and thirty," said Pieter.

"A hundred and thirty-five!"

Pieter turned his head and for the first time looked at her. Then he rose, the whole pale length of him, and bowed from the waist, and the hall, which has a great sense of occasion, went "Ahh."

"Madam," he said. "I give way. It will look very well, this, with hats."

"It will," said Mrs Bolitho, and sat down hard.

Jago brought the gavel down on a hundred and thirty-five pounds. At the side of the hall the Reverend Nance, who had been standing with his lips moving, visibly added it to the roof.

* * *

At the interval I joined the queue for the urn and found myself behind forty inches of white linen.

"Mrs Pellow." Pieter inclined his head. "You will tell me I am a fool, I think."

"I was going to say eccentric. What would you have done with an otter in Rotterdam?"

"I do not know. Posted it, perhaps." He took his cup from the urn lady and considered it as he had considered the otter. "Please to think. A man has died in a village. Here is a foreigner with maps and the wrong kind of paper, and every person in this hall has watched him two days to see what he will do. So. Now they have seen. He wanted an otter." He tasted the tea and bore it. "People forgive a great deal in a man who wants an otter, this."

"And if she had stopped bidding?"

"Mrs Bolitho does not stop. I watched her at the tombola."

He went off down the hall with his cup held out in front of him like a candle, and I thought, not for the first time that day, that there are more kinds of honest than one, and that Pieter de Vries had chosen a narrow one.

I went back to my trestle, where a water-stained *Pilgrim's Progress* lay with its back joint split from head to tail. I had the bone folder in my hand when I became aware of a presence at my elbow. The Reverend Arthur Nance has a way of arriving as if he has been there some time and been too polite to mention it. He stooped over the trestle, a cough drop clicking against his teeth, his bicycle clips still on, though he had walked forty yards from the vestry.

"Mrs Pellow. Forgive me. I wonder if I might put a, a hypothetical."

"You may put what you like. I'm only mending Bunyan."

"Quite. Quite." He looked at the book, and at the urn, and at the ceiling, where a brown stain the shape of Ireland had been advancing since Easter. "A book. Let us say a book that has, ah, left its owner's keeping by irregular means. Might it be, in your experience, possible to buy it back?"

"Stolen, you mean."

"I did not say stolen." The cough drop travelled from one cheek to the other. "I am sure it is nothing. A friend, merely. A friend of a friend."

I have known Arthur Nance for twenty-seven years and I do not believe he has a friend of a friend. He has a cousin in Hayle who sells insurance, and a dentist.

"It depends where it's got to," I said. "If it's in the thief's attic you can't buy it back, you can only tell the police and wait. If it's been sold on, you can find the man who has it and offer him what he paid, and then something on top for his trouble, and then something on top of that for his pride. It's always possible, Reverend. It's the money that's difficult."

"Ah." His eyes went to Ireland again. "Yes. The money."

"Is it a parish matter?"

"No! Gracious, no. No." He patted the trestle, apologised to it, and was carried off by Mrs Chegwidden, who required a blessing on the tombola. I looked at Bunyan's broken joint for some while. A book that had left its owner's keeping by irregular means: I had one of those on my mind already, and it was in a paper sack in Truro.

* * *

The otter took forty minutes and the rest of the lots took another hour and a half, and after that there were the unsold boxes to stack, and the takings to count. Kit went off to the Lantern at nine with a darts match in his pocket, apologising to the door. Imogen counted the money three times on the vestry table, a cigarette burning in a saucer that Mrs Chegwidden had provided with the expression of a woman signing a treaty, and I stayed to help her, or to be near other people. Every night that week I had climbed past the press-room window as one passes a dog one does not trust. It was midnight before Imogen shut the cash box with a snap, said "Well, that's that, then," in a voice that was not about the money, and let me out into the dark.

The tide had turned. It was making small sucking noises at the foot of the Hard, drawing the harbour off like a cloth from a table. I climbed Fish Cellar Lane slowly, between the old pilchard cellars with their holiday curtains drawn, and a smell of wet rope came up with me from the quay. The lamp at the bottom gave out at the third house. After that there was only the grey of the granite and the yellow of my own door, which at night turns the colour of old cheese.

On Thursday I had walked up this lane at eleven o'clock and looked at the press-room window and seen only dark, and gone on to bed. I had told no one. I have told several people since, and it has not got lighter.

The window was not dark. The lower sash stood four inches up, a black wedge in the grey paint, and on the sill lay a curl of white paint like a pencil shaving. Someone had worked a blade in between the sashes from outside and slid the catch back as neatly as you open a tin.

I stood a minute with my hand on the cold stone. Then I went round to my own door, let myself into the shop with my own key, and took the yard rule down from its hook. It has killed nothing larger than a wasp, but it is the only weapon on the premises I trust.

The shop was as I had left it. The till drawer held fourteen pounds sixty and a button. I stood at the press-room door and listened, and somewhere low down in the dark, behind wood, there rose a long, thin, rising complaint, like a kettle with a grievance.

"Quire?"

The complaint ceased, drew breath, and resumed at a higher pitch.

I put on the light. The strip over the nipping press, which was fitted last year against all my objections, ticked and buzzed and came on in three stages like a man getting out of a chair. Cold air poured through the open window and brought the harbour with it. The bench was bare; the lying press stood where it had stood; the chair was in its place. The only thing out of true was the paste cupboard to the right of the stove, a tall pine thing with a ledge door, because the noise was coming out of it.

It was shut. I took the key off its bootlace, where it had hung against my breastbone since Saturday morning, and turned it in the lock, and the door swung out on a flash of white bib and a torn ear, and Quire came out of it like a cork. She went over my foot, hissed at the yard rule, hissed at me, and stalked to the stove where she sat down with her back to the room and began to wash. There was a saucer on the floor of the cupboard. She had drunk every drop of what had been in it, which did not in her opinion lessen the offence.

On the second shelf, between the alum and the clove oil, there was a clean oblong in the dust where a small green book had lain for longer than I had been married.

I shut the door again and turned the key. It went round as sweetly as a clock. I bent with the torch from the shop drawer and looked at the keyhole, at the brass plate, at the pine around it. Not a scratch. Not a bruise. Whoever had been in that cupboard had opened it with a key, and had locked it again with a key, and the only key I knew of was lying on my chest.

I stood with that for some time. Then I went into the shop and rang Demelza.

* * *

She was there in six minutes, which is quick for a woman who has had to find her boots. She came in under the bell wearing her police overcoat buttoned one hole out, tartan flannel pyjama bottoms, and a pair of green gumboots, with her plait over one shoulder and a torch the size of a rolling pin. At her wrist, where the coat sleeve rode up, was a grey cuff that I had darned myself in 1987. I looked at it and then, with great care, at the till.

"Right. Facts," she said. "Door?"

"Locked. I used my key."

"Window?"

"Up. Forced from outside, I should think. I've touched nothing but the cupboard."

"Anyone inside?"

"Only the cat. She was in the cupboard."

"In the cupboard."

"Shut in. With a saucer of milk."

Demelza looked at me. Then she went through into the press room and shone the torch round it, slowly, left to right, and stopped on the sill and on the bright curl of paint. She put her face to the glass. "Chisel," she said. "Thin one. Not a boy, a boy would have put his elbow through. Somebody who knew what they were doing and wasn't in a hurry." She straightened. "Mr Trevithick's spare key is still missing, so whoever's got it had no need of a window."

"Then why use one?"

"To say they hadn't a key." She wrote it down. "What's gone?"

I had decided on the walk from the shop what I would say. I said it. "Ned's notebook. A small green cloth one, with a rubber band. Every job he ever did and every pupil he ever taught, from 1951. It was in the cupboard."

The pencil stopped. "Every pupil."

"I've never read it. I can't bear his hand."

"A list of everyone your husband taught to bind a book," said Demelza, quite gently, "has been sitting in a cupboard for two days after a man was killed over a forged one, and you are telling me about it at ten past one in the morning."

"I did mean to."

"We'll come back to that." She tapped the pencil on the book twice. "And the cupboard was locked. Who has the key?"

"It hung on the third hook of the board in the shop, by the yard bolt, for thirty years. Anyone who ever worked here knew where it was."

She went and looked at the board with her torch. Eleven hooks, ten keys, and the third one bare. "Not there."

"No."

"So someone's had it off the hook, opened the cupboard, taken the book, put the cat in, locked up, and walked off with the key in their pocket." She nodded, pleased with it, as she should have been. "I'll want that cat's saucer. And nobody in that room till the Truro man comes at eight."

I want it understood that I did not lie to her. I have been over every word. Everything I said was true, and I let her have the rest, as one lets a child have the last biscuit. It was a good deduction and a wrong one. I could not have told you why I held my tongue, except that a second key meant a second person who had once stood close enough to Ned to be given it, and I did not care to hand that to a woman in pyjamas at one in the morning. I wanted to turn it over by myself first. That is what I told myself, with the thing lying cold against my breastbone, where it was heavier than it had been at supper.

Demelza taped the sash, and set the shop chair against the press-room door, and sat in it with her coat round her knees and the torch in her lap, and I took the stool. We did not speak for a while. Quire stayed by the cold stove with her back to us both, washing the same shoulder, which is how she sulks.

Before the door was shut I had looked once more into the cupboard. The saucer sat on the floor under the bottom shelf, licked to a bright circle. It was one of mine, from the blue-rimmed set that lives on the tea shelf in the shop, bought with Co-op coupons the year we were married. And beside it, laid straight on the boards with its bowl turned up and its handle pointing to the door, as you lay a spoon for a guest who has come to stay the evening, lay one of my teaspoons.
