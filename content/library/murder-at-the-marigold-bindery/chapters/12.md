# The Strip Light

"Triangles," said Mrs Chegwidden, "are not a matter of opinion."

She held up a template cut from a cornflake packet. It was a long thin triangle with a base of nine inches and a height of eleven, and she held it at arm's length as a priest holds up the Host. Across the trestle from her, Miss Hocking, who taught arithmetic at Truro for thirty-one years and has never forgiven the pupils, held up a second one, which was equal on all three sides.

"Bunting is equilateral," said Miss Hocking, "by nature."

"Bunting is whatever shape is left of the flag," said Mrs Bolitho, in her raspberry hat, which she had not taken off since the otter. "Nobody ever cut a flag for the pleasure of the geometry."

"That is a very common attitude, Ada, and a very sad one."

It was ten past seven on Tuesday evening in the Women's Institute hall, which is a green tin hut on Net Loft Row with a stove-pipe, a stage the size of a double bed and a hatch through which, for sixty years, the Institute has handed one another cake. Eight women sat round a pair of trestles under a single strip light that flickered whenever the urn came on. Three sewing machines stood among us: Mrs Rosevear's mother's treadle, a school electric that ran at two speeds, stop and flee, and a hand-cranked affair of Miss Hocking's that she said had come to her from a Dutch sea-captain and I believed it had come from a bomb-site. Round our feet, in drifts, lay the sort of red and white and blue and yellow scraps that might in a better world have been somebody's best flags.

I was the eighth woman. I should like it known that I had not wanted to be. Morwenna had caught me on the way down from the Court and said that bunting at seven was not a request, that the Institute had eight pairs of hands and four of them could not hem, and that I had a face on me that wanted company. I had said I would come if I could sit near the door. She had said I could sit where I liked, so long as it was in the hall.

"It was *not*," said Mrs Rosevear, from the treadle, with the brimming eyes of a woman who has been rehearsing a grievance since Sunday, "from Pengelly's."

"Doris," said Morwenna.

"It was a *family* recipe. It was my mother's."

"Doris, my handsome. There was a doily in your bin with Pengelly's of Truro stamped on it in blue."

"That might have been anybody's bin."

"It was in your bin, dear, in the Chapel Anniversary Tea, with your name on the entry, and it won." Morwenna did not raise her voice; she doesn't. She spoke as to a child who has fallen off a wall, and put down the hem she was turning. "Nobody's saying a word. But a cake is a promise, Doris. That's all it is. You say, I made this, and you put it on the table, and everybody eats it on your word."

Mrs Rosevear cried, comfortably, into the flag she was supposed to be hemming. Morwenna patted her, and said she would show her how to make it right, a proper yellow loaf, and then she'd be the only other woman in Cornwall who could, and Mrs Rosevear blew her nose and said, "That's very good of you," in a voice of pure doomed gratitude. It had taken eleven minutes, start to finish. In the Institute, justice is a form of catering.

* * *

At half past seven Morwenna lifted a flour sack onto the end of the trestle, and it came down with a thump that made the cups jump.

"Now," she said. "Before it goes under glass for the prize-giving. I want you all to see my hinge."

She took the Regatta Book from the sack in both hands. It is a ledger of foolscap size, half calf, with marbled paper sides in the old Spanish pattern, a red label on the spine stamped REGATTA in gilt, and corners rubbed down to the board by twenty-five years of the thumbs of treasurers. Inside, in a score of hands, it holds the name of every crew that has won the gig race at Trevenna since 1921. Ned bound it in 1964. I remember him finishing it, at the bench, in March, humming "Bread of Heaven" in a different key every time he reached the bridge.

"There," said Morwenna. "Back joint split clean from head to tail at Christmas, the poor thing. I did it with a strip of calf and a bit of paste and a great deal of prayer. Have a look."

The Book went round the trestles. "Lovely," said Mrs Bolitho. "You'd never know," said Mrs Opie. "What?" said Mrs Trewhella, who is deaf as a kipper and sits near the stove. "THE HINGE," said four women together, and she said, "Oh, *yes*," with great conviction, and passed it on.

It reached me last. I took it on my knees as I would take any book that wanted looking at, with a hand under each board, and the thing took my knees with it. It is difficult to describe how it felt. A ledger of that size ought to weigh what a good-sized loaf weighs, and this settled down into my wrists like a sleeping child. Heavy, I thought. A great deal heavier than a ledger.

Well, it was Ned's. He bound everything as if the book expected to be thrown downstairs.

I ran my thumb along the back joint, and then looked for the join, and for a good ten seconds could not find it. I looked for the pared edge of the new calf, the shine of the paste. I know where those things hide. I have spent twenty-seven years hiding them. Morwenna had not left me so much as a ridge.

"That's very good," I said. "That's better than mine."

"Paste and prayer, my handsome."

"It's a good deal better than prayer. Where did you learn to do that?"

"Oh, I've watched a good deal of mending in my time." She took the Book back, gently, and set it in her lap. "You can't live in Trevenna and not pick up a bit. It's the only trade it has, Dot, bar fish."

* * *

The door banged. The rain came in round it, and then Imogen Mayne, in black leather, with her cropped hair plastered flat to her skull and a cardboard folder clutched against her chest. She stood on the mat, with water running off her nose stud, and looked round the hall.

The three machines stopped, one after another, the treadle last. Eight women laid their hands in their laps and looked at her, like a jury that has not yet been told the verdict.

"We are only doing the bunting," said Mrs Chegwidden.

"So I see." Imogen looked at the folder as if it had spoken. "I was looking for the treasurer. I found Daddy's regatta papers. The accounts, and the order of procession, and a lot of letters from the Harbour Commissioners in a tone I'd rather not describe. I thought somebody ought to have them. Unless this is a bad moment."

"It is never a bad moment, my handsome," said Morwenna, and was on her feet before the others. "Come in off that mat before you rust. Sit. Eat."

Then they pounced. I have watched a flock of gulls take a dropped chip and I have never seen it done more thoroughly. In the space of four minutes Imogen was seated in the best chair, in front of the stove, with a cup of tea in one hand and an egg-and-cress sandwich in the other and a second sandwich, of unknown filling, put into her pocket by Mrs Opie for later. She had been asked whether she was eating, whether she was sleeping, whether she was staying, and where. ("The Lantern." "With *Jago*?") She had been asked whether it was true about the will, to which she answered, with admirable dryness, that she was not permitted to know until the solicitor had finished being sorry. She had been asked whether the Inspector was a Methodist. She was asked whether she would come to chapel, and by the end she had agreed to present the cup at the prize-giving, because Miss Hocking said it was the chairman's daughter's place, and Imogen said that Daddy would have been appalled, and Miss Hocking said that was a reason.

"He wasn't an easy man," said Mrs Opie, tenderly. "But he was a good chairman, dear."

"He said no to most things," said Imogen.

"He said it beautifully," said Morwenna. She had a hand on the girl's shoulder; Imogen did not shrug it off. "He never once said no to a flag."

Imogen laughed, a short surprised sound, and put her hand to her mouth; and I saw her eyes go round the hall and find me, and rest, and move on. I knew where she had been at ten past ten on the Thursday, and what she had done to a wall after. I said nothing. I felt as though I had taken her purse.

She took out a cigarette, and a match.

"Not in the hall, dear," said Mrs Chegwidden. "There's a saucer in the kitchen."

"Daddy let me smoke in the vestry."

"The vestry has been thoroughly forgiven," said Mrs Chegwidden. "The hall has not."

* * *

I do not know how it began. A hall full of women who have been sewing for two hours is a hot, close, dangerous place, and the strip light had been going like a moth against a window for most of the evening. Someone complained of her sciatica. Someone else had a head. Mrs Rosevear had a head with the sun, and Miss Hocking said she had one with Mrs Chegwidden, and Mrs Trewhella said "WHAT?" and was told, and said that in her day nobody had a head, they had a duty. It went round the room like a hymn.

"It's these strips," said Morwenna. She had put the Book back in its sack and was pressing two fingers to her temple, with her eyes shut. "I've had a head on me since Easter. They flicker, you see, and it gets in behind the eyes. That one of yours over the press, Dot. I stuck my head in at Easter to say good morning and I came out with a head like a bell. Never again."

"It does flicker," said Mrs Opie. "The one in the post office does it. I told Ada."

"It does *not*," said Mrs Bolitho.

"Does it?" I said. "It's a dreadful thing. Ned would have hated it. It makes everyone look like—"

At that moment Jenifer Opie, who had gone to the hatch for the tea, turned the tap of the urn. The urn is an immense copper vessel, the size of a drum, older than the Institute. It coughed, shrieked, and fired a jet of boiling water clean across the hatch and down the hall. It caught Mrs Trewhella in the feet. Gwen Trewhella, who had not run since the Coronation, ran. She went round the trestle in a half-circle with her skirt held up and her mouth open and a noise coming out of it that I had not heard from a woman of seventy since the Blitz, and Mrs Chegwidden said "Gwen, *do* sit," and Mrs Bolitho's hat came off, both hands, and fell into the trifle. I laughed. I laughed until I had to put down my hem and hold the table with both hands, and tears were running down my face, and for a minute or two there was nothing in the hall but women bent double, and the urn spitting, and the strip light going off and on above us like a lighthouse in a bad temper.

It was the first time I had laughed in a week. I would not have missed it.

* * *

The men arrived at a quarter to nine, together, which neither enjoyed.

Jago Rowe filled the doorway. The Reverend Nance came in under his arm, wheeling his bicycle backwards into the porch and apologising to it, and the two of them stood on the mat and looked at the bunting, which was now strung from the hall beam to the stage rail in a gaudy wet swag, to check the lengths, with Miss Hocking's broom holding up the middle.

"We've come about the Book," said Jago.

"The procession order," said Nance, "if we might. Forgive me."

Mrs Chegwidden took the folder from Imogen and read it with the voice of an usher. "*The Regatta Book is carried from the Slip to the finishing line by the Chairman, who presents it to the winning cox.*" She looked up. "There is no Chairman."

"Then it's the Vice," said Jago. "Aubrey carried it. I'll carry it. That's the size of it."

"Mr Rowe." Nance had gone pink above the collar. "I would not dream of, ah. But in the circumstances. It is a, a sacred trust, I think, a record of, of the community, and since it will be carried, in some sense, in his memory, I had thought that the parish, that I might, with a blessing, be the right person to—"

"A blessing on a ledger."

"A sacred—"

"It's a list of who won a boat race, Vicar."

"It is the record of a hundred crews, Mr Rowe."

"You would drop it in the harbour, Mr Rowe," said Nance, bravely.

"I have dropped nothing in forty years."

"You dropped the gavel," said Mrs Bolitho, from under her hat, which was now sticky. "On Saturday. Twice."

I was sitting nearest the sack. I do not know what possessed me. I think it was a week of dead men and a night of mac-coloured women and a pint of laughter that had gone to my head, but I picked up the Book from where Morwenna had set it, and I stood, and held it out in front of me at arm's length like the Lord Mayor's regalia.

"Gentlemen," I said. "We shall decide it by weight. Whoever carries the Regatta Book is going to want a back like a hod-carrier. Let us see what we are dealing with."

I had my pocket gauge in my cardigan, where it has lived since 1963: a small brass thing like a clothes-peg with a dial on it, which tells you the thickness of a leaf or a board to the half-millimetre, and which I carry as other women carry a compact. I opened the Book's front cover and set the jaws across the edge of the board, as formally as a doctor with a stethoscope. The little needle swung and settled.

"Three and a half," I said.

"Three and a half what?" said Jago.

"Millimetres. Of board. For a ledger, gentlemen, that is a very stout board. You could stop a small bullet." I snapped the gauge shut. "Whoever carries it, I advise you both to take a barrow."

There was a roar. Jago laughed, a slow, deep, delighted noise like a barrel rolling down a cellar, and Nance laughed until his cough drop shot out on to the boards, and the women banged on the trestle, and I was the Lord Mayor, and for a moment I was entirely visible and it did not hurt. Morwenna took the Book gently out of my hands and slid it into its sack. "Don't scratch it, Dot," she said. "It's the only one we've got." She laughed too, the loudest of anyone.

It was Mrs Bolitho who did it. She had risen to her feet to cheer, and she stepped back to give her hat room, and she trod on the leg of Miss Hocking's broom.

The broom went over. The beam nail, which had been holding the first end of the bunting since six o'clock out of pure obstinacy, came out of the plaster like a tooth, and the whole gaudy length of it, three hundred feet of red and white and blue and yellow, fell on the committee in a single piece with a long soft sigh. It covered the trestles, the sewing machines, the egg-and-cress, two bicycles and the Reverend Nance. The strip light flickered. Out of the heap rose a hand holding a cup, and then a face, with a small blue triangle caught on the top of its silver curls like a hat.

"Well," said Morwenna, quite calmly, taking it off. "That's the third time this week things have come apart."
