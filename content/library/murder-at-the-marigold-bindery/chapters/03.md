# What the Spine Remembers

Demelza gave me ten minutes, and she gave them to me by turning her back.

"I'm not here," she said, from the press-room doorway, where she stood facing the shop with her arms folded and the helmet under one elbow. "I'm not watching you do it and I'm not hearing what you say to the furniture. The man from Truro's stuck behind a tractor, and when he comes he'll put the book in a bag and you won't see it again till the trial. Ten minutes, Mrs Pellow. Gloves."

"I have cotton ones. In the drawer under the plough."

"Don't touch the drawer."

She fetched them herself, hooking the drawer open with her pencil and lifting the pair out on the end of it like a dead mouse. I put them on. They were the gloves I wear for fine work, and my hands, which are not fine, went into them with an air of embarrassment.

By ten the press room had been photographed, powdered and given up on for the time being. The aluminium dust lay grey on the edge of the bench and along the stove door. On the boards, between the nipping press and the bench, there was a dark patch the size of a tea tray, and I decided to be a stranger to it. The room smelled of powder and cold iron and the stale breath of a place that has had too many men in it. The book lay where it had lain at seven o'clock. I took my loupe out of my cardigan pocket, put my glasses up on my forehead on their bootlace, and began, as Ned taught me, at the beginning.

A book is a record of what has been done to it. That is the whole of the trade. Sew a gathering, unpick it, sew it again, and the paper keeps the account like a bad tooth: every hole stays, and each is a scar that tells you something. Ned used to say that a good binder reads a book as a doctor reads an old soldier.

I knew the Carne Rutter better than I had thought. I remembered Loveday Penhallow coming in the door in the winter of 1971, in gumboots under a tweed coat, with the book wrapped in a pillowcase, and saying to my husband, "Don't fuss, Pellow," before he had said a word. He told her he never fussed; he dithered, with intent. Five weeks he had it. He took the spine off and made a new one, a tight back, so well that you could not find the seam, and he did it by lamplight because he said the skylight was too honest.

Now I held it open under that honest light and counted. The sewing of 1631 had been done by a man who measured by eye: four stations to a fold, two crowded at the head and a long gap and then two more, like a drunk's staircase. Ned had sewn through those same holes in 1971 and made two new ones of his own at the tail where the old thread had rotted the paper. I found the four old and the two new, on the first gathering and on the last, small and sad and exactly where he would have put them.

In the fat middle of the book there were five holes to every fold. They were clean and they were evenly spaced, an inch and a half apart, as if someone had used a ruler and a spirit of tidiness, and there were no other holes at all. The paper had never been sewn before. Those leaves had not been in this cover when Ned finished with it. They had come to it as strangers.

I turned a middle leaf to the skylight with my gloved fingers, as one lifts a sleeping child. The chain lines were close as the teeth of a comb. In the corner, pale against the light, sat a lion in a little paling fence with a hat on a pole; any schoolchild in Holland could have named it. The ink was a clerk's brown and the hand a neat round one, and the lines went down the page in columns. *To Wm Tresidder for thatching, 3s. 6d. To a ewe sheep, 14s.* A rutter tells a man where the rocks are. This told him what he owed the thatcher.

The first two leaves were different, and I could feel the difference through the cotton. They were heavy, soft, the colour of oatmeal, with the chain lines wide apart like furrows on a hill seen from the road, and across them a seaman's handwriting in ink gone the brown of tea: *from Falmouth to the Lizard keep well off*. The last two were the same. Between them, somebody had sewn a farmer's accounts into a pilot's book and made a good job of it.

I will say I stood there some while. Behind me Demelza shifted her weight and said nothing. The heart had been taken out of the Carne Rutter, and there was nothing in its place that any man could sail by.

The boards had lost their pastedowns as well, both of them, torn away in strips and scrapings so that the millboard showed through like a scraped knee. I could not think what a person wants with the endpapers of a book. They are blank, most of the time, or hold a name and a date, an owner's pencil, a price. Aubrey wrote in pencil on everything; it was a habit that had driven Ned to distraction. I looked about the bench for the stub he carried in his waistcoat, and for any paper he might have written on, and found neither.

There were two things left, and I made myself take them in order, as Ned would have. The last gathering was the first. I lifted it free and turned it to the foot, where the margin is widest and a man might do something nobody would ever see. I took it at first for a flaw in the paper. It was a pin-prick, round and clean, made with a point and not a tear, through the foot of the fold and through every leaf of the gathering at once. I put the loupe to it. It was no larger than a full stop in a hymn book.

I knew it. I do not know how; I had never seen it in my life. I knew his hands, and the fine awl in the tin, and the way he finished a job, holding the book up to the window with his head on one side and his tongue between his teeth, and then the little movement of the wrist that I had taken for a man shaking off a cramp. Twenty-four years I watched my husband finish books. He signed them where nobody would look, and he never told me, and I do not know whom he thought would find them.

I found the cutting-board stool with the backs of my knees, which is how I know it is where it ought to be.

"Mrs Pellow?" said Demelza, to the shop. "Seven minutes. Are you all right?"

"I'm sitting down."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one I've got."

She did not turn round, which was a kindness I have thought about since. I sat with the gathering held in my gloved hands like a bird. On the shelf above the stool the cupboard where I keep the alum and the clove oil, and Ned's old tins, stood shut, and I could see from where I sat that the little brass lock was turned, as I had turned it on Wednesday. Nothing in that room had been taken or touched, as far as I could see, but the book and the hammer. I got up, because I had two things left and not much time.

The second thing was the kettle stitch. It is the knot at head and tail where one gathering links to the next, and in the middle of the book every one of them turned left. Mine turn right. Ned's turned left, and it was one of the three things we quarrelled over in twenty-four years of marriage, and on the other two he was wrong. I looked along the whole run of knots at the head and then at the tail, and each leant the same way, with a little hooked tuck at the finish that I had stopped seeing in my own kitchen because I had stopped seeing him do it. Whoever had sewn the middle of that book had been taught at Ned's elbow.

There were not many people it could have been. I did not count them. I put the thought down the way one puts down a hot dish, carefully, and with the other hand already reaching for a cloth.

I lifted the back board, which was loose in my hand, toward the skylight, and from the hinge came a smell I knew at once and could not, for a moment, place in that room. Cloves. Oranges and nutmeg and string; the week before Christmas; my mother-in-law's kitchen. Ned's paste. One drop of oil of clove to a pint of wheat starch and alum, against the mould, and the smell outlasts the paste by years: I have opened a book Ned pasted in 1964 and been sent back to Christmas. It was there in the hinge, fresh, sweet and sharp, a Christmas smell in a room where a man had died in July.

"Time," said Demelza.

Garrow came in at ten past, with a young man in a car coat who carried a case like a doctor's and had the grey face of one who has been behind a tractor since Truro. I had the Carne Rutter laid open on the bench with the lamp on it and the gloves still on. The Inspector looked at the gloves.

"Mrs Pellow, dear. I hope you have not been interfering."

"Reading," I said.

"Reading what?"

"Your evidence. It isn't what it says it is." I told him. I told it quite plainly and in order, the holes and the paper and the lion in the fence; the heart of the book that wasn't; the pin-prick I did not mention, because I did not yet know how to say it aloud. I said the cover was Ned's work of 1971 and the middle was not the Carne Rutter at all, and that whoever had put it there had done it since the spring, because the paste in the hinge was still sweet.

He heard me out with his head tilted, the way one listens to a child recite. "The middle of the book is not the middle of the book."

"Yes."

"A forgery, in effect, of a work Mr Trevithick was valuing."

"It would appear so, Inspector."

"And who in Trevenna could do a job like that?"

"Anyone who could sew. Anyone who knew how. It takes years."

"How many bookbinders are there in Trevenna, Mrs Pellow?"

"One."

"Quite," said Garrow, and let it lie there between us for the length of time it takes a man to enjoy his own pause. "And she was in the Lantern on Thursday night, was she? With witnesses?"

"With a quiz team and a pint of cider and the whole of the Lantern Regulars, who would be delighted to tell you I was wrong about Elgar." I kept my voice level, with some effort. "I said it was done by someone who had been taught. I did not say by whom."

"I see." He turned to the young man, who held out a long brown paper sack. "We'll have that looked at by somebody with the proper letters after his name, Mrs Pellow, dear, and I'll want it in a statement. You've been most helpful. Most observant."

"I've twenty-seven years at that bench," I said. "They don't give letters for it."

"Quite." He lifted the Carne Rutter in both hands, as if it were a hot plate, and set it down in the sack. It looked very small. He smiled at me. "And in the meantime I'd be obliged if you wouldn't leave the village."

"Where would I go? I haven't been further than Truro since 1974."

"Then it'll be no hardship," said Detective Inspector Garrow, and went out with the sack, and the young man after him, and neither of them looked back at the room.

I took the gloves off one finger at a time. Demelza had been writing again, and she did not look up. I stood at the bench, where the lamp still burned on nothing, and said it aloud in the end, because there was nobody else to say it to.

"Someone sewed that book the way my husband taught, with my husband's paste."

"Then someone's been in your shop," said Demelza, not looking up from her notebook.
