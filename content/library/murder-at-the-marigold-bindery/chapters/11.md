# Tea at Penhallow Court

"You'll want your mac," said Morwenna, "and a scarf. Don't look at me like that. It's rain."

She stood on my step at two o'clock on Tuesday with water running off the brim of her plastic rain-bonnet, a tin under one arm and her handbag on the other, in which there was a second tin, because there always is. I have never known Morwenna Hext go anywhere without cake in some form. She would take shortbread to a lifeboat launch.

"Loveday asked me to tea," I said. "I didn't ask for an escort."

"Loveday asked you to tea, and I go up on Tuesdays regardless, so you can walk with me and save the Court the sight of a woman coming through the shrubbery like a drowned cat. And not another word, Dot. Mind the step."

So we went up the hill together in the rain, two women in macs and headscarves, with our heads down and our shoulders up: the pale kind, with a belt, that every woman in Cornwall owns and none of us remembers buying. A man coming down the lane with a sack on his back looked at us without seeing us and touched his cap to the air between. Nobody on that hill could have told us from any other two. I have found it restful, on the whole, over the years, and that afternoon for no reason I disliked it.

The track runs up past the church and the last of the cottages, between walls so thick with fuchsia and montbretia that they look to be holding up the sky, and on to the long hill where the Court sits with its back to the sea. It is a fifteen-minute walk. With Morwenna's knees it is twenty-five, and I did not mind that either. She told me about Ronald as she climbed. He had known her on Sunday. He had looked up from his chair in the day room at Gwithian House, and said, "Morwenna, who's died?" and she had said, "Nobody, my handsome," and he had looked so cross.

"On a good day he asks who's died," she said. "On a bad one he asks who I am, and I tell him I'm the lady with the cake, and he's very polite."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. He was never a man for long speeches." She got a hand on a gatepost and heaved. "And you, Dot? I've seen how you look. Garrow's been at you."

"He thinks I broke into my own shop for the sympathy."

"You would sooner be flogged."

"I would."

"Well, you've had cake instead, and I hope you ate it."

"I ate it standing up. It was very good." I found, to my irritation, that my voice was not quite level. "It was kind, Morwenna."

"It was flour," said Morwenna, and went on up the hill ahead of me, small and round and entirely cheerful, with the rain bouncing off her bonnet.

* * *

Penhallow Court was built when granite was cheaper than glass and has been paying for it since. It is a long grey house the colour of a tooth, with chimneys at the corners and gutters in which, in July, buddleia stands six feet high. Morwenna always goes in by the back, through the boot room, as family do, and I followed her into a stone passage that smelled of wet dog and floor polish.

Along one wall ran a row of pegs hung with macs, the pale kind, six of them, the colour of dishwater, and I added mine to the row with the sensation of joining a congregation. On a spread of newspaper by the door stood a pair of black rubber sea-boots, enormous, with their tops turned down, a rim of dried salt along the welts, and a white 12 painted on each heel. I noticed them as one notices a stranger's umbrella.

"The gardener's," said Loveday Penhallow.

She had come up the passage behind us without a sound, in gumboots under a skirt of tweed so old it had turned green, with her blackthorn stick in one beautiful long hand and her hearing aid round her neck in its beige box on a cord. As she turned her head, the thing gave a thin shriek of feedback.

"I wasn't going to ask," I said.

"You were going to ask." The aid whistled again, higher. "Don't gawp, Pellow. Come in out of the wet. Morwenna, my dear, I've a headache and a hip and I've had to be civil to a solicitor, so you may tell me what the Institute wants and I shall give it to you."

It is a peculiar thing about Loveday's hearing aid that it whistles whenever anybody lies. I have never established the mechanism; I do not believe the makers know it. But in eleven years of taking tea at the Court I have never known it wrong, and it has led, in a house where a good many people say a good many things, to a rather noisy life.

At the far end of the passage a woman in a pinafore was doing the tiles with a mop. She did not look up. As we passed, the mop stopped, not for long, about as long as it takes to decide that a thing is not your business, and then went on. Sheila Bray has a face like a closed shop and the hands of a docker, and she is the only person in that house who has never once been thanked.

* * *

The library at Penhallow Court is forty feet long and has been drowning since the Coronation.

There is a bucket under every third shelf. I counted eleven, as the rain found its way through the plaster of the ceiling and let itself down in slow bright beads, and each bucket had its own pitch, from a bright tink to a deep mournful plonk, so that the whole room played a tune. It was a very slow tune with no end to it. Round the walls at the height of my knee ran a line of brown, a tide-mark, where the damp had risen from the floor, thought better of it and turned back. The books above the line were vellum and calf and good green morocco gone grey with age; the books below it were mostly a rumour.

And everywhere there were gaps. Little pink slips of Kit's stood up like flags between the volumes, each with its number in his careful hand, and in places there was no book at all, but a clean oblong on the shelf and a cleaner one on the wall behind it, where something had stood since 1700 and had gone, in a cardboard box, to Truro, to be valued. It looked like a mouth that has lost its teeth and is trying not to smile.

"That is Sir Ambrose," said Loveday, from her chair, "who held the Court for the Crown until he ran out of beer. And that is Captain Hannibal, who lost the silver to a Spaniard at cards in 1703. And that, over the door, is Honor Penhallow, who ran the contraband from Sennen to Mount's Bay for thirty years and was never once found out. And that is my father, who lost the rest." She rapped her stick on the boards. "Eleven generations on this hill, Pellow. Seven of them could read."

The whistle that followed this was long and very shrill. Morwenna, setting out cups, did not even look round.

Sheila Bray brought the tea, put the tray on the table without a word, and went out without one. Loveday looked at Morwenna, who looked at the pot.

"Be mother."

Morwenna took the big Georgian teapot, which was silver and had been dented by a Penhallow, in her left hand, and filled my cup with a long easy turn of the wrist that I remember thinking rather beautiful. Then she glanced down, and paused, and changed the pot into her right hand, and filled Loveday's with the slight stiffness of a woman putting on her good gloves. Neither of them remarked on it. I did not, either. People pour as they please.

"Now," said Loveday. "You have been to see me, and you have not told me what you have found. I asked you on Friday. What have you found?"

"I've found out how little I know."

"Hm. That's more than the Inspector." She turned her good ear to me. "He came on Saturday. He said the Carne has been *interfered with*. I told him that was what the vicar said about the choir boys in 1951."

"Loveday," said Morwenna.

"Well, it was." She sat back. "You needn't tell me, Pellow. I saw his face. When you know, you'll tell me, and I'll know what to do about it, and we'll say no more." She looked at the wall where the ghost of a shelf-gap began, and her hand tightened on the stick. "Do you know the story of it?"

"Everyone knows the story of it."

"Nobody knows the story of it. They know a pilot's book." She nodded to Morwenna. "Tell her."

"It's yours to tell, my dear."

"Then I shall. November 1631." Loveday lifted her chin and her voice dropped half an octave, into the register that I imagine her family reserved for the telling of this sort of thing. "A gale from the south-west, three days of it, and a ketch out of Bideford, the *Hopewell*, with a cargo of Spanish wine and a captain who did not know the coast. A pilot went out to her from Sennen in his own boat in the dark. Jory Carne. Forty years old, and a wife in the cove, and the finest pilot between the Lizard and the Bristol Channel. He took the *Hopewell* in towards the Longships to bring her round the Land's End, and she struck."

The rain went *tink, tink, plonk.*

"The Longships is a reef, a mile off the Land's End. They say a man can drown there with his dinner in sight. Carne drowned. So did the captain, and so did the wine. Three days afterwards the sea gave back the pilot. He had a pouch of oiled wool strapped inside his shirt, and in the pouch, dry, because he had greased the covers with tallow, was his book." She said the next as if reciting a lesson learned at a knee. "*From Cape Cornwall to the Longships, two leagues and a half, keeping the Brisons well open to the west.* My father made me learn the first line when I was six. The widow sold it to Sir Ambrose for a winter's coal and a pair of boots. The Penhallows have kept it from that day to this."

She looked at me. "I should like to know who has had their hands on it."

"Who has handled it, since Christmas?" I asked. "Apart from Aubrey."

"The boy with the clipboard, Kit. A man from the record office who took photographs and left a bill. Aubrey himself, three times. Sheila Bray dusts the shelves but I've told her she is not to touch that one, and she hasn't, she's not that sort." The aid whistled, a small one. Loveday ignored it. "And Morwenna."

"I read to her," said Morwenna. "Tuesdays. Eleven years. Don't I, Loveday? I've read you the whole of Gibbon twice, and Trollope, and half the Bible, and the minutes of the Rural District Council."

"She is the only one of you I trust with the shelves." Loveday jabbed her stick towards the third bay. "Fetch Pellow the Hakluyt. The one with the map. Mind the bucket."

I watched her go. I would like to say I watched for any reason at all, but it was a binder's habit and nothing else. Morwenna crossed the library, stepped round the bucket, and stopped at the shelf. She did what nine readers in ten do not. She pushed the neighbouring books back on either side, took the spine in the middle of her palm, and eased the volume out without once hooking a finger over the head-cap, which is how every spine in England gets its first wound. She carried it to the table on both hands, laid it on the cushion Loveday keeps for the purpose, and opened it with a hand under each board, so that the joints took no strain at all.

It was a first edition of Hakluyt, in limp vellum, the size of a paving slab. I have held perhaps two books of that age in my life. She laid it before me as one sets a plate before a guest, and stood back with her hands folded, and said, "Mind the map. It's a fold-out."

Well, I thought. Eleven years of Tuesdays. A woman picks up good habits from a shelf the way she picks up burrs from a hedge.

* * *

I asked for the lavatory at ten to four, and was sent down the back passage, past the boot room, and found it exactly where the Penhallows have kept it since the Restoration, behind a door with a china handle.

Sheila Bray was in the passage with her mop and her bucket. She stood up straight when she saw me, as if I might be an inspector.

"Mrs Bray. A word about Tamsin."

The mop stopped. "She's been up the Bindery, I hear."

"She has been sleeping at the Bindery," I said. "In the net loft, since the middle of June."

"In that army bag from the Co-op." Sheila Bray took the mop out of the bucket, wrung it with two quick turns, and put it back. "I'm not daft, Mrs Pellow. I wash her jumpers. They smell of tar and cat."

"You never said."

"She never said. If I'd said, she'd have stopped, and gone somewhere I couldn't see." She looked at me then, a flat grey look that went all the way through. "She has hands, that girl, and nothing to do with them. Will you have her?"

"I can't pay her."

"She's not asking to be paid. She's asking for a bench. I can't give her one. I've five in two rooms and a dog."

"Then she may come in the daytime, and say she's working for me. It's no more than she's been doing."

"And nights?"

"The stairs are bad."

"They're not so bad she can't step over the third," said Sheila Bray. "Don't tell her I know. She'd stop." The mop went round a tile. "And Mrs Pellow. She's a good girl. She's not a sneak. If she's seen anything she shouldn't, it'll be because the thing was there to be seen."

I looked at her. She looked at the floor. In a village you learn that a woman who has been told nothing for twenty years has learned to say a good deal in very few words, and that the polite response to these is to hear them as spoken.

The size-twelve boots stood on their newspaper by the door as I went back. I did not ask about them. It seemed to me that the mop had already answered that.

* * *

When I returned to the library Morwenna had the Hakluyt back on its shelf, and Loveday had her chin on her stick, and the buckets were still keeping time.

"We have settled the tent," said Loveday. "The Institute may have my long tables and the big urn for the regatta, and a boy to carry them, and the proceeds to the lifeboat. I should like it recorded that I gave in handsomely." She reached for her hearing aid and settled it. "And Pellow. If that raincoat of a man should ask you, as he asks everything, Morwenna sat up with me until midnight last Thursday. The night it happened. We played piquet."

"Nobody has asked me."

"They will."

"She cheats, you know," said Morwenna, brightly, picking up her tin. "She counts her tricks twice."

"I do not."

The aid gave a squeal that set the buckets rocking. Loveday closed her hand over it. Morwenna laughed, and I laughed, because it is impossible not to when a woman of seventy-four is being shrieked at by her own ear, and the laugh went round the library and was lost among the shelf-gaps.

I took it, I think, as one takes a stranger's umbrella: as something offered, which I had no particular reason to refuse.

We said our goodbyes in the passage. Loveday did not come to the door. Sheila Bray opened it, and stood aside, and did not look at me, and the rain came in. It was four by my watch as I pulled on my mac among the others. Behind us, in the hall, the grandfather clock cleared its throat, thought about it, and struck four, eleven minutes late, and nobody in the house seemed to mind.
