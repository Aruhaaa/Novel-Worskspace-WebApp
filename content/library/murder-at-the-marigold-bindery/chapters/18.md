# Kit's Mistake

Ned taught Kit to sew in the summer of 1965, when Kit was ten and had to sit on a hassock and the family Bible to reach the frame.

The hassock was St Petroc's. Ned had borrowed it in the spring to mend the pulpit Bible and never taken it back, and the then vicar mourned it to the end of his days. It said PRAY FOR THE NAVY in wool. Kit sat on it with his knees up and his tongue out of the corner of his mouth, and Ned sat opposite, which meant doing everything backwards, since Ned was left-handed and the boy was not. He never once said it was wrong. He said, "Again, but slower," and at the end of the afternoon he said that Kit had a good needle in him, and Kit went red from the collar to the hair. I was thirty-four, at the window with a basket of mending, and I remember thinking that I had never seen two people so quietly pleased.

I thought of it that Friday afternoon, because Kit was standing in the middle of the shop with his hands hanging at his sides, and he looked, for the second time in my life, like a boy waiting to be told that he had done it wrong.

It was ten past three. I had put a pan of paste on the gas ring in the corner for something to do with my hands, and the smell of clove stood in the room like a guest. Tamsin was at the Co-op for sugar, and I blessed the sugar.

"Sorry," said Kit. "Auntie Dot. Sorry. I've got to tell you something. I've been trying since Friday week, and every time I got near it something fell off a shelf."

I took the spoon out of the paste, and wiped it on the rim, and said, "Sit."

He sat on the stool at the counter, the one customers never use. He put his hands between his knees.

"The Wednesday," he said. "Before. I was doing the Carne for the valuation, the collation, you know, you count the leaves and hold each one up to the window. And the chain lines were wrong. Too close, for a book that age. I thought it was a binder's blank at first, flyleaf stock, you get it. Then I turned a leaf and there was a lion in a fence."

"And you took it to Aubrey."

"I took it to Mr Trevithick. He went very quiet. He always called me Christopher. He said, 'Christopher, you will say nothing about this to anyone. A man can be mistaken, and if I am not mistaken it is a good deal worse. I shall deal with it.' And I said yes. I said, Yes, Mr Trevithick, because, you know what he was like, and because I thought he'd put it right. He always did." His thumb went along the edge of the counter, the left one, the nail bitten to the quick. "And he's dead. And nobody knows I knew."

"He wrote to the solicitor that same night," I said. "I've read the letter. It's dated the twelfth."

His head came up, quite white. "Did he?"

"He did."

"Then he believed me." He said it as if it were the first thing anyone had given him in a week. Then his face did something I preferred not to watch, and he looked at the pan.

"You did what your employer told you," I said. "It is a thing most people are punished for not doing."

"That's not the bit." He drew a breath through his teeth. "Sorry. The Thursday. I came here, Auntie Dot. At six."

I stirred the paste. It is a thing the hands do.

"I'd borrowed Ned's Cockerell, for the binding notes. I thought I'd put it back before the quiz. The door was on the latch and Mr Trevithick was at the bench with the lamp on and the rutter open in front of him. And he was happy. You'd have said so. Like a man who's found his glasses. He said I'd done him a great service and he'd say so in writing. I put the book on the shelf and I put my hand on the bench for something to lean on, because I thought if I stood there he would ask me to say about the paper again, out loud, and I didn't want to. I moved the lamp. It was in my eyes. And I went out, and I went to the Lantern and sat next to you till closing, and I never said."

"Did you tell Demelza?"

"She asked me flat, the Friday, in the lane, whether I'd been in the press room. And I said not since Easter last year." He looked at his hands. "I don't know why. My mouth said it. Sorry."

"You told a lie to a police officer who used to take you to the pictures."

"Used to," said Kit.

I put the spoon down. I went to the paste shelf, past the ring and the cat, took down Ned's toffee tin and opened it, and under four bone folders and a seam of cedar dust I found the corner of burned paper. I had meant to do this the other way round, to tell him first what I knew and let him tell me what he knew, and I had let him go first, and it was too late to be tactful. I put the scrap into his hand with the loupe.

"There is something I should have said on Monday."

He read it twice through the glass. *K. – cat. 114 –.* And lower down, in the silver of the pencil, *Mrs P. in the mor.* I watched the colour go out of him by stages, the way it goes out of a sky.

"That's my number," he said. "I put 114 on it myself. I typed it on the valuation sheet. Mr Trevithick would have... K. is..." He did not finish.

"It is your number. It isn't you."

"Does it matter?"

It does not matter, I thought, to anyone with a jury. "It was in the stove," I said. "Somebody burned the pastedowns so they should not be read. I found this under the grate on Monday."

"Monday." He looked up. "You've had it since Monday. And Demelza?"

"She saw it that morning. She said she would come with a bag when she knew what she was going to say, and to whom."

"She didn't bag it." He said it flatly, to the scrap. "She hid it from Garrow."

"She postponed it."

"It's the same thing in a uniform, Auntie Dot." He put the paper down on the counter as one puts down something hot. "If he finds that out, he'll have her. You can't give it to him. You'd be giving him her."

I looked at him. He had been caught in a lie, and he had a charred scrap of paper in front of him with his own initial on it, and the first thing he had thought of was the woman who had thrown him over. "You might consider yourself for ten seconds," I said. "I should take it as a favour."

"Sorry," said Kit, and very nearly smiled.

I put the scrap in my cardigan pocket, where it joined a pencil, a loupe and a ball of string. The cardigan was by now the most heavily incriminated garment in Cornwall.

The bell went, and nobody pretended it was a customer.

Garrow came in first, in the raincoat, with a folded sheet of foolscap in his hand. Pascoe came behind him, and a uniformed constable I did not know with a face like a shut gate, and behind them in the doorway, in her helmet, with the notebook held against her chest in both hands, stood Demelza. The lane at her back was full of the Friday light, and Mrs Bolitho's geraniums, and, a decent distance down, a white Ford the colour of a bandage with two more men in it.

"Mrs Pellow, dear," said Garrow. "Mr Pellow. I'm obliged to you both for staying put." He unfolded the foolscap and cleared his throat and read aloud in the voice of a man giving the minutes of a meeting about drains.

"*Whereas it has been made to appear on oath before the justices sitting at Truro this afternoon that there are reasonable grounds for believing that evidence relating to the death of Aubrey Charles Trevithick, on or about the thirteenth day of July, is to be found on the premises known as the Marigold Bindery, Fish Cellar Lane, Trevenna, comprising the shop, the press room and the net loft...*"

I went on stirring. The paste had begun to catch at the bottom. I could not stop and I did not want to; the smell of clove came up out of the pan, warm and sweet, Christmas in a shop in July where a man was being read a warrant. The pots stood in their row along the counter, and I watched Garrow's mouth over the top of them. I heard "seize and remove," and "the person of Christopher John Pellow." I thought of the girl who would be coming up the lane with a bag of sugar, and blessed the sugar again.

"Christopher Pellow," said Garrow, folding the paper, "I am arresting you on suspicion of the murder of Aubrey Trevithick. You do not have to say anything unless you wish to do so, but what you say may be given in evidence."

Kit stood up. He did it carefully, as if the floor might not take his weight. He said "Sorry" to the boards, in the voice of a boy of ten with a needle through his thumb.

"Your left thumb, Mr Pellow," said Garrow, "is on the underside of the lamp that stood over the dead man's work. You told Constable Kitto on the fourteenth of July that you had not set foot in that room since Easter last year. One of those statements is untrue, and the lamp can't tell lies."

"I was going to say. I came here to say it to my aunt first."

"I'm sure you did."

"He did," I said. "He has been in this shop since ten past three. I can give you the time to the minute."

"I'm sure you can, Mrs Pellow, dear. You may give it to me again at Truro." Garrow looked at the pan. "You may put that down."

"If I stop stirring it, Inspector, it will be glue."

"Then stir," said Garrow, and, to do him justice, for an instant he looked very tired.

From the press room came the sound of drawers being opened by a man who did not know which of them stuck. Kit turned at the door. He looked at nobody; he looked at the row of pots.

"Auntie Dot."

"Yes."

"Sorry. The cat. I've been giving her the good tin at six, the pilchards. She won't touch the other. She hisses at me. It's how you know she's fond."

"I shall give her the good tin."

"And not the..."

"Not the other."

"Thank you," said Kit, to the doorframe, and went out between Pascoe and the man with the shut face. I heard his shoes go down the step, one, two, as they had gone down it at ten, on errands for Ned.

Demelza stood against the jamb to let them by. She looked at the lintel. She did not look at Kit, and she did not look at me, and I had never seen her so closely occupied with a piece of wood.

"Constable," said Garrow, going past. "The second car."

"Sir."

They put Garrow in the first car, alone, and Kit in the back of the second between two men who did not know him, and Demelza in the front beside the driver with the notebook on her knee. The blue lamp stayed dark. It went down Fish Cellar Lane at the pace of a hearse, past the geraniums and the net lofts and the corner by the old pilchard cellar, and the tail-lights turned the colour of Mrs Bolitho's flowers, and went round the bend, and out.

Quire had come to the step. She sat in the last of the sun with her torn ear to the lane and her bib very white, and she watched them to the end of it, as she watches a hole in the skirting, and did not hiss. Then she turned her head and looked at me, and at the pan, and at the empty stool, and with the air of a person who has been kept waiting asked for her tin.

I found I did not know which was the good one.
