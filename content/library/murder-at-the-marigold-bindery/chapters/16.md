# Man in the Water

Nobody runs in Trevenna after the age of forty, because of the steps, and I was fifty-eight, and I ran.

There are forty-three of them in three flights, cut out of the cliff by the pilchard-women with no thought for anybody's knees, and the third flight is greener than the others and has a rope for a rail. I took the first two without noticing them. On the third I had the rope in my hand, and it burned, and I came off the bottom step on to the Hard with my glasses bouncing on their bootlace and my own breath loud in my ears.

The harbour was a black dish with the lamps of the Lantern lying broken in it. The tide had perhaps an hour and a half on it, over the mud, and the water at the foot of the quay steps stood higher than I would have guessed. At the top of the steps stood a steamer trunk with brass corners, strapped up for a journey, and on its lid a herring gull, which turned its head to look at me and did not trouble to move.

"Rope!" said a voice. "Dot. Anything."

Jago was in the water. He was at the bottom of the steps with the flood to his chest, in his shirt, his coat thrown in a heap on the stone, hauling at something pale with both hands. I had seen him lift the end of an engine. I had never seen him look small. The pale thing was a man's back, the linen jacket ballooned with air, riding face-down like a gull asleep, and it was swinging away from him on the swell and coming back, and each time it came back Jago got a little less of it.

There was a stack of lobster pots on the Hard, and across the stack lay a boat hook, the kind with a brass tip, which belonged to the *Margery Jane* moored under the wall. I took it. I went down the steps on my backside, because the weed on them was as slippery as soap, and I reached out as far as I could with my skirt in the water and caught the jacket by the collar.

"Pull," said Jago.

"I am pulling."

"Pull harder."

"I am fifty-eight."

"That's the size of it," said Jago, through his teeth, and the jacket came.

We got him in between the boat and the wall, where there was a gap of about a yard and a half, with the *Margery Jane*'s fender grinding against the granite on one side and the stone going by on the other, rough as a file. His head knocked on it twice. I heard it. Then Jago had him by the belt and the collar and went up the steps backwards like a man carrying a sack of coal out of a flood, and we laid him on the stones of the Hard among the lobster pots, which smelled of old bait and rope, and Jago sat down heavily where he was and let his arms hang.

I knelt. In 1943 I learned artificial respiration from Miss Treffry of the Third Plymouth Guides, who taught it with a sort of fury, kneeling astride a volunteer in the church hall and leaning like a woman rolling pastry. In 1972 Ned and I had spent six Tuesday evenings in the Methodist schoolroom with a man from St John Ambulance and a rubber doll called Annie, a course Ned attended in a rage of embarrassment with his cap on his knee. I remembered both. I could not remember which of them was supposed to be better, so I did the sensible thing and did what Annie had taught.

He was not breathing. I turned him on to his side and water ran out of him in a quantity I would not have credited, and then I turned him back, and tilted his head, and put my mouth over his, which tasted of harbour and Polo mints, and breathed, and pressed with the heel of my hand, and counted. I counted in the voice of Miss Treffry. I was aware of people arriving: the scrape of boots on the Hard, Kit's torch shaking, Tamsin with a blanket from the Lantern's settle, Wilf Hosken saying "Lord" over and over in a conversational tone, and under it all a thin continuous murmur which I took to be the Reverend Nance saying the Collect.

"Right," said a voice above me. "Facts."

Demelza came down the last flight at a dead run with her helmet in her hand, and went on her knees on the other side of him. She must have been halfway up Net Loft Row.

"Four minutes," I said. "There was a pulse, I think. Thin as a thread."

"Is there now?" Two fingers went under his jaw. "Right. Go on, Dot, I've got him. Kit! The Lantern, nine-nine-nine, ambulance and the Inspector. Trevenna Hard, the quay steps. Run."

Kit ran. I let her take over. I had the strange sense of having been relieved from a watch, and I sat back on my heels, and my arms began to shake.

He coughed. It was an ugly sound, as though something were being dragged up out of a drain, and it was followed by a second and a third and by a long ragged pull of air, and Demelza turned him on to his side and held him there with her knee against his back. He did not wake. His eyes were half open and showed only white. There was a cut behind his right ear, welling thinly, and I looked at it and thought: the boat, or the granite. Or. I am not a doctor, and I said so, aloud, to nobody, twice.

The ambulance takes forty minutes from Truro. It is a statement about Cornwall as much as about the ambulance. We spent them on the Hard, in the lamplight, among the lobster pots: Demelza with two fingers at the Dutchman's throat and her face quite empty, which is what her face does when she is working; Tamsin and Kit holding a blanket over him like a roof; Nance on his knees on the wet stones with his bicycle clips on, still murmuring; and the tide climbing the quay steps a stair at a time, behind our backs, so that I could count the hours in the weed lines, each one a darker green and a shade more wet.

Jago sat on a lobster pot with his shirt clinging to him and the sea running out of his trousers and passed his hands over his head again and again, as though to check that it was still there. He shook with a big slow shaking like a boat on a mooring. I put the second blanket round him.

"You were quick," I said.

"I was close."

"How close?"

He looked at the water.

"Where were you going, Jago?" Demelza did not look up from the Dutchman's throat. She said it as she might have said the time.

"Walking."

"The Lantern's at the top of the Steps. Your door's behind you. You were at the bottom, on the quay side, with your coat on." She nodded at the heap on the stone. "You didn't take it off to come out for the air."

"I heard a man go in," said Jago, "and I went in after him. That's what I've got. That's all I've got."

And he shut his mouth. I knew that mouth. It was the mouth that had wiped one tumbler for a full minute on the Friday night and said, *Leave it, Dot.* He sat there steaming in the lamplight with his head down, and the only sound he made was the sound of a very large man trying to breathe quietly.

"Dot," said Demelza. "His pockets. Anything that says what he's taken, or who to ring."

I did it because I was told. He was on his side with his sodden jacket rucked round his ribs, and I went through it as one goes through the pockets of a child's coat. A hotel key on a brass tag, *The Drowned Lantern, 2*. A leather wallet, which I put into Demelza's hand without opening. A handkerchief like a wet rag. And in the inside breast pocket a Dutch paperback, swollen to twice its size, the cover showing a woman looking over her shoulder at a house, the pages stuck together like a slice of cake. As I drew it out something slid from between the cover and the first leaf, where the board had held it dry, and fell on my knee.

It was a piece of paper folded in four. It was soft as a handkerchief, and it weighed what rag weighs. A binder knows old paper by the thumb before the eyes have caught up, and my thumb knew it, and it was dry at the middle and cool at the edges, and the fold had that faint crackle at the crease that only rag will give.

I did not decide to. I want that understood. My hand did it, the way it squares a pile of paper, and when I understood what it had done the leaf was in my cardigan pocket with the Jam Tarts' sheet and a lemon sherbet, and Demelza was bent over the Dutchman with her back to me, and nobody on the Hard was looking in my direction. It is the one advantage of being fifty-eight and in a cardigan. I have never in my life been looked at in an emergency.

Headlights came down the lane at twenty to twelve, two pairs, a long way apart. The ambulance came first, its light turning blue on the wet of the Hard, and two big calm men got out of it and did everything without speaking, and took the Dutchman away in a red blanket on a stretcher, with Demelza walking beside it with her hand on the rail. Behind it, at his own pace, came the Inspector's Rover. Garrow got out in the raincoat bound in rexine and looked at the wet stone, the trunk, the lobster pots, the gull (which at last had gone), and Jago Rowe in a blanket. He took a long time about it.

"Mrs Pellow, dear," he said. "Again."

"Inspector."

"Mr Rowe. You're wet."

"I am."

"I should like to know why."

"I pulled him out."

"And the rest of it?"

Jago said nothing. It was his entire statement. Garrow looked at him with the face of a man being shown a locked door, and then at me, and I put my hands in my cardigan pockets, and one of them closed on a folded leaf of paper, and I looked at the harbour with my most innocent expression, which is the one I use for customers who ask if I do gold-tooling.

I got up the first flight and the second. On the third, where the rope is, my knees went all at once, as though someone had cut a thread, and I sat down on a step. It was cold and wet and I found I was very interested in the weed on it. A hand came under my elbow. It was a thin hand, bony, with a gentle strength in it, and I thought absurdly of a stranger offering me his seat on a bus. I looked up. It was Nance. His bicycle clips were still on.

"Forgive me," he said. "I am sure it is nothing."

We went up the lane at the pace of the very old. He wheeled his bicycle in one hand, which he had fetched from the wall of the Lantern without being asked, and held my elbow with the other, and said not one word, which I had not known he could do. At my gate he waited until I had found my key.

"Good night, Mrs Pellow."

"Good night, Mr Nance. Thank you."

"Not at all," said Nance. "I am sure it is nothing." And he went off down the hill with the bicycle ticking beside him.

Quire was on the kitchen table, offended by the hour. I put on the forty-watt bulb and took the leaf out of my cardigan and unfolded it under the light.

It was damp at the edges, where the paperback had held the water out like a man holding a door, and dry at the middle. Laid paper, rag, with the chain lines as wide apart as a woman's finger. Brown ink gone the colour of weak tea in the thin strokes, in a careful upright hand with the long *s*, the hand of a man writing with his tongue between his teeth. I laid it between two sheets of blotting paper from the dresser drawer and put Ned's Cruden's Concordance on top, a book which has been of use to me exactly once, and then, because I could not help it, I lifted the corner.

At the head of the page, ruled off with a line that had taken some trouble, was a heading, put there so that a man could find his place in the dark. *Cape Cornwall to the Longships.* The leaf had been folded in four. The creases were soft with handling, and none of them was mine.
