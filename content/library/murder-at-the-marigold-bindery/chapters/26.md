# A Second Dot

It rained the whole of the last Sunday in July, the steady, well-meaning rain that Trevenna keeps for days when it would like to be left alone, and by ten o'clock the Bindery smelled of clove from the shop door to the yard.

I had been up since five with the paste. The seals had come off on the Monday, peeled away with a razor blade by DC Pascoe, who apologised to the door and then to me. Inspector Garrow had telephoned on the Tuesday and called me Mrs Pellow, without the dear, and I had not known where to put the receiver. Morwenna had made a statement of eleven pages, signing every one, and Garrow said it was the first he had ever read that did not want its commas moved. She was to be tried at Truro in the autumn. Kit had come home on the Saturday night in the back of the same white Ford, with his bootlaces in a paper bag, and slept fourteen hours on my settle with Quire sitting on his chest and hissing at him, which is how you know she is fond.

Imogen had buried her father on the Monday, with Mr Nance giving the hymn and half the Lantern in the churchyard wet to the knee, and at the lychgate I put the swan ring into her hand. She tried it on her thumb, where it turned, and said "Accurate," and nearly smiled. Mr Nance had since had a letter of three lines from her, selling him back the Psalter for the nine hundred pounds Aubrey paid, to be settled when the roof was mended; he showed it to me in the street and apologised for weeping. The Institute met on the Wednesday and voted, with Doris Rosevear abstaining and Gwen Trewhella shouting that she had been in favour for the last ten minutes, to keep Ronald Hext at Gwithian House out of the bunting fund until Christmas. So it turned out that the position could be reversed after all, though it took seven women and an urn.

And I was seen. I do not know how else to put it. A woman I had not spoken to since 1974 crossed the Hard to tell me she had always thought it was Morwenna. People held doors. Ada Bolitho brought in the hinge of the otter's glass case and stayed an hour, and the till took a hundred and forty-three pounds twenty in the week, which is a good deal for a bindery whose door was sealed for three days of it. It is disconcerting, at fifty-eight, to be looked at. I find that I do not entirely hate it.

On the bench, between blotters, lay twenty-four leaves of the Carne rutter: the four that had sat in the false book and the twenty that had come out of the Regatta Book. They had dried flat in a week under Ned's Cruden's Concordance and the Chapel hymnal, and Tamsin counted them every morning at eight, as one checks a baby.

"Twenty-four," she said. "Still."

"Counting them won't breed them."

"It might."

At a quarter past ten the shop bell went, and Pieter de Vries stood on the mat and dripped. The white linen jacket had gone the colour of wet paper. There was a square of plaster behind his right ear, and the round glasses had been mended at one hinge with a twist of wire.

"Mevrouw Pellow." He held a Delft-blue biscuit tin out in front of him in both hands, like a man returning a baby he had found on a train. "I have brought what I should not have bought."

He set the tin on the lying press and took off the lid. Forty leaves of brown-ink rag lay in it, interleaved with tissue. "My assistant carried them from Rotterdam on his knees," said Pieter. "He tells me he has not been so frightened since his driving test. Your police have counted them against the list of Mr Pellow and photographed every one, and returned them with an expression."

"You paid for them," I said.

"I paid. I shall not ask for the money. It would be vulgar to write to a lady in her position." The glasses came round to look at me. "I did not ask where they came from. This is a fault in my profession. I find, lying in a hospital, that it is the whole of my profession."

It was the longest speech I had heard from him, and he looked relieved to have got to the end of it. Then he took from his pocket a flat parcel in tissue and gave it to me with a small stiff bow, and in the tissue was a bone folder, long and pale, one end shaped to a point and the other cut with a notch for the thumb.

"Dutch," he said. "It was my grandfather's, from Delft. It is not valuable. It is only good."

I tried the edge along my thumbnail, and it was. "Thank you," I said. We regarded one another, two people each braced for the other to say more, and then he bowed again, rather deeply, and spoiled it by dripping on the floor.

"Does your head hurt?"

"In the wet weather. It is the head that remembers." He looked at the pots on the shelf, the stove, the cat asleep on the cutting board. "Very pleasant, this," said Pieter, and I think he meant the whole room.

Tamsin had already tipped the tin. She laid the leaves beside the others in rows of eight along the bench, her lips moving, and when she reached the end her face was the face of someone who has put the last piece in a puzzle.

"Sixty-four," she said. "Sixty-four."

"Well," I said.

Demelza came at half past ten, out of uniform. She wore the grey jumper with the darned cuff and nothing over it, which was the first time I had seen her in a thing she was not hiding, and she carried a carrier bag from the Co-op.

"Right. Facts," she said, and took out Ned's green notebook. It was scuffed at one corner, and the exhibit label had been peeled off and left a pale square. "Photographed, every page. Garrow signed it out himself. He said to give it to the lady of the house, and then he heard himself, and he went the colour of a boiled ham."

I put it on the paste shelf between the toffee tin and the space where the swan ring had been, and did not open it. I mean to read it one wet Sunday, in order. Morwenna said I only think it is a bad hand, and I find I want to know.

"He's put it in his report that Constable Kitto made the arrest," Demelza said to the shelf. "In writing. I've read it twice."

"Well deserved."

"I said some words on a slipway, Dot. You did the rest."

The bell went again.

Kit stood in the doorway with the rain running down his neck, not coming in, in the way people stand at the edge of a pond. Demelza, who had turned to go, stopped on the mat, and I discovered a sudden need to rule a line on a sheet of waste paper at the far end of the press room, from where I could see everything.

"Sorry," said Kit.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say sorry." He looked at his bitten thumbnail. "I'm sorry I said it."

"Kit."

"Sorry for saying that as well."

"Thursday," said Demelza. "Seven. Mine." She had not moved, and her plait hung down her back the colour of wet rope. "It will be dreadful. I can't do mince. Bring nothing, you'll only apologise for it."

Kit opened his mouth, shut it, and nodded with such force that water flew from his hair. "Right," he said, in a voice I had last heard from him at ten years old on a St Petroc's hassock, the day his first kettle stitch lay down flat. Demelza went past him into the lane. At the corner she looked back once, and I looked at my paper with great attention and ruled a line I shall never be able to use.

"Sorry, Auntie Dot," said Kit. "Did you—"

"I saw nothing," I said. "I was ruling."

By eleven the rain had slackened, and a mud-coloured Land Rover stopped at the foot of the lane. From the shop window I watched Jago Rowe come round to the passenger side and hand Loveday Penhallow down into the wet, in her gumboots, which were on the right feet. She came up Fish Cellar Lane on his arm, in daylight, in full view of Net Loft Row, and she did it slowly, so that nobody should miss it.

"Pellow. Don't fuss." She stood on the mat and surveyed the bench, thumping her blackthorn on the boards. Her hearing aid, I noticed, said nothing at all; I put it down to the truth, or the battery. "I have come to say three things and Rowe has come to say a fourth, and we shall do it standing, because if I sit I shall not get up. One. The library goes. All of it, damp and all, at Truro on the twenty-third of September, with the buckets in the catalogue photographs. If it is to be said that Penhallow kept a thief in its library, it may as well be said that Penhallow keeps a roof out of it, and the one can pay for the other. In the open, off Kitto's list, with the whole of Cornwall bidding. Two. The Carne stays. Plain, Pellow. Aubrey would say accurate. None of your gift-shop calf. You will bind it, with the girl, in whatever you've got, and it will sit on my table in the hall where it can be handled by people who ask. Three." Her chin went up. "Rowe drives me. In the front. In the daylight. I thought you should hear it from me, since forty others will have told you by Tuesday."

"I'm very glad," I said.

"That's the size of it," said Jago.

"Fourth," said Loveday.

He took from his coat a flat ledger bound in sailcloth, swollen with damp, its back hanging by its tapes. "The station's service book. Eighteen eighty-four to nineteen twenty-two. The Institution wrote to Wilf from Plymouth, wanting it saved before the district inspector comes in October. Wilf's honorary secretary, and Wilf says the Bindery. Thirty-one pounds. And a form."

"In triplicate."

"Quadruplicate. They've a new one." He opened the book at a slip of paper and laid his thumb, the one beside the missing finger, under a line. "It's got her in it. For the tiebreak."

I read it over his hand, in a clerk's blue-black copperplate: *Wrecked on service off the Brisons, 14 November 1910. Crew saved. Lifeboat* Ellen Penhallow.

"My great-aunt's boat," said Loveday. "She paid for it herself in 1894, and the sea took it off the Brisons in a gale, and the Institution wrote a letter of regret. I could have told you at the Lantern, if anyone had thought to ask me. Nobody asks me anything. They ask the vicar."

"You weren't in the quiz."

"I should have been," said Loveday, and the hearing aid, for the first time that day, gave a very small whistle.

She crossed to the bench and looked down at the sixty-four in their rows. She put one finger of that beautiful long hand to the edge of the first leaf, not on the writing, and read the head of it half aloud. "'Keeping the Brisons well open to the west.'" Her voice dropped. "Jory Carne." She did not finish. "Do it properly, Pellow."

"I will."

They went down the lane as they had come, on his arm, slowly. By noon the rain had thinned to nothing.

By two the Bindery held nobody but us, and the sun had come in at the skylight at last and laid a long yellow oblong over the cutting board, which is scarred like a map with every job of thirty years. Tamsin and I sewed the Carne.

We sewed it into its own cover, the 1853 calf with Ned's spine of 1971, scraped clean of the day-book and of Morwenna's wheat paste and her clever hinge. Sixty-four leaves, eight gatherings, five cords on Ned's frame; the other frame still sat under a tarpaulin in a honey-house with a police label on it, and I do not yet know whether I want it back. I turned my kettle stitches to the right, as I always have, so that anyone who opened the book in a hundred years could tell, as one tells a signature, whose it was.

Tamsin pasted at my side, with Ned's paste and the face of a person defusing something. About four o'clock she did her first corner, on the mend to the back board, where a piece of the old calf had lifted. It came up a small, round, lumpy fist of a thing with a pleat in it. I looked at it for as long as it took to see that it would hold, and said nothing, which is the hardest thing I teach.

"It's awful," said Tamsin.

"It'll hold."

"That isn't the same."

"No," I said. "It's the first one."

When the last gathering was sewn I turned it to its foot, and found Ned's mark where I had found it a fortnight before: a pin-prick so small that a lamp will lose it. Morwenna had left it, she said, for somebody who knew. I took the awl from the drawer. I laid its point a quarter-inch to the right of his, because I am right-handed and he was not, which is the only fact about the two of us that anyone could verify, and I leaned until I felt the paper give.

"What's that?" said Tamsin.

"A dot. So that anyone who takes the book apart in a hundred years will know somebody was here."

"Is it a secret?"

"It was Ned's. He never told me; he told a pupil. This one's mine." I laid the awl down. "You'll have to decide about yours."

Tamsin looked at the two marks for some time, and I saw the thought go across her face and get put in a drawer for later, which is where I keep my own best ones.

Something thumped onto her knees. Quire turned round twice, tucked in her paws, and settled with her bib to the skylight and her torn ear towards the room. Tamsin held her hands up in the air as if she had been told to.

"She's on me," she whispered. "She bites everyone."

"She has sat on two people in her life that I know of," I said. "One is awaiting trial at Truro. The other has put out the good tin at six every morning since the Friday. I do not say Quire is a poor judge of character. I say she is a first-rate judge of tins."

She had settled the way she had settled once before in another lap, and I found that I could look at it.

The police had returned the backing hammer on the Thursday, in a paper bag labelled in biro, and it had lain on the bench ever since, because I had not been able to decide which nail it belonged on. When the sun had gone off the cutting board I took it out of the bag and carried it to the wall. There are two nails there. There always were: Ned's on the left, mine on the right, and for thirty years we had each hung it as our hands thought best and never remarked on it. I had not known until this summer that a nail could say so much.

I hung it on mine, face to the wall, and let go. It swung a quarter-inch on its nail and was still.

"Tomorrow it's yours to hang," I said. "Mine goes to the wall and Ned's went out, and you can have either. Or you can put up a third, and hang it whichever way your own hand does it, every night the same, and be done with the pair of us."

Tamsin looked at the wall, and the two nails, and the clean space between them where there was plenty of room.

"Right," she said. "So. Where do you keep the nails?"

I showed her.
