# The Wrong Nail

The door was locked, which was right, and the cat was not in the window, which was wrong, and I stood on the step of the Marigold Bindery at ten to seven on a Friday morning with a bag of buns going soft in my hand, trying to decide which of the two to be cross about.

I had come up from Hocking's, where Elsie Hocking sold me six currant buns without lifting her eyes from the till. "Fine morning, Mrs Pellow." "It is." "Tide's making." "So I see." That was the whole of it, and I do not hold it against her. At fifty-eight, in a grey cardigan, with my hair cut by my own hand with the kitchen scissors, I am a kind of weather in Trevenna. People are glad of me and could not tell you what colour I was.

Fish Cellar Lane climbs from the harbour like something that has been asked to and means to make a point of it. The lanes here are named for what was once done in them: Net Loft Row, Pilchard Steps, and mine for the cellars where the catch was salted down in the days when there was a catch. Nobody has salted anything there since 1920, though on a warm morning the granite still remembers. The hydrangeas were out in their tubs, blue as bruises. There was a gull on every chimney, one to a pot, like clergy. Behind me the tide was half way up the Hard and the first boats had begun to lift on it.

At the top, where the lane gives up and turns into a track to my cottage twenty yards on, stands the Bindery: shop at the front, press room behind, and over both a net loft that nobody has hauled a net into since Ned's father's day, and which I use for millboard and the sort of paper one cannot bring oneself to throw away. You reach it by granite steps up the outside wall. The third one creaks. The door is yellow because Ned's mother loved marigolds, and when Ned married me he named the place for the door, which is the sort of romance I was brought up to trust.

That Friday the Trevenna Antiquarian Book Fair was to open in St Petroc's hall at noon, with Aubrey Trevithick, chairman of everything, cutting a ribbon. I had agreed, for thirty pounds and the novelty of being asked, to sit at a trestle there with a pot of paste and mend loose hinges at fifty pence apiece, beneath a card in his own firm hand: MRS PELLOW OF THE MARIGOLD BINDERY, REPAIRS WHILE YOU BROWSE. Hence the buns. Paste wants cooking at first light so that it has cooled by ten, and Ned's rule, which I have never had the nerve to break, was that the first bun of the day is eaten standing beside the pan.

I had two keys to my own door. One was in my cardigan pocket. The other, a brass Yale on a loop of green wool, had been in Aubrey Trevithick's waistcoat since a quarter to six the evening before, and I had half expected to find it on the mat with a note of thanks in violet ink. There was nothing on the mat. I let myself in with mine.

The shop was cool and smelled as it always does, of leather and size and the ghost of Ned's pipe, which three years and two coats of whitewash have not shifted. No cat on the sill. I said "Quire?" to the room and the room declined. The door to the press room, which I keep shut against the draught from the lane, stood open the width of a hand, and the air that came through the gap was warm.

I pushed it with two fingers.

The first thing I saw was the cane, ebony with a silver collar, lying across the boards like a rule dropped on a drawing. Then the shoes, good brown brogues, side by side, as though he had stepped out of them into his own length. He lay on his side between the nipping press and the bench with one arm under him, the silver moustache combed, the summer tweeds buttoned for the last time by himself. His Panama had come off. It lay a foot from his hand, crown down, like a bowl set out for coins. The back of his head was dark, and I looked at that for as long as it takes to understand something, and then I looked at the hat instead.

"Mr Trevithick," I said, which was foolish. Then, "Aubrey," which I had not once called him in twenty-seven years, and neither of us seemed to mind.

I have seen how a body looks when whatever ran it has gone. It is not a thing one mistakes, and it was not a thing I could improve by fetching anyone. I put out a hand to steady myself and found the stove. It stands against the back wall, an iron thing the size of a small cow, and I light it for paste and for nothing else; I had let it die on Wednesday after the last pot. The iron was warm under my palm. Not hot. The warmth of a flank. In July, with the skylight shut and no fire of mine in it for two days, I stood with my hand flat against it like a woman feeling a child for fever, and could think of nothing sensible to say to it.

On the bench under the skylight lay the book. I knew it before I had decided to look, as you know a neighbour's back in a queue: the Carne Rutter, Loveday Penhallow's pilot's book, which Ned had mended in the winter of 1971 while I made tea and was told not to breathe. It lay open and wrong. The back board had been lifted away and the endpapers were gone, torn out so hard that the board showed raw where they had been, like the inside of a loaf. The gatherings had been half unpicked from their cover, and the thread still hung from them in long brown lengths, trailing off the edge of the bench like the roots of something pulled up in a hurry.

I touched nothing. I would like that on the record, along with the stove. I looked, which costs nothing and in my experience is the part most people skip. The back door to the yard was bolted on the inside, as I bolt it. The window to the lane was latched. The skylight was shut. On my way to the telephone I looked at the lock of the yellow door and found nothing in it, and no green wool on the counter, none on the bench, none on the floor that I could see without bending. A locked door, a dead man behind it, and no key anywhere.

The telephone is a black Bakelite thing on the shop counter that Ned bought second-hand in 1963 and that rings like a person clearing his throat. I dialled the three nines with a finger in the little holes, waiting each time for the dial to come home.

"Emergency. Which service?"

"The police, please. And I suppose an ambulance, although I don't believe he'll be wanting it."

There was a pause with a click in it, and a second voice, a man's, asked where, and I told him. "Trevenna?" he said. "That'll be Kitto," in the tone of someone glad of a shortcut. The line went away and came back, and at the other end of it somebody was swallowing hard.

"Kitto."

"It's Dot Pellow, Demelza. I'm at the Bindery."

"Mrs Pellow." Another swallow. "Sorry. Toast. Go on."

"There is a man dead on my press-room floor."

"Who?"

"Aubrey Trevithick."

There was a silence with a crumb in it. When she spoke again the toast had gone out of her voice. "Right. Facts. Are you certain he's dead?"

"I'm fifty-eight, Demelza. I'm certain."

"Anyone else there?"

"Only me. And the cat, somewhere."

"Have you touched anything?"

"The stove. I leant on it."

"Right. Don't touch anything else. Stay in the shop, let nobody through, and shut that door if it's open. Four minutes."

I gave her seven, allowing for the hill and the toast. From behind the paper rack Quire came out, torn ear first, and hissed at me. It is her usual greeting and I found it, that morning, very nearly a comfort. She went up onto the windowsill and sat with her back to the press room and her white bib to the street, and would not look round, which I put down to temper. I filled the kettle. I mention this because I am told it is the sort of thing that goes in a statement, and because it was the only thing in the building I could think of to do.

Then I went back and stood in the press-room doorway, because I had been told to shut the door and I have been told a good many things in my life by people who had not seen the room. It was while I stood there that I noticed the hammer.

There are two nails in the wall by the lying press, a hand's breadth apart. The left one was Ned's. It had held nothing since March of 1986, and I would not have let anyone hang a coat on it. The right one is mine, which Ned drove in the first winter I was married so that I need not reach across him, and on mine, face to the wall, by the leather loop through the end of its shaft, the backing hammer has hung every working day for three years. I could find it blind. This morning it was not on mine. It was on Ned's, and it had been hung face-out, the round steel of it turned toward the room and looking at me, the way he always hung it and the way I never have.

I thought: someone has been in my room and touched my hammer. I did not think the other thing, because nobody does, not at once. One takes it for a strangeness, like a picture hung crooked in a house where one has lived a long time. I stood and looked at it until the shape of it began to seem like a word I had written too often.

What I thought about, in the doorway of my own press room with a dead man and a kettle, was the evening before.

He had come into the shop at a quarter to six, as I was thinking of turning the sign, leaning on the cane because of his hip, with a flat leather satchel held to his chest as though somebody had threatened to take it. "Mrs Pellow. I require a bench, a lamp and a door that locks."

"It's a quarter to six, Mr Trevithick."

"Then I shall be brief." He was never brief in his life. He looked round the shop as he always did, as if it were a small tidy country he had thought about annexing. "There is something I wish to look at properly. I have not the light at Seagate, nor the tools, nor, I regret, the nerve."

"What sort of something?"

"Tomorrow I shall have something to say to you. I would rather be accurate than prompt, and I am not yet accurate." The swan on his signet ring tapped the head of the cane. "Accuracy is a form of courtesy."

"So is closing at six."

I gave him the spare key mainly to get him out of my shop. I would like that understood. I took it off its nail and told him I wanted it back first thing, and he said I should have it, and a word with it, and bowed, which he did to women, waiters and bishops, and I never worked out which of the three he thought I was. Then he went out in a cloud of camphor, with the satchel.

I went down to the Lantern. Quire Straits came fourth at the quiz, which I maintain was robbery and said so, at length, to anyone with an ear. I came up Fish Cellar Lane at eleven or a little after, with my cardigan buttoned one out and my mind on the fact that Elsie Hocking's husband had written Brahms for a piece that was plainly Elgar. The press-room window looks onto the lane, under the loft steps. It was dark. I saw it, in so far as you see a thing you have walked past ten thousand times; I did not turn my head, because there was nothing in it to turn for. I went on up to my cottage and my bed and slept like a woman with nothing on her conscience.

I did not think I should tell anyone that. A woman walks past a dark window: it is hardly the stuff of a statement. (I thought a great deal, afterwards, about what is and is not.)

Down in the shop the bell over the door rang, and the draught of it came through to me along the floor. I did not turn round. I was watching the hammer, which had begun to move on Ned's nail, a quarter of an inch one way and back, as though someone had only just let go of it.
