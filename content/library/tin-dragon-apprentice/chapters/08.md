# What the Warden Left Out

On the nineteenth it did not snow. It rained, which in Gantry is worse, because the soot comes down in the water and you wear the sky on your face.

I had spent three days trying to catch the Warden alone. This is harder than it sounds in a building with one door and one Warden, because a man who has spent nineteen years avoiding a single conversation has worked out most of the routes. He was at the Bell, or at the Pit, or asleep, or in the vestry with Fenwick between us like a large friendly wall. But Fenwick had told me one thing without knowing that he was telling it. "Warden takes his tea on the roof," he had said, buttering something, "in all weathers. Half past six. He'll not thank you."

The roof of the vestry is a lead flat, eleven feet by nine, reached by a ladder-stair in the passage behind the lockers and a trapdoor that sticks. In one corner stands a brick hut the size of a privy. In the hut is a stove. I learned about the stove before I learned about anything else, because when I put my head through the trapdoor at twenty-five past six the hut was belching smoke from every seam it owned, and out of the middle of it, coughing, with a tin mug in his brass hand, came the Warden of the Furnace Cathedral of Gantry, weeping like a man at a wedding.

"Boots," he said. And then, because it was a roof, in a different voice: "No. Stay on. Wipe them."

"Your damper's gone," I said.

"Go down."

"It's a Pargeter combination, forty years old if it's a day, and the damper pin's sheared, so the plate's dropped across the flue. It's choking itself. You're burning wet coal and breathing the evidence."

"Marchetti. Go. Down."

Rain ticked on the slates. I held up my oilcloth book instead, opened at the page where I had drawn two keyholes, and put it on the lead under the eave where he could see it and the rain could not. On the facing page I had written the words in block capitals, since my ordinary hand is not to be trusted.

"There are five of those inside it," I said. "I've drawn two. One in the skull, one in the tail, one in the keel in the cold place, and I haven't seen those, but they're there. They aren't for anything I can name. And on the rib beside the left one, somebody scratched that."

He read it. His face did not move. It stayed so still that I knew what it was costing him.

"Where were you instructed to go?"

"The shoulder."

"And you went to the shoulder."

"I went where the shoulder was. It's a big shoulder."

"I told you to mend a gland. I did not tell you to take a survey." He closed the book with the brass fingers, very gently, and handed it back. "Sweep the left aisle."

"I've swept it."

"Sweep it again."

"I'll sweep it after your stove."

I had the damper plate out of the flue-mouth while he was still thinking of the next order. It came out in two pieces, a long one and a short one, with a rusted stub of pin between them like a pulled tooth. I knelt in the doorway of the hut with my knees in the wet lead and went to work, and for a minute and a half the only sounds were the rain, the cough, and the small bright ticking of his brass fingers on the mug.

"I did not ask for that," he said.

"No."

"The stove has been smoking since September."

"I know. I could smell it from the nave." I cut a new pin from a six-inch nail with a hacksaw and a file; it took eight strokes. "It's no trouble. I can't do the keyholes. I can do dampers."

"You are..." He stopped. He tried again with less in the way. "The tea is on the shelf. If you want it. I made too much."

"Thank you."

He had not made too much. There was exactly one mug.

I had the plate back on its pivot and the new pin through it and a clean draught coming up, a pull I could feel on my face, when he sat down on the upturned coal-box that was the hut's one seat and said, to the stove, "Nineteen years ago."

I did not turn round. You don't, with a seized valve. You keep your hands moving and let it come.

"The third week of January. I was thirty-three. I had been Warden seven months, because the old Warden died in September and the Guilds wanted a man who understood bangs. I was a sapper. I understood that things which sleep are not safe, only quiet. Before that winter the Bell was rung on Sundays and a prayer said, and the thing slept because it slept."

"And that night?"

"That night I woke in the vestry, in my boots, because the floor was moving." He turned the mug a quarter-turn. "I had barred the nave doors with an oak beam as thick as my body. It lifted the beam as you would lift a pencil. It went out through the doors and down Ember Hill in the dark at a pace you would not believe from a thing so long, through the square, onto the towpath of the Great Cut. Two and a half miles. Every lamp along the Cut went out as it passed, one after another, like a man pinching candles."

"Did anyone try to stop it?"

"Nobody was fool enough. I ran behind it with a lantern. It paused at each bridge to look underneath." Something that was almost humour came and went. "At the end is the Tidegate, and in those days the first wall of it was Founders' stone, with the Brack standing on the other side a yard higher than the Cut. It put its head against the wall. It leaned. I heard the stone go, coping to sill. It was a sound like a very large man clearing his throat."

"And you stopped it."

"I stood on the towpath and spoke."

"What did you say?"

"What one says." The mug went round again. "That it was all right. That it should go home."

He said it the way you cross a stream on stones: quickly, and not where it was wet. I knew better than to say so. I went on easing the pin through its hole, and watched him from the side of my eye, and he looked at the mug.

"It turned. It walked back the whole way beside me, up the towpath, chin almost on the ground, and I walked at its shoulder, and when we reached the Hill it climbed into the Pit and coiled up in the same three turns as before, to the plate. At dawn the Guilds were on the steps with their hats in their hands. They ordered the Lulling made nightly. I have made it nightly since."

"The Frost Strike," I said.

"That was the newspapers. The Cut froze at the edges that night, ice half an inch thick in the third week of January, as it might in any year. They liked *Frost*. They added *Strike* on their own account."

"My father says it, when something goes wrong in the Basin. He says, 'Well, it's not the Frost Strike.'"

"It is a good phrase for it. It sounds like weather." The brass fingers pressed the rim. "There were costs. The Guilds have them in a book. I have not read the book."

I let that one lie. A seized thing does not come loose because you shout at it; you find what is holding it and you ease that. Behind me the stove drew with a long clean note, and the smoke stopped trying to come out of the seams, and the rain went on. I knew what I was carrying. I had carried it for fourteen days and I had told him only that it asked, once, whether the clock was the only one that talked to it. I owed him a trade, and the only coin I had was the one I'd been holding back.

"It asked me what salt is," I said.

He put the mug down.

I have watched men lower a charge into a shaft, and I have never seen anything done so slowly. First the base touched the lead and he held it there, two fingers on the rim; then he let the weight go, a quarter at a time; then he took his hand away, and looked at the hand. The rain had grown louder. When he spoke it was not in the voice he orders in.

"When?"

"The fifth. The first night. I said I didn't know."

"Good."

"Why good?"

"Because if you had known you would have told it. And it would have asked what it tasted like, and then where, and then..." He rubbed the moustache with the back of the brass hand. "It has heard that word through the floor for two hundred years. The Tidegate men say it all day. *Salt on the gate. Salt tide at the Bar.* It is the one word in the whole city that is about somewhere else."

"It's the sea."

"Yes."

"I know it's the sea. I'm a canal girl, not a fool. It's three miles below the gate and I've never seen it, and a thing that has been in one room for two hundred years wants to know what it is. Why wouldn't it?"

"Do not say it to it," said Orlowe.

"I haven't said anything. I told you I haven't."

The trapdoor banged open and a head rose through it like a moon through a hatch. Fenwick came up the last rungs with a tray in one hand, a steaming tureen under a plate, two bowls, a ladle, and under the other arm Dorcas, in a waistcoat I had never seen: charcoal grey, one brass button, and across the back in white wool the words ON DUTY, which cannot have been easy to do, Dorcas being what she is.

"Well, now," said Fenwick. "Wet morning. Onion soup."

The Warden and I looked at each other. Neither of us had cried, and neither of us was going to, and I think we both knew exactly where the other was keeping it. Fenwick set the tureen on the coal-box and lifted the lid, and the steam went into both our faces at once.

"It's the onions," said the Warden hoarsely.

"Onions," I agreed. I wiped my eyes with the heel of my glove and left a stripe of soot across my face.

"Terrible onion year," said Fenwick, to nobody, ladling. He put Dorcas on the Warden's knee. She regarded him, bit him once on the brass finger, and settled. The Warden sat very straight with a pigeon in a waistcoat on his knee and a bowl in his good hand, and tears ran down into his moustache, and Fenwick, who I am certain had been standing in the passage under the trapdoor for the last quarter of an hour, looked up at the rain, said that onions were a great trial to a man, and was seen to wipe his own face with a spoon.

It was a very good soup. We ate it without a word. When the bowls were empty Fenwick took the tureen away, and Dorcas was returned to the rafters with an air of affronted dignity, and the trapdoor fell shut behind them, and I said what I had come up to say, though it was no longer quite the thing I'd come up with.

"Somebody called Hask lay down inside that animal and asked to be forgiven. By who?"

"Hask is two hundred and twelve years dead."

"Then who'd know what he meant?"

"If he wrote it anywhere but on a rib, it is in the Founders' Ledger. The Charmery keeps it, under glass, and the only man who reads it is the Provost, and he reads it for the dates." He rubbed his knee. "Whatever Hask meant, he did it on purpose, and the Guilds have had two centuries to make sure nobody asks."

"It's three miles from the Tidegate to the Bar," I said. "I looked at the chart in the Tide Office. The Great Cut runs straight from this hill to the gate. It's a canal. It's a road with water on it."

"No."

"I'm not suggesting anything. I'm saying it's a road."

"The Guilds will never allow it near water," said Orlowe. "They have not permitted it within a hundred yards of a puddle in nineteen years. The gutters of this roof run to the Hill culvert. The Pit has no drain. I put a hand-pump in the vestry because the old well went too near the Brack's level. They will never allow it." He stood, one hand on the brick, favouring the knee. "And neither will I."

It came out too fast and too round, like a nut that has been run down a thread so often that the metal has gone bright. He went down the ladder-stair without another word, and I heard the trapdoor drop and his uneven tread go away along the passage toward the Pit, where he would stand a hand's breadth from the rail and look into the glow.

I picked up the mug to carry it down. It was an army mug, enamelled once, with the enamel long gone. Round the rim, where the two brass fingers had held it the whole time he talked, fifty or sixty little crescents had been pressed into the tin, overlapping, neat, bright at the edge, as if a very small animal had been chewing it. Brass is harder than tin. You do not leave a mark like that by holding something. You leave it by holding on.

I thought of a man two hundred years dead, lying on his side in the dark with a nail. I thought of a Ledger in a glass case that nobody read except for the dates. I turned the mug in my hand and counted the dents, because I count when I would rather run, and then I put it down on the shelf beside the stove, which was drawing like a good stove, and went to find out how a canal girl gets into the Charmery.
