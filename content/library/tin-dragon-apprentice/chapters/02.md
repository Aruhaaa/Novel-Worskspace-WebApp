# Cold Hands, Warm Tea

"Boots," said a voice out of the dark. "Off."

I stopped with one foot over the threshold and the soot-snow melting down the back of my neck, and looked for the man the voice belonged to. A lamp was coming across the nave at the pace of someone with a bad knee, which is to say unevenly: a step, a longer step, a drag, and a small bright click. The click was the lamp's handle against something that was not a finger.

"Hobnails ring on slate," said the man behind the lamp. "The sound goes up. Boots off, by the door, on the mat."

"I haven't got hobnails."

"Boots."

I took them off. Warden Bastien Orlowe was a broad grey man of fifty or so, with a moustache the colour and condition of a tobacco pouch, an army greatcoat gone green at the seams, and a face that gave orders even when it was resting. He looked down at my feet in their wet stockings, and something in the face came apart a little at one corner.

"The floor is cold," he told the lamp. "It's slate. The heat all goes up the mains and the floor gets what's left." He thrust a bundle at me. "Wear the socks."

They were grey wool, thick as loaves, darned at both heels in two different colours. I pulled them on over my stockings. The warmest building in Gantry, and its floor wanted my toes; the socks were the first honest thing anyone had handed me all day.

"That is the first rule," said Orlowe. "Come."

He walked me through a door in the north wall into steam and the smell of barley. The vestry was a long low room with a vaulted ceiling, two stoves, a table scarred by a century of elbows, and in the middle of it a copper vat the size of a bathing tub, double-skinned and riveted, with a brass tap at the foot and a lid you could have landed a boat on. At the vat, with a ladle in a fist like a ham, stood a man the size of a door. He was bald as an egg. His forearms were thicker than my calves. He wore a waistcoat knitted in seven colours that did not agree with one another, and on his shoulder sat a pigeon in a waistcoat of its own, buttoned.

"Well, now," said the man. "Well, now. You'll be the new one."

"Odile Marchetti."

"Fenwick Mott. Head stoker. Soup." He said it like a list of ranks. "That's Dorcas. She feels the draught." The pigeon considered me with one orange eye and found nothing in my favour. "You'll have soup."

"I was promised tea."

"Tea and soup. Warden hasn't had his tea. He never has it till somebody's watching."

"Fenwick," said Orlowe.

"Well, now. I'm only saying."

The Warden sat at the table and pulled off his gloves one finger at a time. The right hand was a hand. The left had two fingers of brass, the ring and the little, jointed at the knuckle with screws no bigger than lentils, and when he lifted the cup they ticked against the china with a small regular sound, like a watch being wound. Someone had cut those threads by hand, and cut them beautifully. I wanted to ask who. I wanted to ask more things than the night could hold.

Fenwick set a bowl in front of me: barley, bone, carrot, and a leaf of something bitter. He fished a long iron paddle out of the vat to stir it, and I looked at the vat again, because I look twice at anything with a lid.

"Lost a pocket watch in there when I started," he said comfortably. "And a pair of tongs. And Constable Dray's whistle. Went in on one Quarter-Day, came out on the next, none the worse, bar the whistle, which came out with a flavour." He chuckled into his chest. "It's a deep boiler. A man could drop a small anvil in the bottom and not miss it before spring."

"Fenwick."

"I'm stirring."

He was, too, and with his other hand he was knitting something the size of a thimble on a pair of needles he had taken from his apron pocket without looking. "Waistcoats," he said, seeing me see. "Forty-one pigeons in the rafters. Thirty-eight waistcoats. Three on the needles. It's the draught off the roof, you see. Dorcas has a herringbone. She's particular."

I put the spoon in my mouth, so as not to say anything. I had eaten nothing since a heel of bread at six that morning, and it was the best soup of my life.

"Rules," said Orlowe.

He held up three fingers, and the two brass ones came up stiffly with the third, a little behind, like boys following an older boy over a wall.

"One. The Hearth Pit has a rail. You do not touch it. Not with your hand, your tool, or your sleeve while you are sweeping. You do not lean on it, sit on it or set down a cup."

"Why?"

"Because it is the rail." He drank. "Two. You do not give orders to the dragon."

I waited, in case a second sentence was coming. "I wasn't planning to."

"They all did. Boll said 'stop that' to the pipes. Wray told it to hush. It is what people do when a large thing is near; they talk to it as if it were a horse." He said the next part quickly, like a man reading off a card. "The Lulling is a charm laid over it. An order from an untrained mouth can cross the charm. So you say nothing to it that begins with a verb."

"What if it's on fire?"

"It is a furnace. It is always on fire." The moustache lifted at one corner and put itself down again. "Three. At midnight there is the Lulling, at the Ember Bell. You attend. You hold the lamp. You do not miss it, you are not late for it, and you do not leave it for any reason up to and including the roof falling in."

"And if you're ill?"

"I am not ill."

"If you were."

"Then you would hold the lamp," said Orlowe, "and Fenwick would hold me up, and we would manage."

He passed me the milk jug. His fingers touched mine on the handle, and they were cold: not the cold of a man come in from the street, but the cold of a pump handle in January, a cold that has lived somewhere a good while. The nails were blue at the beds. I looked, and he saw me look, and he took the jug back and put his gloves on again, left hand first, over the brass.

"Hot-bricks," said Fenwick, to nobody, and went to see to the stove.

"Bring the lamp," said Orlowe, and we went into the nave.

I had not seen it, not properly. The lamp showed it to me the way a dentist shows you the inside of a mouth: a little at a time. A hundred and twenty feet of slate floor, dished toward the middle like a saucer, so that anything dropped found its own way to the Pit. The Pit itself, a round well twenty feet across with a black glazed lip and an iron rail around it, waist-high, and heat coming up out of it against my face, dry and slow, like a hand held close. And around the Pit, three times, the coils.

Each plate was the size of a shovel blade. They overlapped like roof slates and shone the dull bright grey of a new pewter jug, riveted at every edge with a neatness that made my hands itch. The body was as thick as a boiler. Between the plates along the flank I could see the red-orange of the Ember, steady and low, moving the way a sleeper's chest moves. On the highest coil, a dozen feet over my head, rested the head: a long scooped jaw as big as a rowing boat, shut, and over the left eye a lid of tin plate, shut too. I looked for the blue I had seen at the door. There was nothing. It had been a rivet. I had said so.

"Don't stare," said Orlowe.

"I'm not staring. I'm counting."

I was. I count when I would rather run. The jaw had a double seam of rivets, one row either side, domed, even, every one the twin of the last, and I began at the hinge and went along the left and back along the right with my lips shut: forty, a hundred and nine, a hundred and sixty, and when I stopped he was watching me with his head on one side.

"Two hundred and six," I said.

He did not answer for so long that I thought I had miscounted. "Nobody has finished the count," he said. "Wray gave up at sixty. Boll reached a hundred and nine, which isn't even an interesting number."

"Did Vaszary finish?"

"Vaszary did not count. Vaszary delivered opinions." He turned the lamp away. "Your locker is in the passage."

The lockers stood in the passage off the vestry, four tall tin cupboards, numbered, with a dent in each at kicking height. The third door gave when he pulled it, and out came a smell of violet pastilles as if it had been waiting. Inside hung a silk cravat the colour of a bruised plum, knotted in a way that was plainly a statement. On the shelf lay a pair of rabbit-lined gloves, a tin of pastilles, and a copy of *Principles of Applied Glyphwork* whose spine had never been cracked.

"Vaszary's," said Orlowe. "He left in a hurry."

"Without his gloves."

"Without his gloves." The Warden regarded the cravat the way a man regards a stranger's dog. "Fenwick will find a box."

On the inside of the door three names had been scratched with something sharp, each with its tally. IGNATZ BOLL, eleven strokes. P. WRAY, six, and under them in capitals, NO. And C. VASZARY, in a looping hand with a flourish under the Y: four strokes, and a fifth begun and abandoned halfway, like a man who had got as far as the door.

"Scratch yours if you like," said Orlowe. "Most do."

"I don't think I will."

"No." And, to my surprise, he nearly smiled. "I would not either."

At a quarter to midnight Fenwick came into the nave with a hot-brick in each hand, wrapped in flannel, and put one into each pocket of the Warden's greatcoat as if he were filling a stove. Then he went away to bed and left us the dark.

The Ember Bell hung at the east end of the Pit from an iron gantry, a bell the size of a bathtub with its lip at the height of my chin. Orlowe knelt on the slate, bad knee stuck out to one side, and with a stub of chalk drew around it a figure I could not read: a ring inside a ring inside a five-pointed star, with lines running out from it like the spokes of a wheel. Along every line he pressed a length of solder wire with his thumb. It looked like a child's drawing of a machine. "Chalk and solder," he said, not looking up. "Hold the lamp lower."

Then he stood, laid one hand flat on the Bell, and began.

I don't know what I had expected. Thunder, perhaps. It was a lullaby. A man sang a lullaby in a language worn smooth as a step, low and even, more breath than voice, and I caught *ember* in it, and *down*, and a number of verbs I did not know, all of them going the same way. The chalk lines took on a faint glow like the last of a cooling fire. Where he stood the air began to smoke, and then I saw that it was not smoke but frost. His breath came out white. A rime formed in the moustache. I watched the colour leave his lips, and his ears, and when the lamp swung, the backs of his hands; the bricks in his pockets were doing no more than a spoonful of tea in a flood.

Minutes went by. The Ember behind the ribs slowed and dimmed, a shade, then another. Up in the rafters forty-one pigeons said nothing in their waistcoats.

And then from the Hearth Pit came a sound like a pot lid being lifted, one small iron scrape, as though something underneath had had enough of waiting. After it, so quiet I would have missed it if the nave had not been built to carry sound, a voice no thicker than a thread said, "No."

A boiler says all sorts of things as it cools. A rattle, a knock, a sigh in the flue. It does not say no. I filed it under pipes.

Orlowe did not stop. His voice did not waver by so much as a note. But the chalk in his left hand, which he had forgotten he was holding, snapped in two, and I was the only one in the nave who saw it, because I was the only one looking at his hands.
