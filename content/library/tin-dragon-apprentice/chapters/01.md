# The Seized Valve

I had my right arm in the Pennywhistle gearbox up to the shoulder when the lock-keeper told me I was a disgrace to my trade, and since I could not reach my spanner without moving, I let him finish.

"Forty minutes," said Ludo Platt. He stood over me under an umbrella that had lost three ribs. "Forty minutes in my gearbox and the water hasn't shifted a thimble. My grandfather could have freed that valve with his teeth."

"Then he's welcome to the job." My cheek was pressed to the cast-iron housing, which was colder than the sleet, and the words came out sideways. "He'd want to hurry. You're paying by the hour."

"I'm paying by the result."

"Then you're paying nothing, and I'm here for the company."

The valve was a four-inch ground paddle in the lower gate, driven through a worm and wheel, and what had gone wrong with it was what goes wrong with everything on the canals in November: wet, neglect, and a lock-keeper who thinks grease is a luxury, like butter. The packing in the gland had swollen into a plug. The plug had gripped the stem. The stem had gripped the wheel, and the whole business held the Pennywhistle gate shut with the stubbornness of a drowned boot. Three of Platt's lock-hands had already tried a cheater bar, which is how you turn a seized valve into a bent one, and then they had sent for the girl from Number Four Basin, because she was thin, cheap, and owed everybody.

I had thirty-one inches of arm and thirty of them were in the box. My fingertips lay on the gland follower, a bronze collar the size of a pat of butter. I held the spanner by its jaws, handle pointing at the collar, with a wrist that had about an inch of play in it.

"Stand back," I said. "You're dripping in my ear."

"It's sleet, girl. It falls."

"Then let it fall somewhere else."

I tapped the follower three times with the end of the handle, short and hard and a finger's width apart, the way my father taught me on the engine of *Marguerite's Patience* when I was seven and his hands were bigger than my head. Dario Marchetti says the first knock introduces you, the second makes your case, and the third is a bluff. Somewhere in the box forty years of rust took the hint. The stem clicked like a knuckle. The wheel turned a quarter under my palm, the paddle lifted, and the culvert let go a long gurgle, like a man clearing his throat before he says something unwise.

In the chamber, the coal lighter that had sat there since breakfast, its crew eating bread on the cabin roof out of pure boredom, began to sink down the wall like a lift.

I drew my arm out in stages, like a cork. The sleeve was black to the shoulder. I wiped my hand on my trousers, which was no help to either, and checked my left wrist, where the old burn lies in its pale comma, as I do after any job with iron in it. Nothing new.

"Six bits," said Platt, who had recovered his purse.

"Eight."

"It took forty minutes."

"Forty-three, and the price is eight, and I'll throw in the reason. Somebody repacked that gland with a bootlace. Tarred. It swelled like a pudding. You want a quarter-inch flax braid and tallow, and you want it changed before the frost, not after."

He looked at the umbrella, then the lock, then me. "Seven," he said, and counted seven bits into my palm one at a time, as if each had been pulled from his jaw.

"Odile!"

Pim Rooke was coming down the towpath at a run, which is how he does everything, his satchel banging and the sleeves of his jacket flapping ten inches past his hands. It was a coalman's jacket, six sizes too big, and it had reached him by a route he would not discuss. He was eleven. He had a gap where his top front tooth should have been, and on his feet he wore sacking tied on with twine. In the two years I had known him he had sold me a map of the Brack's mudbanks, the fact that Platt's wife drank, and once my own scarf.

"I've a ticket," he said. "For you. Odile Odile Odile."

"I'm busy."

"It's pink." He dug in the satchel, where tickets lay sorted by colour like the plates of a very small and very sinister pack of cards: white for the Ticket-huts, blue for the Council, yellow for the Tide Office. "Pink is the Commission. Noon, Gast Hall, by order. A bit for the run."

"A bit for a run you'd have made anyway, to keep your feet warm."

"Then a bit for the warming. And two more if I don't tell Mr. Platt what it says."

Platt was reading it over my shoulder already. I gave Pim a bit. He bit it, as he bites everything, to find out whether it was real, and went off along the towpath with the face of a boy who had done a good morning's trade. The ticket said: *Miss O. Marchetti, mechanic, is required to attend at Gast Hall, second floor, at noon this Quarter-Day, upon a matter touching the Cathedral.* Green wax. A seal as big as a crown piece.

"The Cathedral," said Platt, with unconcealed relish. "What have you done to the Cathedral?"

"I've never been inside it."

"That's what they all say."

I washed the worst of the gearbox off in the lock-side trough and walked up into the city. On every square a tide-clock stood on its pillar with its single hand, and today's said ebb, a quarter down, and the Brack, which I could smell, agreed with it. They don't tell you the hour, the tide-clocks. Nobody in Gantry has ever much needed the hour. What we need to know is how much water is coming.

Gast Hall stood on Treadle Street, the newest building in the city and the only one made of marble. Among all that slate and soot it looked like a tooth in a coal scuttle. Inside, it was so warm that my sleeve began to steam, and the smell of canal came off me in a way that made the doorman breathe through his mouth. A clerk named Anselm Roux received me on the second floor. He had a face like a folded receipt and a desk where the pens lay in order of length.

"Miss Marchetti. You are wet."

"It's November."

"You are also black to the shoulder."

"Platt's valve. It was seven bits and a gearbox, if you'd like a share."

He did not smile. I have known sluice gates with more expression. "The post of apprentice to the Dragon-Warden," said Roux, "has fallen vacant."

"The dragon has a warden?" I said. "I mean, I knew. I'd never thought about it. It's a boiler."

"The Commission is assured it is a boiler. The Charter of the Hearth is assured it is also a Warden, an apprentice and a ceremony at midnight. Three candidates have been engaged since the first of October. Mr. Ignatz Boll lasted eleven days. Miss Perpetua Wray, six. Mr. Casimir Vaszary of the Charmery, four." He read the names off like three weather reports. "All have declined to continue. None has said why."

"And you want me to find out."

"I want you to fill the post." He turned a page. "By the Charter, the Warden must keep an apprentice on the books on every Quarter-Day. If the Cathedral has none at sundown, the heat tithe lapses."

"The what?"

"The levy on every hearth in Gantry, collected today, paid out tomorrow as Hearth Tickets. No apprentice, no tithe. No tithe, no Tickets, until the Council sits again in January."

I thought of Cinder Row, which lived on Hearth Tickets the way a lamp lives on oil. Roux watched me think of it. He had, I supposed, a column for that.

"Then find someone else," I said.

"You are the fifth name on the list."

"What list?"

"A short one. Persons of mechanical competence, available at short notice."

"Available."

"And under obligation to this Commission."

There it was. I told him I mended locks, which I did, and well, and that I had every gate between Number Four Basin and the Great Cut wanting me twice a month, and a father with a boat. I told him I was not going to shovel a tin kettle's ashes for a stranger at four crowns a month and my keep, which was the wage he named. I told him no in three different ways, with a measurement in each, and Roux waited until I ran out, the way you wait for a kettle.

"Yes," he said. "And this."

He took a paper from the drawer and laid it on the desk, turned so I could read it. It was a note of hand, and the hand was my father's: the big loose M, the tail on the D like a dog's. *Dario Marchetti, bargemaster, secured upon the barge Marguerite's Patience and her fuel licence.* The sum at the foot had been written out in words and then again in figures, in case anyone had hoped.

Three hundred and twenty crowns.

I did the sum the way I do all sums, by what it weighs. A hundred and sixty days of a labourer's wage. The barge, with her engine and her hold and my mother's name on the stern, would not have fetched three hundred and twenty at a fire sale. My father had told me the note was forty. He had told me with his big laugh, and I had believed him, because a daughter does.

"The Commission is prepared to freeze the interest," said Roux, "for as long as you hold the post. The note stands. It ceases to grow."

"And if I don't hold the post?"

"Then it is a note." He laid a pen across the paper, parallel to the edge. "Sundown, Miss Marchetti."

I signed. It is strange how little ink it takes to become somebody's apprentice.

As I laid the pen down, the door opened and a smell of bergamot came into the room ahead of a plump man in a bowler hat. He wore a pince-nez and an overcoat the colour of good tea, and two men behind him carried rolled plans in their arms like firewood. He looked at the paper, then at me, and then he took my inky hand in both of his. They were warm and dry, like a pair of slippers left by a stove.

"Splendid," said Hartwell Gast, Commissioner for Fuel, whose name was on the building, the lamp-posts and half the stoves in the city. "Splendid, my dear. I knew the Cathedral would find itself a spirited girl."

And he was gone down the corridor, plans and all, with the smell trailing after him like a dog.

"Odile," I told the corridor. "It's got five letters. I counted."

"I would not take it amiss," said Roux, blotting. "He says it to the Mayor."

*Marguerite's Patience* lay at Number Four Basin, seventy feet of riveted iron with a stern cabin, a two-cylinder compound engine, and a brass whistle on her funnel shaped like a goose's head, which my father called the Goose and which could be heard the length of the Great Cut in a decent wind. He was in the engine hatch with a rag and a kipper, and he came up out of it laughing before I had finished my first sentence.

"The Dragon-Warden's 'prentice!" He wiped his hands on his trousers, which made them no cleaner, and took me by both shoulders. Dario Marchetti is thin and stooped, with a laugh too large for his chest and grease under every nail he owns. "They'll name a pie after you. Odile Marchetti, tamer of tin. Will there be a medal?"

"There'll be four crowns a month. Dad. Roux says the note's three hundred and twenty."

My father's hand went up to his throat, where the thimble hung on its string. It was my mother's, silver, with a dent in the crown where she had pressed too hard on needles for twenty years, and he had worn it there since the winter she died. He rolled it between finger and thumb, the way he does. "Three hundred and twenty," he said. "Is that all? I'd have thought they could manage another nought. It's a Quarter-Day number, Odile. Roux puts a nought on everything today to watch the debtors faint. You didn't faint?"

"I didn't faint."

"Then you passed." The thimble went round. "It's forty crowns and some silliness. Don't spoil your first night thinking about silliness."

"Dad."

"Take the brick."

He had one in the cabin stove already, as if he had known; he wrapped it in a grey sock and put it in my hands, and I took it, because it is easier to take a brick than to take a father by the shoulders and shake him. I packed my bag: two wrenches, a hammer, a coil of flax, a tin of tallow and ninety-one other things. I kissed him on the stubble. He told me dragons were only boilers with opinions, and went back down the hatch before I could see his face.

The sleet gave up at about four. By the time I was climbing Ember Hill the soot-snow had started in its place, the grey flakes that come off the furnace stacks from November onward, soft as pencil shavings, settling on my sleeves and refusing to melt. Every street on the hill ran up to the same place. I had looked at the Cathedral every day of my life, over the roofs from the Basin, and thought about it about as often as you think about your own lung. Now it stood in front of me: slate and iron, a roof like an upturned keel, and doors forty feet high.

I put my palm on them. They were warm. Warm like bread in the hour after it comes out, when you cannot bear to wait and cannot bear to put it down.

Cut into the left leaf was a smaller door, a wicket, standing open a hand's width on lamplight. I pushed it with the toe of my boot, and it swung without a sound, and I stepped inside.

The nave went up into the dark farther than any lamp could follow. At the far end, sunk in the floor, a round of orange glow lay in the Hearth Pit, and around it, shining the colour of pewter wherever the light touched, lay something coiled like a rope of kettles. Plate on plate on plate. I looked for the seams, as I always do, and the seams were there, riveted and neat as a boiler-maker's best, and I felt better. Seams meant machine.

High above the coils, where the neck lifted into the dark, something pale shone. It was a long way up. An eye the colour of skimmed milk, with a blue in it like the blue at the root of a flame, and it opened a crack, no wider than a door left on the latch. It looked at me. Then it shut.

I told myself it was a rivet catching the light. There was no light up there to catch.
