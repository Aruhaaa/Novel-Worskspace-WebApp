# A Question for the Furnace

The cheap work in a furnace cathedral is ash. Ash on the flags, ash on the rail I was not to touch, ash in the creases of my elbows and, for reasons I decline to go into, in my ears. By my second full day I had swept the nave twice, black-leaded the frame of the Ember Bell, and learned that a Warden's apprentice, for the first week at least, is a chimney with opinions.

The coal came at dawn in carts, up the hill and in at the north door, where it was tipped into three iron hoppers on rails. Fenwick counted it. He counted the way other men say grace, with his eyes shut and his lips moving and one hand flat on the hopper's side, as if taking its pulse.

"Two tons," he said to the first hopper. "And two. And two." He opened his eyes. "Six tons, Odile. Every day, and the Commission sends it, and up it goes through the mains. Mind, you'd not credit what that comes to in the Row."

"What does it come to in the Row?"

"A ticket and a queue." He slapped the hopper gently, in apology. "A Hearth Ticket's four minutes at a standpipe, and you stand in the snow for an hour to get one. Well, now. It's not the dragon's doing. It's the price of the sack. I take the end of the vat down to Slake Street of a Tuesday, and it's never enough. Never. A man stirs what he's given." He handed me a shovel. "Black coal on the left. The grey-flecked on the right is lullcoal, from the Charmery. One shovel in ten goes in with the rest. Don't breathe it if you can help it."

I breathed it. It smelled of warm hay and cough syrup, and one lungful made me yawn so hard my jaw cracked. I spent the afternoon shovelling at arm's length with my face turned away, like a woman feeding a goat she does not trust.

Supper was at ten, in the vestry, off whatever Fenwick's vat had left. The Warden ate standing up, as though sitting down would be an admission, and asked me three questions: whether the Bell frame was done, whether I had touched the rail, and whether I had slept. Yes, no, and not much. He nodded, as if I had passed a minor examination, and went away to do whatever he did in the two hours before the Lulling, which I was beginning to suspect was nothing at all, only waiting, the way you wait for a tooth to be pulled.

At midnight I held the lamp while Orlowe chalked and chanted and went the colour of putty. Nothing said no. I decided I had imagined it, which is how I deal with most things I cannot measure.

It was the hinge that kept me awake. On the left side of the dragon's head, where the jaw met the skull, there was a pivot as thick as my wrist, and every time the thing breathed out, which it did about once a minute with a long soft hiss of steam, the jaw shifted a hair's breadth and the pivot let go a squeal. It was a high, ugly, wounded sound, somewhere between an F and a goose, and I could hear it through two walls and a blanket. A mechanic can sleep through a flood, a fire and a family quarrel, but not through a bearing that wants oil. At half past one I gave up, took the lamp, a ladder from the passage and my bag, and went out in my socks.

The rail stood twelve feet back from the head. I set the ladder outside it, against the highest coil, so that my feet never touched forbidden ground, and climbed until the hinge was level with my face.

From up there the nave was another country. The Ember moved under the plates in a slow orange pulse, and the coils ticked and settled as they cooled for the night, like a ship at anchor. In the rafters the pigeons slept in a row, buttoned to the chin, like clerks. Everything smelled of hot dust and, faintly, of hay.

The pin was as thick as my wrist, with a domed head the size of a saucer. It had walked a quarter-inch out of its bronze bush, because the cotter that should hold it had gone, and every breath dragged the end of it across the housing plate. That was the squeal. Dry, dirty, and walking: three things wrong, and I could cure two. I scraped the bush with a pick and packed it with tallow and flake graphite, and while I worked I talked, as I always talk. My father does it, and his father did. You say what you see. It stops your hands from shaking.

"Right, you old kettle. Let's have a look at you. Dry as a biscuit and grey with forty years of nobody. Easy. That's it. Nearly."

The dragon breathed out. The jaw shifted, the pin shrieked, and I winced for it and said, the way my father says it to any engine that complains, "Does that hurt?"

"Yes," said the floor.

I say the floor because that was where it came from: up through the ladder rails and into the soles of my feet before it reached my ears, a low voice, slow, like warm gravel poured from one hand into the other. "Yes. Thank you for asking."

The wrench went out of my fingers. I heard it go. I had a good while to hear it, because the slate was twelve feet down and the Cathedral had been built to carry sound. It struck, rang once, and began to roll, tink, tink, tink, down the dish of the floor toward the middle as everything in that room did, and fetched up against the foot of the Pit rail six inches from the lowest coil, and lay there.

I did not go after it. The rail was the rail. The coil was a coil. My legs had turned to string.

"Who's that?" My voice sounded like someone younger and less convinced.

"There is nobody else," said the voice. "There is only me."

"Where are you?"

"I am very large. You have your hand upon a part of me." The metal under my palm gave a small tick, as if something inside were counting. "Upon the part that hurts."

I took the hand away as if from a stove. Then, because I am what I am, and because the alternative was to scream, I put it back and said, "Since when?"

"Since the pin walked. Eleven winters, and a little over."

"Eleven winters! It wants a cotter. Somebody's lost the cotter, and nobody's looked, and the pin's been chewing itself against the housing plate..." I heard what I was doing. I was giving an estimate to a dragon.

"You can talk," I said.

Nothing. The Pit ticked. Above me the hinge, freshly greased, said nothing at all.

"You can talk," I said again, more firmly, the way you speak to a gauge that has stuck. Nothing again. Sweat ran down my ribs. I shut my eyes, opened them, and tried it as a question, because I was losing my nerve and wanted the thing to answer no so that I could go to bed. "Can you talk?"

"I am talking," said the voice, and I would swear it sounded puzzled. "Is that not plain?"

It had ignored the sentence and answered the question. I noted that, the way I note a pressure drop, with no idea yet what it meant.

I did what any mechanic would do. I looked for the trick. I rapped the plate with my knuckles and listened for a speaking-tube, laid my ear against a rivet, and peered behind the ladder for a boy with a trumpet. Within reach of my arm there were a hundred and ninety rivets, and not one of them was a mouth.

"Why did you thank me?" I said. "When I asked."

"Because nobody has asked it." The gravel eased, as a man's voice eases when he has waited a long time to be questioned on a subject. "They ask whether I am working. The large man asks it of the hoppers, and the Warden asks it of the Bell. In two hundred winters no one has asked whether it hurts." A tick. "It is a good question. I should like to be asked it again."

"What are you called?" It seemed the next thing to ask.

"The men call me Ferrous. It is a word for iron. I do not remember a name that was mine." The metal ticked. "I am the one who is kept."

"Kept by whom?"

"By the rivets. By the Bell. By the large man who counts the coal aloud, whom I do not mind. By the man with the cold hands, who comes at midnight and says the words." The gravel slowed. "He has not asked me anything in nineteen years."

I held on to the ladder. I was a canal mechanic of sixteen with a burn on my wrist, a note of hand against my name and tallow to my elbows, and a tin dragon was telling me its troubles.

"You count things," I said. "Do you?"

"What is near. There are two hundred and six rivets in my jaw. There are eleven thousand and four slates upon the roof, of which two have slipped since Tuesday. You have been upon that ladder for one hundred and sixty-two breaths."

"Is that right?"

"It is correct."

"Did you hear me count the rivets?" I said, and I think I wanted it to like me.

"I did. You did not lose your place." There was a pause, with something almost shy in it. "You asked what I am called. I have not asked you. What are you called?"

"Odile."

"Odile." The word came up through my soles like a stone being turned over. "Five letters. Ferrous has seven, and it is not even mine. I shall keep yours." The metal ticked. "May I ask you another?"

I should have said no. I should have said it was half past one and I had a Warden who gave orders in his sleep. "Go on."

"What is the salt?"

"The what?"

"The salt. It is a word that comes up through the floor. The men who tend the water say it. The salt is up. Salt on the gate. I have heard it for two hundred years and nobody has told me what it is." The voice lowered, as if it were embarrassed to ask. "Is it large?"

I opened my mouth. I knew a great deal about salt. I knew it cost four bits the stone. I knew it came up the Brack on a spring tide like a bad smell and stiffened the gate hinges, and that a sixteenth of an inch of hull plate went to it every year as surely as to a tax. I could have told that dragon exactly what salt did to a rivet. I had not the faintest idea what it was. I had never been below the Tidegate. I had never seen the sea.

"I don't know," I said. It was the first thing I had said all night that I was sure of.

"That is all right," said the voice. It did not sound disappointed. It sounded like a man who has asked the way of a hundred strangers and had the same shrug from every one, and is polite about it.

I stayed where I was, with my cheek against the cold coil and my heart going like a badly-set governor, and the silence went on until it got on my nerves. "I should go," I said, to nobody. Then, because I am my father's daughter and cannot leave a room without asking whether it minds, "Is that all right?"

"It is all right," said the voice. "It is a very long time since anyone asked whether a thing was all right. Again?"

"Again what?"

"Ask me another."

I backed down the ladder with my eyes on the great shut face and did not stop to fold the thing. I went out of the nave in my socks with the lamp held in front of me like a shield. The wrench stayed where it fell, at the foot of the rail, ticking as it took the heat of the Pit.
