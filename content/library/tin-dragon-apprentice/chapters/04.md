# The Hinge of the Jaw

At four in the morning the Cathedral clock ticks like a man tapping his foot outside a locked door. It hangs above the nave doors, a steam-fed escapement the size of a cartwheel, and by day you stop hearing it. At four you hear nothing else.

I stood under it in my socks with a lamp and told myself I had come for my wrench.

I had come the long way, through the passage past locker three and its violet smell, and past the vestry, where Fenwick slept on a cot with his waistcoats hung round him like the flags of a small and peaceable nation. His snore was a slow bass thing, like a boiler considering a change of career.

It lay where it had fallen, a grey dash at the foot of the Pit rail, lit amber by the Ember. Between it and me there were forty feet of cold slate and one rule. Beyond it the coils rose in their three turns, and on the highest the great head lay on its side with the tin lid shut over the clouded eye, and the jaw, which I had greased, was silent for the first time since I had come. Far back behind the ribs the glow rose and fell. It looked like a sleeper. It had looked like one the night before.

I had argued the matter with myself in the cell. A girl who works with machines learns to distrust any evidence that arrives in the small hours, when the mind makes friends with every noise. I had been tired. I had breathed lullcoal. Boilers mutter. I had put it on the shelf with the other explanations, and then I had got up, because you do not trust a reading until you have taken it twice.

"Are you awake?" I said to the air, not loudly.

The clock ticked eleven times. Then the floor said, "I am awake in the way a kettle is awake before it sings."

Two things happened. The hair rose on my arms, and I discovered that I was delighted. I am not proud of the second.

"What do you do," I said, "all night?"

"I count." The warm gravel was unhurried. "The slates. The ticks of the clock. The Bell, which has been struck forty-one thousand and some times, and which does not sound like a bell to me. It sounds like being sat upon. Last night I counted a girl on a ladder. She went up fourteen rungs and came down in nine."

"I was in a hurry."

"You were frightened. It is much the same thing, but the steps are shorter."

Now. A mechanic does not stop at two readings, and a mechanic with a talking dragon is still a mechanic. The clock was fed off the same mains as the rest of the Cathedral; if the dragon governed the pressure, it ought to be able to stop the clock by thinking about it. It was a fair experiment. I turned to face the clock and, in the voice I use on a governor, I said, "Stop the clock."

The clock went on. Tick, tick, tick, with an air of modesty.

"Stop the clock," I said, louder. Then, "Stop the clock, please." I tried "Halt," and "Cease," and "Belay that," which I had from a bargee who had once been in the navy, and then I ran out of commands and stood with my arms folded, like a person who has shouted at a very large animal and found that it means to wait out the argument.

"Can you stop the clock?" I said, without hope.

"I do not think so," said the floor, at once. "It is not mine. I should not wish to, in any case. It is the only one in the building that talks to me at night."

I looked at the clock, which ticked on, innocent. I looked at my own hands. I had been going about it the wrong way round, like a man who rattles a locked door and swears at it and never tries the bell. Orders got nothing. A question got an answer. It was as plain as a pressure gauge, and I had walked past it for three days.

"What would you like to be called?" I said.

I had not known I was going to ask. The pause lasted so long that the clock got through twenty ticks, and when the voice came it was lower, a rumble I felt in my teeth. "Not Ferrous."

"What, then?"

"I do not know. Ask me when I do."

After that there was nothing for it but to finish the job. It is a failing of mine; I cannot leave a half-set pin. I carried the ladder back to the highest coil, hooked the lamp on a rung, and climbed with my second wrench in my belt. The pin wanted tapping home with a drift and a hammer, and then its cotter. I patted my bag, which was a mistake, because I knew perfectly well it held ninety-one things and not one of them was a cotter pin. I had used my last in September.

"Where," I said, to the hinge, "am I supposed to find a three-sixteenths cotter at four in the morning?"

"There are two in your hair," said the floor.

I put up my hand. I wear my braids pinned flat to my head with a cotter pin through each, as I have done since I was twelve, because a hairpin gives out in a week and a cotter pin lasts a decade. The dragon had counted them, of course. It had not been a question for the dragon. It had answered anyway, as though I had asked the room.

I pulled out the left one. The braid came down my back like a dropped rope. I tapped the pin home, slid the cotter through its cross-hole, spread the legs with my pliers and bent them flat, and the thing breathed out twice while I did it and the jaw moved twice and neither time did the hinge say a word.

"What is the small thing?" said the floor.

"This?" I held it up, absurdly, in the lamplight. "A cotter pin. It goes through the pin, and the pin goes through the hinge, and it stops the big thing from walking away."

"From the place where it belongs."

"Well. From the place it's been put."

"I see," said the floor, and said nothing more for a while.

The side door banged. A voice said "Well, now," and Fenwick came into the nave in his stocking feet with Dorcas on his shoulder and a tin of seed under his arm, and the pigeons began to come down from the rafters one at a time, in order of precedence, to settle on his arms. It was a quarter to six. He looked up and found me on my ladder with my hair down one side and a pair of pliers in my hand.

"Talking to the hinge," said Fenwick.

"I've finished with the hinge," I said, rather too brightly. "It's done. It was the cotter."

"Well, now. I talk to my boilers myself. Gets the best out of them." He peered at the jaw. "That's been squealing since I came, that has. Warden said to leave the jaw be."

"Nobody told me."

"Well, now. Nobody told you not to, either." He beamed at me, which is a considerable event, like a barn door opening on a summer. Then his eye fell on my braid, and he said, with the sharpness that sometimes surprised me in him, "You've lost a pin."

"It's in the hinge."

"Well, now. That's a good home for a pin." He regarded the hinge with respect, as one regards a man who has been given a medal.

I looked down at the great shut face. "Does that feel better?" I said, to the hinge, because it seemed the safest place to say it.

"It does not hurt," said the floor. "I had forgotten that it was possible."

"Better, is it?" said Fenwick comfortably, and smiled at the hinge, and I understood that he had heard nothing at all. He stood on the same slate, seven feet from me, and the voice that had gone up my legs like warm water was not for him. It was an odd thing, to be the only person in a room who could hear the room.

"Warden's down at six," Fenwick said, scattering seed. "Looks at the Ember first thing, he does, like a man looking into a cradle. Soup after." And he went off towards the vestry, with the pigeons following him in their waistcoats.

I was on the ground, coiling the flax, when Orlowe came in. He was in his socks, with his gloves in one hand and a lamp in the other, and he did what Fenwick had said: he went first to the Pit and stood at the rail with his hand a hair's breadth above it, and looked down into the glow. It stood a shade brighter than he had left it, gold at the edges instead of its night-orange, the way a stove comes up when someone has opened the damper. I had not noticed. He had been reading that glow for nineteen years. Then his eyes went along the foot of the rail, and stopped.

I have seen men go pale. Not like that. The colour did not drain; it left in one piece, like a blind coming down.

"Whose is that?"

It was my wrench. A fourteen-inch pipe wrench with an ash handle and my father's initials burned into the grip. "Mine," I said. "I dropped it."

"When?"

"Half past one. Last night. I was greasing the left hinge."

"You were on the ladder."

"Outside the rail. I never touched the rail."

"What did you say to it?"

"To what?"

"Don't." He had come halfway across the nave without my seeing him move, limp and all, and the lamp shook in his hand. "What did you say to it?"

I could have lied. I thought about it, with the clock ticking over my head. But I am my father's daughter in this, and we cannot lie well. "I asked if it hurt," I said.

"You are lying."

"I'm not."

"You are a girl of sixteen who has been in this building three days, and you are standing in my nave telling me—"

"Does that hurt," I said. "That's what I said. Those three words. And it said, *Yes. Thank you for asking.*"

He sat down.

He did not choose to. His bad knee gave, and the rail was behind him, and he sat on it, the iron rail he had told me not to touch with so much as my sleeve, and put the lamp between his boots and his hands between his knees. The brass fingers were clicking against each other. He did not seem to know.

"You're sitting on the rail," I said.

"I am aware."

We stayed like that for the time it took the clock to count out forty. Behind him the Ember rose and fell, and I had a notion, which I put away at once, that it was listening.

"Did you give it orders?" he said at last, without lifting his head.

"I told it to stop the clock."

"And?"

"The clock went on."

A sound came out of him that was not a laugh but lived in the same street. "Good," he said. "Good." He rubbed his face with his good hand. "And Fenwick?"

"He came in while I was talking to the hinge. He didn't hear a thing. He thought I was talking to a hinge."

"He would."

I had told him the words, and the hour, and the clock. I had not told him about the salt. I could not have said why, except that the question had been put to me, and a thing like that is not passed on like a coin. I held it back, and he did not ask, and we both pretended there was nothing to hold.

"Who else have you told?" said Orlowe.

"Nobody. Who would I tell?"

He looked at me then, properly, for the first time since I had come in at the wicket. His eyes were a washed-out grey, like a sky somebody had been at with a cloth, and there was a fear in them so old and so well-kept that I thought: it has a cell of its own, and this is the first time anyone's been let in to see it.

"Keep it that way," said Bastien Orlowe, "or they will put you in the Pit with it."
