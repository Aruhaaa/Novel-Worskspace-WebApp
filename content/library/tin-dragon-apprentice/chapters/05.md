# Soot-Snow Market

The queue for Hearth Tickets on Slake Street began at the Ticket-hut and ended, by the time I found its tail, at the foot of Cinder Row Bridge: four hundred people standing in the soot-snow, not talking, because talking costs breath and breath costs warmth.

I had come down the hill for hinge-grease. The right jaw hinge was going the way the left had gone, and the Cathedral's tallow tin, according to Fenwick, had gone where tins go. Cinder Row market sold the only graphite grease within a mile that did not carry a Commission stamp and a Commission price. It was the eighth of November, and I had been five nights in the Cathedral. In that time I had learned that the Warden took his tea strong and his silences stronger, that Dorcas the pigeon bit, and that a tin dragon left alone at four in the morning wanted to know what a drain was, what the Row smelled of, and whether hot-bricks had feelings. It had not asked about the salt again. I was grateful. I had no better answer than the first.

The Warden and I had not mentioned it. He lulled, I held the lamp, and by daylight he watched me the way you watch a pan you suspect you have left on the stove.

At the head of the queue stood a standpipe: ten feet of fluted cast iron with a brass cock at waist height, hissing steam from the mains into the cold. A Ticket bought four minutes of it. You held your hands to the iron, or a kettle, or a brick, or a child. The woman at the cock held a blanket.

She was young, in a man's coat, with a baby inside the coat and the blanket folded in four. She laid one quarter against the iron and counted aloud, low, to ninety. At ninety she wrapped that quarter round the baby's feet, turned the blanket, laid the next quarter to the pipe, and counted again. Four quarters, four minutes, and the baby warmed in sections like a loaf. She did not hold her own hands to the iron at all. The steam wet her sleeves and her cracked red fingers, and she never looked at them. When the cock ran dry she tucked the blanket round the child, said "There" in a voice with nothing left on it, and went back to the end of the line.

I stood with my list in my hand and my own hot-brick in my pocket and was ashamed of the brick.

"Odile Odile Odile." Pim had arrived at my elbow, as Pim does, from a place where nobody had seen him come. "I've a map."

"I don't want a map."

"Every standpipe in Gantry. A hundred and thirty-one, in my own hand, with a crown by the ones that have pressure and a cross by the ones gone dead. You'll want it, working up at the Cathedral. Three bits."

"One."

"Two and a promise." His eyes were on his satchel strap, which was held together with string. "A promise you'll look out for boots."

"What size?"

"Whatever's going."

It was a good map. It had been drawn in pencil by a child with a surveyor's eye, every street named, every cock counted. I folded it into my coat.

"Coal's fourteen," said Pim, conversationally, falling into step. "Fourteen bits the sack. Yesterday it was twelve. By the New Year they'll sell it by the lump, with a receipt." He gave the queue a look that on an older face might have been called an opinion. "I don't make the news, Odile. I only sell it."

Hester Pallitt's stall stood under the arch of the Bridge, where the cobbles were dry: a trestle of tubs and tins and coils of rope, and behind it an old woman in a great many shawls, like the figurehead of a ship that had been given up on and taken in.

"You'll be the Goose girl," said Mrs. Pallitt, before I had opened my mouth.

"I'm Odile Marchetti."

"Dario's. I'd know the chin." She raised her voice to the next stall. "Gerda! It's the Goose girl, Dario's girl!" Heads turned along the Bridge. "Every bad Quarter-Day for ten years, child, that man has brought us a hold of coal, and not a bit asked. And every time, the Goose goes off at the foot of the Row and every window on Slake Street goes up. Do you know what that whistle means to us? It means somebody's coming."

I had not known. I had known the Goose, an ugly brass honker my father blew at every lock and bridge on the Great Cut because he liked the noise. I had known we sometimes went down the Row on bad Quarter-Days with the hold full. I had thought of it as cargo. I had not thought of it as a gift, or of a gift as something that costs. I thought of a note of hand with my father's loose M on it, and for the first time wondered how much of three hundred and twenty crowns was coal.

"Graphite grease," I said. "A pound. And a dozen cotter pins, three-sixteenths."

"Six bits for the grease."

"It's grit. I can feel it from here. One part in eight, by my thumb, and the rest is mutton fat that's had a hard journey."

"That grease was rendered by my own hands from a sheep I knew."

"Then your sheep had a hard life. Three."

"Five, and I'll throw in the pins."

"Four, and the pins at cost, and I'll tell my father to blow the Goose at the foot of the Row next time he passes."

"Four and a half," said Mrs. Pallitt, with deep satisfaction, "and you've a bad head for sentiment, like your dad." She wrapped the grease in a cabbage leaf.

The square under the Bridge had five tide-clocks, a gift from the Tide Office in some forgotten fit of generosity, one on each side and one in the middle, and that morning they said High Water, Low Water, Half-Ebb, Half-Flood and Slack. At no time in the history of the Row had any two of them agreed. This mattered more than it sounds, because Orrin Gunn's eel pies were priced by the tide: a bit on the flood, two on the ebb, when the eels were running fat. With his clock stuck at Slack, Gunn could not tell when to put up the price, and his customers could, and did.

"Fix it," he said, "and the pie is yours. Hot."

It took nine minutes. The tide hand had slipped on its arbor, where the clutch spring had gone, and I wedged it with one of Mrs. Pallitt's cotter pins and set the lunar train by the smell of the Brack. The hand swung round to Half-Ebb, shuddered, and went on, and Gunn took off his cap. The Row, which has learned to cheer at very small things, cheered.

I was halfway through the pie when a tall young man in a lime-green cravat came across the square like a heron wading. He was nineteen or so, with floppy yellow hair, a coat too thin for the season, and a nose chilblained to the colour of a boiled sweet. He was shivering, with great dignity, as a man shivers who has decided in advance not to.

"Miss Marchetti. I have come to inform you, with the greatest courtesy, that I intend to resume my place. Obviously, the post is mine."

I swallowed. "Casimir Vaszary."

"Admittedly, my withdrawal was sudden. A strategic withdrawal. Matters of scholarship, inevitably, and my uncle the Provost was consulted. But I am a Fellow-elect of the Charmery. I am qualified in glyphwork. You, if I may say so, oil hinges."

"Your gloves are in locker three," I said. "Rabbit-lined. So's the plum cravat."

His hand flew to his throat. "That is a very fine cravat."

"It has a knot like a bad decision."

"Do not mock the knot." He drew himself up. He was a head taller than I, and it made him look, if anything, more breakable. "It was a Charmery knot. It took me an hour."

"You're a pretty thing, Vaszary, I'll give you that. They'd put you on a biscuit tin."

"Dear girl—"

"Don't."

"Dear Miss Marchetti, you are shouting at a scholar in a pie-shop—"

"It's a pie-stall. I'm not shouting. And it's Odile." I put the pie down. "I counted the jaw. Two hundred and six rivets. Wray gave up at sixty, Boll at a hundred and nine, and you didn't count at all."

"I did not need to count. It is obviously—" He stopped, put a knuckle to his nose, and sniffed. "It is a very large number in any case."

"Why did you leave?"

"I told you. Scholarship."

"Why did you leave?"

"Nothing frightened me." He said it too fast. His face had gone the colour of a plate. "It is a tin dragon, Marchetti. It says nothing. It is, obviously, a tin dragon."

I put that away in the part of my head where I keep things I will want later.

The pie went cold in my hand.

I mean that it went cold. It did not cool. Between one bite and the next the heat left it, as if somebody had opened a door in the world. All round the square voices dropped. A baby began to cry in the thin flat way that babies cry when they are too cold to bother. Breath, which had been a faint smoke, turned into rope. I looked down Slake Street and watched the frost come up the standpipe at the end of it, from the cobbles upward, like a white weed: ten feet of fluted iron in four breaths, furring the brass cock, and the hair and eyebrows of the people nearest it.

It lasted, by the pawnbroker's window clock, which was the one clock in the Row that told the hour and stood between a tray of wedding rings and a fiddle with no strings, four minutes and ten seconds. Then it lifted. The warmth came back into my fingers like blood into a foot, and the pins and needles with it, and the whole square stood rubbing its hands and cursing in the soot-snow.

"That's the third this week," someone said, with the weary flatness of the poor. "It's the pipes. Always is, this time of year."

"That was a draw," said Casimir. He had gone quite still, with both hands in his armpits. "A heat-draw. A charm does it. You chalk it, and the thing it's chalked on—" He stopped. He looked at me with his eyes very round. "Obviously not. Nobody is drawing anything. Obviously."

I had no time to ask him. Pim was already off, as he is, in a straight line through the crowd with his satchel banging, crying "Frost on Slake! White at Drum Alley! A bit for the news!" As he went by the chestnut brazier his sleeve brushed a man in a bowler hat, and something small and folded passed from the one to the other as neatly as a coin at a conjuror's. The man in the hat did not look up. I saw it the way you see the edge of a page, and I was looking at the standpipe.

The Row had begun to shout the news from street to street. White at Tallow Steps. White at the Bridge. White at the foot of Drum Alley. I took out Pim's map and a stub of pencil, and because I can no more leave a pattern alone than a loose bolt, I marked every standpipe the Row said had frosted. Casimir had gone. I did not see him leave. For half an hour I went from mouth to mouth and on my own two legs, and by the end I had eleven marks.

Then I borrowed a yard of string from Mrs. Pallitt and tied one end round a cotter pin. I pushed the pin through the paper at the Cathedral's place on top of Ember Hill, laid the other end of the string against the first mark, and swung it round.

I counted again. Eleven. They sat on the string as evenly as rivets on a boiler seam, every one the same distance from the pin, in a ring as round as a drawn circle, and in the middle of the ring, where the point of the pin stood up through the paper, was Ember Hill.
