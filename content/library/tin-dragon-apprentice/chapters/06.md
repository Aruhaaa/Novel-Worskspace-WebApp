# The Reasonable Man

Bergamot reached the Cathedral a full minute ahead of him, which I would have found suspicious in anyone less well-mannered.

I was on my knees inside the doors with a bucket, because the Warden had decreed that if the Commissioner was coming the floor would be clean, and that I was the floor's only available cleaner. It was the tenth of November, a morning of low yellow cloud, and the soot-snow had been coming down since dawn in a way that made the nave windows look breathed on. The wicket opened. A gust of cold, a smell of orange peel and warm tea, and then Hartwell Gast came in with his hat in his hand.

I would like to say I disliked him on sight. I had the grounds. But he was the sort of man who walks into a cold room as if he had brought the weather with him and it was mild. He stopped on the mat and took off his boots without being asked. Under them his socks were silk, and darned at both heels. He caught me looking and winked.

"A boy never forgets a darn, my dear."

Behind him came Roux with a notebook, and two surveyors: Mr. Pettifer, with a chain wound over his shoulder like a bandolier, and Mr. Quane, who carried a brass level on a tripod and the expression of a man who has been told not to touch anything and means to obey. A boy brought up the rear with a staff.

"Warden!" Gast took Orlowe's hand in both of his and then, I saw, held on. "Good heavens, man. Your hands. Like a winter pump. We shall do something about that." He turned the hand over, glove and all. "I mean it. Nobody should be asked to give the city his hands."

Orlowe took it back like a man retrieving a letter. "Commissioner."

"And Mr. Mott." Fenwick had come in from the vestry with Dorcas on his shoulder, in his seven-colour waistcoat and an expression of deep alarm. Gast stopped dead in front of the pigeon. "Is that a herringbone? On a pigeon?" He said it with such reverence that Fenwick went pink to the top of his head. "Sir, you are an artist. My mother would have given you her best needle."

"Well, now," said Fenwick. "Well, now."

He shook everyone's hand: the surveyors', the boy's, mine. The grip was warm and dry and brief and sincere, and I disliked how well it worked.

Roux unrolled a plan on the trestle by the Bell. It was a drawing of the dragon from the side, forty feet of it, with dotted lines running through the neck and the hip and the root of the tail, like a butcher's chart.

"I will be plain, Warden," said Gast. "I would rather you heard it from me than from the newspapers." He stood by the rail with his hands clasped behind him, not touching it. I noticed that. So did the Warden. "A year ago a sack of coal in Gantry cost nine bits. This morning it costs fourteen. By Christmas, sixteen. A labouring man earns two crowns a day, and a family in Cinder Row burns, if it can afford to, a sack in three days. Last winter eleven children in the Row died of lung fever. I knew their names. I went to the burials."

I looked at the floor. My mother died of lung fever. It is a thing I have found: you think you have put it away, and then someone says it in a room.

"This creature," said Gast, with the plain affection of a man talking about a large old horse, "burns six tons a day. A third of its heat is lost to the clay between here and the Row. The Row, in return, receives the right to queue. I do not blame the engine. It was built in another century for a smaller city. But I ask you, Warden: how many winters must Cinder Row pay for a monument?"

"Commissioner—"

"I propose a better thing." He nodded to Roux, who unrolled a second plan: a squat iron stove with a glass eye in its door. "The Gast Cell. A stove of my own design, a quarter of the coal for twice the heat. A hundred of them, one at the head of every street in Gantry. The engine is eighty tons of good tin. We melt it. We pour the Cells. The Ember is divided among them, a spark to each. A warm kitchen within a hundred yards of every door in the city. No Tickets. No queue. The Hundred Hearths."

"And the pigeons?" said Fenwick.

"The pigeons," said Gast gravely, "shall have the Hall."

It was impossible not to like it. That was the trouble. I stood there with a wet cloth and ran the figures, as I do, and they were good figures: a sack of coal for three days, a stove for a street. Somewhere in a cellar on Drum Alley a child was burning a stair, and this man was offering her a hearth, and he meant it.

Orlowe had not moved. "The Ember doesn't divide, Commissioner. It isn't a coal."

"The Charmery assures me—"

"The Charmery has not stood here at midnight. Take its heat in spoonfuls and I will not answer for what wakes."

"Wakes?" said Gast, and smiled with real warmth. "Warden, you speak as if it were a bear."

Mr. Pettifer laughed, and then so did Mr. Quane, and Roux's pen moved. Orlowe did not laugh. I saw the brass fingers curl.

The door crashed open.

"WHAT IS HE SAYING?"

She was four foot ten if she was an inch, in a coat of black bombazine, with white hair in a bun held up by two chisels and, jammed to her ear, a brass trumpet the size of a hunting horn. Behind her in the doorway three Boiler-Wrights looked at the Warden's mat and decided not to follow. Ottoline Brandt, Guildmistress of the Wrights, seventy-one years old, crossed the nave in her boots, to the great distress of the slate, and rapped the plan with the trumpet.

"SCRAP!" she said. "HE WANTS IT SCRAP! ELEVEN HUNDRED STOKER POSTS, GAST, ELEVEN HUNDRED! A HUNDRED STOVES DON'T STOKE THEMSELVES AND THEY DON'T PAY GUILD DUES!"

"Mistress Brandt, I assure you the Guild shall build the Cells, at generous rates—"

"WE BUILD BOILERS! WE DO NOT BUILD TEAPOTS!"

The pigeons left the rafters in a single grey cloud. She went on for some time about her grandfather and the Founders' contracts and what she thought of the Commission's arithmetic, at a pitch that rattled the lamp-glass, and I watched the room, and I watched it go. The surveyors had begun to look at their boots. Roux had put down his pen. She was right, and she was ruining it: a shouting old woman defending her own pay, beside a man in darned silk socks who had buried children. Even Fenwick looked at the floor.

"Mistress Brandt," said Orlowe.

He did not raise his voice. He did the opposite. He spoke so quietly that we had to lean in to hear, and I saw that he did it on purpose, as you lower a lamp to bring a crowd close.

"Commissioner, I do not say you are wrong about the Row. I have carried its children up this hill in winter. I have seen the stairs burn. I say only that you ask me to melt a thing no one has ever understood, on the strength of a plan no one has tested." He looked at the surveyors, one and then the other. "Give it a winter. The Charter provides an Assay on the first of December, in public, with the Council watching. Let your engineers measure the Ember then, and let the figures speak. If you are right, you will be right in December. Between now and then the Row will be no colder than it is."

Nobody spoke. Mr. Pettifer, who had been drawing a dotted line through the dragon's neck, quietly blotted it with his sleeve.

"Warden," said Gast at last, "you are a persuasive man." He took off his pince-nez, polished them, and put them back. "Very well. We shall be sensible. The first of December. But I shall have my engineers at the Pit, and my measuring-tap in that flank, and I will not be turned from it."

"WHAT DID HE SAY?" shouted Ottoline.

"A WINTER, MISTRESS BRANDT," shouted Fenwick, and every pigeon that had begun to come down went up again.

"Miss Marchetti," said Gast in the doorway, pulling on his boots. "Might I borrow you for ten minutes? Mistress Brandt has been kind enough to give me luncheon at the Hall, and I should like a word with the young person who has to live with all this."

The Hall of Rivets stood across the top of Ember Hill square, a cliff of black stone with its door at the head of a stair, every step stamped with a rivet pattern and polished by two hundred years of Wrights' boots. I counted them. Thirty-one. Ottoline had stormed ahead, and her Guildsmen had followed with the look of men going to a funeral, and the great door stood a hand's width open behind her.

Gast stopped on the third step from the top and looked out over the roofs. It was quite a view. The soot-snow fell on all of it, evenly, without malice.

"Let me tell you something I don't tell Councillors," he said. "I was born in a cellar on Drum Alley. Eight of us. The winter I was seven, we burned the stairs. Plank by plank the stairs went into the stove, and by February we climbed in and out on a rope. My mother called every one of us 'my dear', because she hadn't the breath to spare for names." He turned his hat in his hands. "When I say it to you, it is only warmth. I have never meant it for less."

"I'd rather you used my name."

"Of course, my dear."

"Odile."

"Of course." He smiled, rueful, and I could not tell whether it was a joke. "You know, I'm told you are a young woman who understands how things work. So let me tell you how this works. The Guild is a great and proud body, and it owes the Commission a great deal of money. The Wrights borrowed to rebuild their foundries in the lean years. I have been patient. I am, as it happens, holding some collateral." He said it lightly, the way you mention an umbrella. "One does what one must."

I did not understand it. I filed it, as I file everything, under *things with a weight*.

"And I'm told you are in the Commission's debt yourself," said Gast. "A small matter, a barge. I should like to see that note erased. Not frozen. Erased." He let it sit there. "I ask very little. A report, now and again. What the Warden does at night. Who comes and goes. Whether the Cathedral is as quiet as he says. I am told no Commission man has been past that rail in nineteen years, and I don't like not knowing. Nobody who has been cold likes not knowing."

I looked at him. He looked back, kindly, the pince-nez catching the light. He was offering me three hundred and twenty crowns for a favour a decent person might do without blinking: tell me what the old man does at night. And I thought of the old man's hands. Of snapped chalk. Of a floor that said no.

"No," I said.

He nodded slowly, as if I had said something sensible about the weather. "Think it over, my dear," said Gast. "I'm not a hurried man." And he went down the steps, humming, and across the square to where his carriage waited at the kerb.

The door of the Hall of Rivets was still open a hand's width behind me. As I turned, something brass came poking out of the gap, a long cone, swivelling slowly this way and that like a sunflower looking for the sun. It found me. It stopped.

The door opened another inch. Ottoline Brandt looked up at me out of a face like a walnut, with her bun skewered by its two chisels.

"Don't," she said.

The door shut.

I told no one. At midnight I held the lamp and watched the Warden go the colour of putty, and at four, when the Cathedral clock had begun tapping its foot, I went down to the nave in my socks with a cup of tea I did not drink and stood below the great shut face.

"Are you awake?" I said, which had become our password.

"I am," said the floor. "I have a question, if I may."

"Go on."

"The man with the warm voice said a word. He said it to the others, and the old woman shouted it, and the room went still." The gravel slowed. "Scrap. What is scrap?"

I thought of lying. It would have been a kindness of a kind, the kind my father does, with a big laugh and the thimble going round. But it had asked, and nobody else in that building was going to tell it.

"It's what's left of a thing when nobody wants it to be itself any more," I said. "They break it up and melt it down and sell it by the pound. He means to do it to you."

For a while there was only the clock. Then, behind the ribs, as I watched, the Ember went down: one shade, the way a lamp goes when someone turns the key, and then a second, and stayed there.
