# Chalk and Chill

It took two of Orrin Gunn's eel pies, one apology and most of my self-respect to get Casimir Vaszary to teach me a charm, and I want it noted that the apology was the cheap part.

Since the night in the ribs the dragon had been telling me, a little at a time, because I asked a little at a time, where it felt things. The arm, which was a leg again. The jaw, which no longer hurt. And the left eye, under its lid of tin, which it described as a stone in the mouth: not painful, only cold, as cold as the patch under its heart. A mechanic who has been told that a part is cold wants to warm it. I could solder a copper spur and run it to the mains. I could not, I had worked out, do it without somebody noticing. A charm, on the other hand, is invisible, and the only man in Gantry who owed me a favour for being rude to me was nineteen years old and wore a cravat the colour of a tangerine.

He was waiting by the iron gate of the Charmery's practice yard at five o'clock on the twenty-second, with the expression of a man who has agreed to something in a weak moment and has regretted it in at least three tenses.

"Admittedly," said Casimir, "it is irregular. Candidates from the street are not instructed in glyphwork. It is, in point of fact, a rule."

"I'm not a candidate. I'm the Warden's apprentice."

"Obviously you are. I am the one who was *replaced*." He took the pie from me and bit it with injury. "This is cold."

"It's eel. It's meant to be."

The yard was a gravel square behind the lecture hall, walled in black brick, with a row of tin dummies along the north side, their arms held out like tailors' models waiting to be fitted. The Charmery lights its lanterns in green glass, a cold flame that gives light and not a particle of warmth, so that the whole place looked like the bottom of an aquarium. At the iron tables sat students in three scarves apiece, hot-bricks in their laps, bent over slates. Nobody looked up. In the Charmery, I was to learn, looking up is a sign that you have finished.

"Sit," said Casimir. "Observe."

He chalked a spiral on a slate, a tight one, with three cross-bars through it like the rungs of a ladder that had been wound up. Then he took a coil of solder wire from his sleeve and laid it along the chalk line, pressing it down with his thumb, a bead at a time, exactly as I had seen the Warden do at the Bell.

"A glyph is a pipe," he said. "The chalk says where the heat goes. The solder is the pipe. The caster is the boiler."

"That's a plumbing diagram."

"Crudely. Obviously. But yes." He flung the end of the cravat over his shoulder, which I think was not necessary to the process, and laid two fingers of each hand on the outer ring. "Both hands, never one. One finger is a pinhole and you scald yourself. Watch the slate."

I watched. A flake of soot-snow had landed in the middle of the spiral. Four seconds went by. The flake sat there, and thought about it, and went to water, and the water to a wisp. The slate under my own hand, six inches off, had turned as warm as a plate. The tip of Casimir's nose, which was already chilblained, went the colour of a boiled sweet, and his fingers went white to the second knuckle. He took them off the ring and shook them out, and tucked them under his arms with what I took to be a flourish.

"And that," he said, rather high, "was nothing."

"What does it cost?"

"Everything has a cost. Heat is not made, Marchetti, it is moved. The glyph does not warm the slate. It takes the warmth out of the caster and puts it in the slate. Every charm in the world is borrowed from the person who casts it. A pinch of heat for a pinch of warmth." He looked at his fingers. "The Chill is what you call it when you have borrowed too much."

I had a tin disc in my pocket the size of a crown piece, an offcut of the same plate as the eye-lid, which Fenwick had found me in the scrap bin and asked no questions about. I laid it on the slate and began.

I am not boasting if I say my lines were good. I laid the solder in the groove of the chalk as I would lay a gasket, bead by bead, tight, with no overlap, and Casimir watched me do it with an expression of increasing indignation. "That is," he said, "acceptably straight."

"Thank you."

"It was not a compliment."

I put two fingers of each hand on the outer ring.

It was like putting your fingers in someone else's mouth: warm at first, then wet, and then something took hold. It did not feel like heat leaving. It felt like heat being asked for, politely, by something that did not mean to stop asking. The disc in the centre of the spiral went from cold to cool to warm, and I thought *there*, that will do, a stone in a mouth would be glad of that. But the gauge in my head said warm was not yet the warmth that would reach through a lid and a glass, and I have never in my life left a pressure at ten when the right figure is twelve.

"Odile," said Casimir.

"A bit more."

"Admittedly I said a pinch."

"I'm nearly there."

"Odile." He had stopped being clever. "Look at your hands."

I looked. They were not mine. They were two grey things lying on a ring of wire, the colour of old candles, and I had no sense of them whatever. I could see that my fingers were there. I told them to lift, and they did not hear. The chalk had fallen out of my other hand a while before and I had not known. There was a ringing quiet in my ears, and the lanterns had gone a deeper green, and my mouth was full of the taste of pennies.

Casimir hit my forearm with the whole of his own, a flat, hard shove that knocked my hands off the ring. Then he was pulling a hot-brick out of the nearest basket, wrapped in flannel, and slapping it into my lap and folding my hands round it with his.

"Obviously," said Casimir, in a voice I had not heard from him, "you gave it a fist. *Sit down. Sit.* You are grey. Your mouth is grey, did you know that? Do not speak." And he sat on the bench beside me and took my hands out of the flannel, and rubbed them between his, briskly, like a man scouring a pan, with his face turned away toward the dummies.

Feeling came back the way it comes back after you have slept on an arm: a thousand pins, in order, and none of them kind. I said a word that I will not set down. Casimir said it was perfectly normal and I should say it again.

"How do you know when to stop?" I asked, when I could.

"You stop when you cannot feel the chalk. A good caster stops a finger before that. My uncle says the best are the ones who are a little cold all the time and have learned to like it." He kept rubbing. "The Chill is grey lips, dead hands, and then, if you are fool enough to go on, the cold-sick. Days of it. Shaking, and nothing will warm you. Students have been carried out of this yard on a door."

I thought about that. I thought about a man at a Bell with two bricks in his coat and frost in his moustache, giving this every midnight, a great deal more than a pinch, for nineteen years. I had nearly lost my hands on a coin-sized spiral. I did not know the figure for a Lulling, and I did not like that I didn't.

"Does it work?" said Casimir, grudgingly. He was looking at the slate.

The disc lay in its ring of solder, in the middle of the spiral. I picked it up with my fingers, which hurt. It was as warm as a bun, and it stayed warm, and I put it in my pocket against my ribs and felt it there like a small, steady animal.

"It works."

"Well, then. Admittedly it was inelegant." He cleared his throat. "Is it..." He stopped. He adjusted the cravat, took a long time about it, and said it to the knot. "Is it quiet at night? The Cathedral."

"Mostly."

"Good," said Casimir, and did not look at me, and talked for ten minutes without drawing breath about the Charmery's dining arrangements.

* * *

I walked home along the towpath because my fingers wanted to find out if they could. It was full dark, and the soot-snow was coming down so evenly that it seemed to be raised from the ground rather than lowered from the sky. Number Four Basin was where I had left it. *Marguerite's Patience* lay against the wall under the one lamp, seventy feet of tarred iron with a wheelhouse like a sentry-box and the Goose polished on the funnel, and from her cabin chimney came a thread of smoke and, as I came down the gangway, the sound of a cough.

My father coughs the way a pump coughs when it has lost its prime: a heavy dry heave, a pause, another. It went on for twenty seconds. I counted. Then the cabin door opened on lamplight and he stood in it, thin and stooped and enormously glad, wiping his hands on a rag, with the thimble swinging on its string at his chest. Grease under every nail. He is the only man I know who could stand in a doorway coughing a lung up and look as if he'd been waiting all day for a visitor.

"There she is," said Dario Marchetti. "The Cathedral's finest. Mind the step. Mind the other step. I've kippers."

"You've a cough."

"I've a draught. The draught has a cough." He took my face in both hands, which were cold, and looked at it, and let it go. "You're grey about the lips. Are they working you?"

"It's the weather."

"It's always the weather. Sit."

The cabin was nine feet by eleven and I knew where every thing in it was. The bunks, the table that folded into the wall, the Primus I'd re-jetted at ten, the stove with its kettle on the hob and above the hob the iron rack where we put anything we wished to be rid of. There was a paper standing against the kettle. Pink. I know that pink the way I know a rash.

I picked it up while he was getting plates down, and I read it twice, because it was short.

> THE FUEL COMMISSION OF GANTRY. NOTICE OF CALL. FINAL. To Dario Marchetti, bargeman, of Number Four Basin. The Note of Hand held by this Commission upon the vessel *Marguerite's Patience* and her Licence to Carry Fuel being in default of principal, the sum of THREE HUNDRED AND TWENTY CROWNS is hereby called. The indulgence granted upon the appointment of a kinswoman to the Cathedral post extends to interest only. Unless the principal be paid in full on or before the Long Night, the Licence stands forfeit and the vessel to follow. By order. A. Roux, Clerk, for the Commissioner. 11 November.

The eleventh. The day after I had said no on the steps of the Hall of Rivets.

"Ah," said my father behind me. The plates had stopped. "That. That's a Quarter-Day number, that is."

"It says Long Night."

"Long Night, then. A Long Night number. Festival of lamps." He gave the laugh, the big one, that I had loved all my life and could have hit with a spanner, and it turned halfway into a cough, and he held on to the table. "What better night to be put out?"

"Three hundred and twenty."

The thimble was going round on its string. The dent in the crown caught the lamp once, twice, three times. "Roux told you," he said.

"On the third. In his office. You'd said forty."

"Forty," said my father, "is a number a man can hold in his head." He sat down on the bunk, quite suddenly, as if somebody had cut a string. "A hundred and ninety of it is your mother. The Infirmary, and the doctor, and the coal that last winter when the bricks wouldn't do. I borrowed on the licence for the cylinder head, and then I borrowed against that to pay the borrowing. It isn't coal, though some of it was coal. I was going to tell you when I had a plan."

"Have you got a plan?"

"I've a kipper," said my father, and did not smile, and I saw what the joke had cost him.

We ate them off the paper with our fingers, because the plates were drying: smoked till they curled, from the stall under the tide-clock, with bread and the heel of a pat of butter. The stove ticked. The Basin water slapped the hull, and a long way off a lanternman was calling the hour. Neither of us said a word. I have had more eloquent silences, but not many.

There was a thing sitting inside me where I had put it, in with the others, under *things with a weight*. One signature on a paper in Gast's warm hand and the pink slip would burn and my father's cough would be only a cough. It would have cost me a report now and then, on a man whose hands were blue. I thought of that. I thought of the Pit, and a voice asking me what scrap was. I looked at the thimble, going round.

Twenty-nine days to the Long Night. I decided on the second kipper that I would say nothing to anyone and that I would win, and I had no idea what winning would be made of. A seized thing cannot be forced. You find the part that wants to give.

He had stopped eating. His hands lay on the table on either side of the paper, big-knuckled, oil-black in the cracks, and the nails were blue at the beds, the way they are every November, and he is always too busy being cheerful to notice. I reached across and took them in mine, palm to palm, and held them there, as I had held my mother's in the last winter, when she told me my hands were the only warm thing in the house and I, being twelve, believed her.

Mine were still pink from Casimir's brick. It was all the heat I had. I gave it to him, with no glyph and no solder and nothing to pay it back, and he looked at the table, and let me.
