# Inside the Ribs

The left foreleg seized at ten to nine on the night of the sixteenth, with a sound I can still produce on request: a long dry squeal, like a cartwheel dragged through a gate, then a clunk, then a hiss of steam from the shoulder that came out in precisely the wrong place.

Forty-one pigeons left the rafters in order of rank. The second coil shuddered from the rail to the jaw. The Ember flared a shade behind the ribs, and the Warden, who had been standing under the Bell's gantry with a cup of tea he was not drinking, turned so fast that his knee went off like a cap pistol.

I had been sweeping ash. I put the broom down slowly, as you do near a strange dog, walked as close to the rail as the rule allowed, and said to the floor, under cover of the hiss, "Where is it?"

"In my arm." The gravel was thin and quick; I had never heard it hurry. "It is like holding a stone, and being asked to hold it for longer."

"The shoulder?"

"I believe so. I cannot see it. I can only be it." A pause, in which the steam sang. "Thank you for asking."

The leg lay folded along the second coil, a limb as long as a punt pole, ending in claws like rake-heads. For as long as I had known them the claws had hung loose over the lip of the Pit. Now they had closed on the plate beneath like a fist round a coin.

Orlowe limped to the rail and stopped a hand's breadth short of it. I watched his lips go the colour of putty.

"Gland," I said. "Shoulder gland, if I've any sense. The packing's dried out or the follower's seized. It's the fault you get on a lock-gate paddle, only bigger."

"How long?"

"I haven't seen it. An hour. Two."

"You have until midnight." He was not looking at the clock. He was looking at the claws. "A thing in pain does not sleep. I lull it, and it lies there with the arm, and it does not sleep, and at three or four in the morning..." The moustache moved. He began again, further back. "It must be mended before the Bell. That is not a request."

"I know it isn't. You've got the voice."

"I have not got a..." He looked at me, and something happened at the corner of his face and was put away. "I do not go inside. The hatch is two feet by two and a half. My knee. You are small."

"I'm five foot three."

"You are small for a boiler." From him I took it as a compliment. "Mott goes with you. Nobody goes into the ribs alone, in case what you are mending needs a second pair of hands to pull you out by the ankles."

"I'd have asked for him."

"Boots," said Orlowe. "On."

I looked down at my socks.

"The tin on that side will take the skin off a sole. Boots. And the leather gloves from the shelf in the passage, the big ones. They are..." He turned and addressed the rest of it to the Bell. "I had them washed."

Fenwick arrived at a run, which for Fenwick is a kind of weather, with a bull's-eye lamp in one fist, a tin flask under his arm and Dorcas on his shoulder, who was handed back to the vestry in disgrace. He stood under the curled claws with his head tipped back. "Well, now," he said. "That's a nasty cramp."

The hatch was behind the shoulder in the plating of the second coil, eight feet up, reached by the ladder I had used on the jaw. It was a plate two feet by two and a half, bolted round with twelve nuts and painted over so often that the paint had a grain of its own. I took the nuts off with a box spanner. It took eleven minutes. Nobody spoke, and the whole time the foreleg ticked as it cooled, the way a stove does.

Fenwick tried the hatch with one leg, then the other, and then, with an expression of mild scientific interest, with both arms first. He went through in the end the way a cork goes into a bottle, with a pop and an apology, and his voice came back out of the dark, enormously pleased. "It's roomy once you're in!"

I went in after him.

The first thing was the heat. Not the Pit's heat, dry and slow, like a hand held close, but the heat of a boiler-house on a Monday: close, damp, tasting of hot oil and scorched felt. The second thing was the smell beneath it, which I did not place until I had taken three steps along the walkway. It was the Basin at low water, when the sluice is drawn and the mud shows. Mud and weed and old rope, in a dry boiler on top of a hill.

I filed it under *things that should not be in boilers*, which was becoming a deep drawer.

Fenwick turned up the lamp. We stood in a tube eight feet across that curved away out of sight on both hands, and it was made, rib by rib, the way I would have made it if anyone had ever handed me a dragon, a free hand and a lifetime. The ribs were hoops of tin plate a hair under three-sixteenths, flanged on both edges, two feet apart, every lap double-riveted at a two-inch pitch with a regularity that made my teeth ache. Underfoot ran a walkway of expanded metal, warm through my soles. Overhead, along the spine, ran the main, a copper pipe as thick as my thigh, lagged in felt and tarred cord, and off it down every rib went the branches, thinner and thinner, to the width of a thumb, so that the whole inside looked like a leaf held up to a lamp. Veins. Each junction wore a brass tag with a stamped number. I read 117, 118, 119, in a hand that had cared about its sevens.

"I held the lamp in here once," said Fenwick, low, at my elbow. "Year of the big pigeon. I thought it was the biggest church I'd ever stood in."

"And now?"

"Smaller," said Fenwick. "And bigger."

"Which way is the shoulder?" I said, and I did not say it to him.

The answer came up through the plate under my boots and in through my shinbones and the bones of my face, so that I heard it with my teeth. It was no longer gravel. It was gravel that had gone into a barrel and come out the other end larger and warm as a loaf. "Forward. Left at the third rib, where the walkway narrows. Mind the valve wheel at the height of your knee."

"Thank you."

"Fourteen," said the dragon.

"Fourteen what?"

"Your steps, since the hatch. You put your left foot down harder than your right."

"Always ask," said Fenwick comfortably, behind me. He had heard me put a question to the air and he took it, as he took everything, in good part. "My mother never went down a cellar without asking the cellar. What did it say?"

"Left at the third rib."

"That's what I'd have said."

It is a strange thing to be warned by a boiler about where it will bark your shin. I minded the wheel. The dragon counted me past it in a voice of private satisfaction: twenty-two, twenty-three. I asked it, as we went, if it would mind not doing that, and it said it did not mean to, that it was how it knew where it was, and that for two hundred winters it had known where everyone was by their feet. I told it to carry on.

At the ninth rib the walkway forked. The left branch ran on forward to the shoulder. The right dropped by a short ladder toward the belly, and at the far end of it, forty feet along the keel, behind a grille of bars as thick as my wrist, burned the Ember.

I have lit a good many fires. I had never met one that looked back. It hung in a chamber the size of a hogshead and turned slowly on itself, orange at the rim and whitening toward a core I could not look at, a stove seen through its own fire-bars, if the stove were the size of a hay-wain and had opinions. It lit our faces from below. Fenwick lowered his voice another notch without noticing.

Under the grille, where the glow ought to have lain across the floor-plate like a hearth rug, it did not. The light stopped a few yards short of the bars at an edge as clean as a cut. And on the plate beyond the edge, in a boiler that had not dropped below the boil in two hundred years, lay a patch of tin the size of a door, furred white.

Frost.

The cold came up the ladder to meet us. I felt my sweat go chill on the back of my neck.

"Well, now," said Fenwick, very softly. "That's new. That wasn't there the year of the big pigeon."

"What is the cold place?" I asked the floor.

"I do not know. It has been there as long as I have been awake. It is nearest to my heart and furthest from the warmth."

"Does it hurt?"

"No. It is only cold, like the place where a tooth was." It considered. "I have never had a tooth. I have heard of the place."

We took the left branch.

I found the first bolt the way I find most things, by walking into it. Where the walkway narrowed to a plank, one rib was thicker than the rest, and the lamp-hand caught the edge of a boss of hammered brass, and I looked down at a black bolt-head the width of a saucer, domed, set through the plate itself and run up tight under a collar. In its face, where any sensible engineer would have cut a slot or a hex, there was a hole.

It was round, with a notch cut from one side and a smaller notch opposite, and when I held the lamp to it there were wards inside: little steps of them, one behind another, going down into the dark. A keyhole.

I have turned ten thousand nuts. A nut wants a wrench and does not care whose. This wanted one tool with one bit, cut for it and for nothing else, and I stood with my thumb on the lip of it and the unreasonable conviction that I was being looked at.

It was cold. I put my glove to it, then my bare hand, and snatched the hand back. A plate at a hundred and eighty degrees, and a bolt in the middle of it as cold as a flagstone in a cellar.

"How many of these are there?" I said.

"Five," said the dragon, with the air of a creature who has had a long time to count them. "One in my skull, behind the hinge of the jaw. One in my tail. One in each flank. And one under my heart, in the cold."

I put the lamp across the tube. On the opposite wall, at the same rib, there it was: another, and not the same. The notch on the other side; a third where the first had two. "They're different."

"No two are alike."

I took out my book, the little oilcloth one where I keep torque figures, and drew both, notches and wards, as best I could in that light. Fenwick held the lamp steady and asked nothing. The dragon said nothing either while I drew, which was tact, and I have not forgotten it.

I did not ask what they were for. I want that set down, because it is the only time in my life I have declined to ask a machine how it works. I knew the size of the answer, if not its shape, and a question put to that voice gets answered. I shut the book.

"Well, now," said Fenwick at last, to the bolt. "Those are keyholes. A man doesn't put keyholes in a boiler to keep the damp out."

"No," I said, and turned the lamp away from them, as you turn it from a sleeping face.

The gland was twelve feet on, where the walkway ended in a ledge under the shoulder joint: a bronze stuffing-box the size of a pie dish with six studs, all of them furred green, and a thread of steam leaking sideways from the packing, thin and hot as a wire. The follower had seized. Two hundred years of verdigris had welded it to the box. It is the commonest fault there is, and I have never been so glad to see one.

I gave it what it was owed first. Penetrating oil on the studs. Then the nuts eased a flat at a time, in rotation, the way you slacken a cylinder head, with sweat running into my eyes and the gloves too big and the steam-thread singing past my ear. The follower stayed where it was. The dragon counted my breaths, softly, without being asked, and after a while I found I was breathing to its count.

Then I took the twelve-inch spanner by its jaws and set the handle against the follower.

"Excuse me," I said, to the gland, to the dragon and to Fenwick, who nodded gravely. "First knock introduces you."

I tapped. "Second makes your case."

I tapped. "Third is a bluff."

The third knock landed, and the gland let go with a sigh like a man putting down a trunk. The follower turned under my fingers.

"Which part," said the dragon, "is the bluff?"

"All of it. That's what makes it work."

Far off in the nave, through three inches of tin and forty feet of ribs, came a long scrape and a clack, the sound of a man opening a hand he has held shut for a week. The steam-thread died. I tightened the studs, crosswise, two flats at a time, and repacked the box with graphite yarn out of my belt, and when I had done and wiped my hands the dragon said in a small private voice, "It is a leg again."

"It was always a leg."

"I had begun to think it was something I carried."

"Well, now," said Fenwick, wiping his face with his cap. He had heard nothing, but he had heard the claws. "That'll be the cramp gone. Soup?"

It was a quarter past ten. We had an hour and three-quarters in hand and the best part of the Warden's temper. We sat on the plank with our boots hanging over the keel-plate and passed the cup between us: barley, mutton, and something he calls a secret and I call mace. It was hot enough to sit inside a dragon and drink, and it was the best thing I had eaten in a week, and I include the last one.

"Thirty-one years I've fed the thing," said Fenwick, to the cup, "and never once been in to say thank you." He lifted his face to the roof of the ribs, the lamplit tin, the veins. "Thank you, I'm sure. In case anyone's listening."

I looked at the floor. "I think it heard," I said.

*He is welcome,* said the dragon, only for me. *I count his soup. The smell comes through the wall.* I let that stay between me and the tin.

Fenwick set the lamp down on the walkway between us, low, to pour, and the light, which had come at the ribs head on all evening, now came across them. Every machinist knows that trick. Hold the lamp low and a scribed line you cannot see by day jumps out of the metal like a vein on a hand. I was looking at the rib beside the bolt only because there was nothing else to look at, and I was reading it before I knew that it was writing.

The letters were no bigger than barleycorns, scratched with a nail or a scriber by someone lying on his side with his cheek against the plate, which was the only way to make them at that height and in that light. The hand was cramped and slow, with a long tail on the K.

T. HASK. FORGIVE ME.

I know the name. Everyone in Gantry knows the name. It is on the foundation stone and the Charter and, in small type, on the back of every Hearth Ticket ever printed: *Founded in the Kindling by T. Hask.* The Guilds say he built the dragon, and the city round it for company. And the man who built the dragon had lain down in the dark in the last hour of the work, or the first hour of something else, and asked someone's pardon in letters you could read only from the floor.

Fenwick had followed my eyes. He leaned in, a vast shadow behind the lamp, and read it aloud, slowly, with his lips going, the way he reads recipes. "T. Hask. Forgive me."

He took off his cap. He held it against his waistcoat in both hands, and for the first time since I had known him Fenwick Mott said nothing at all. Not "Well, now." Not anything. Far along the keel the Ember turned on itself. The cold place breathed its thin cold breath at our backs.

"What does forgive mean?" said the floor.
