# The Marquess's Misprint: Story Bible

For the writers only. Readers never see this file.

## Logline and premise

**Logline.** When a wet-copy blunder in a respectable newspaper announces that the Marquess of Stanmere is engaged to a printer's daughter, the compositor who made the error and the nobleman who finds it convenient agree to let it stand for one Season, and discover that a lie in print is much harder to retire than a lie in conversation.

**Premise, in order, including every major reveal.**

1. *The error (Wednesday 5 to Thursday 6 June 1816).* The Marquess of Stanmere, hunted by matchmaking mothers (above all Lady Quarles, who has put it about that he will offer for her daughter Verity), sends his secretary Mr Minton to the *Morning Gazette* with a paragraph denying the rumour. The *Gazette* sub-contracts its inner forme (pages two and three: Fashionable Intelligence and advertisements) to Barnaby Pargeter's printing house in Hand and Pen Court, off Paternoster Row. That night the roof leaks onto the wet copy; the apprentice Toby Mudge trips over the shop cat and drops a page of type (it is "pied"); and Winifred Pargeter, Barnaby's daughter, 26, resets it from a blotted slip and a heap of mingled lines, in a hurry, with the gouty Barnaby abed and the *Gazette*'s reader absent. Two lines of one measure have fallen together: the denial's name-line and a line from Winifred's own advertisement for fair copying. The *Gazette* appears with an announcement, in the *Gazette*'s own phrase "authorised", that the engagement of the Marquess to "Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row" is "entirely well founded". At 4.40 a.m. Winifred reads the first dried sheet, sees the error with eleven hundred copies printed and the cart in the yard, and understands that she can escape Clause 9 only by breaking Clause 6: stopping the cart means a reprint, a delivery three hours late, and a second breach in the quarter (the first was the rain-bound cart of 11 May), which gives Mr Underhill, who has been looking for cause since Lovejoy began courting him, the right to void the contract. She lets it go. That is her secret: she knew before the cart left, and chose.
2. *The temptation.* Julian, Marquess of Stanmere, 31, reads the paragraph at breakfast and for ten minutes does nothing, while the morning's visiting cards (the mamas withdrawing) pile up on the salver. His aunt Lady Constance Dyer, who has run his family since his father died, tells him he will do nothing, and he finds that he agrees. Julian is also, secretly, "A Kentish Farmer", whose nine letters on the Corn Law, the poor-rate and the labourer's bread have run in the *Gazette* since September 1815 and been set in type by Pargeter's. Minton carries the manuscripts in a plain cover to Mr Ingram, the *Gazette*'s reader; the proofs come back by the same road with tart queries in the margin signed "W.P.", and Julian has kept every one. Winifred has never been told the author's name.
3. *The Articles (Friday 7 June).* Julian calls at Hand and Pen Court. Winifred owns the error. He proposes, instead of a correction, "stet" (let it stand) until Michaelmas, Sunday 29 September: he gets peace from the mothers and time for his Kent work, the Pargeters escape the *Gazette*'s Clause 9 (any error requiring correction voids the contract and forfeits a £500 bond), and Winifred will have the sole right to end the engagement, by jilting him. She sets seven Articles of Agreement in type on the spot. One forbids any attachment of the affections. Both sign.
4. *The Season.* Lady Constance guesses the truth in a minute and decides it is to be done well. Winifred becomes a sensation by refusing to lie aloud: asked if she is engaged she says "It is in the *Gazette*", and society takes this for discretion. Julian takes up the same formula. The rival printer Mr Hector Lovejoy, who prints the scandal sheet *The Town Crier* and holds £740 of Barnaby's notes, works out from a cracked capital W exactly what happened and decides to wait. Lord Rupert Tremayne, Julian's old schoolfellow and a charming debtor, courts Winifred. The Dowager Duchess of Ormsgill commands a dinner. The wooden press, the Old Lady, runs away down Ludgate Hill with Julian clinging to it. At the dinner Winifred outshines everyone.
5. *The midpoint (night of 4 July).* After the dinner Winifred tells Julian her secret (she saw it and let it go); Julian shows her the pocket-book of her marginal queries and she realises he is the Farmer. They kiss under the archway where the cart went. She will not say yes while her father's notes are Lovejoy's: "Ask me at Michaelmas, when I don't need you." Julian agrees. From here on they are not pretending to be in love; they are pretending not to be.
6. *Kent (30 July to 21 August).* A house party at Stanmere Court in the drowned Weald. Winifred sees Julian among his tenants and the hungry labourers, and prints the Weald Relief Subscription on the Old Lady in the estate office. Rupert proposes to her (honestly, as a business arrangement) and is refused. Agnes Fell, Lady Constance's timid companion, is secretly "Peregrine Pry", the Crier's wickedest columnist, paid by Lovejoy, who has quietly bought up her brother Matthias's debt, to keep him out of the King's Bench prison; on the night of 3 August she copies Winifred's counterpart of the Articles, left out of its case, and she is under pressure from Lovejoy. The Medway floods; the household rescues a family from a hop-kiln roof; Winifred prints a warning handbill in forty minutes. At dawn, exhausted, Agnes posts the Articles to Lovejoy.
7. *The low point (Saturday 31 August).* The Crier prints the Articles in full, with Pry's commentary and the carter's story that Winifred saw the error and let the cart go. Mr Underhill, the *Gazette*'s editor, ends the contract and forfeits the bond; Lovejoy's notes (now £1,140, because he has bought another creditor's account) fall due on demand. Julian arrives and offers a special licence on Monday. Winifred refuses to be an erratum corrected by matrimony. She then learns he has already tried, behind her back, to buy her father's notes from Lovejoy (on Tuesday 27 August; Lovejoy refused; Julian had found at three that morning that Article 3 forbade lending but not buying, and by four hated the argument; the call is also why Lovejoy prints on the 31st and not at Michaelmas), which broke the one Article she cared about. She tells him she will keep the Articles to Michaelmas and does not wish to see him.
8. *The turn (1 to 12 September).* Lady Constance confesses to Julian that she burned twenty-one yearly letters his mother sent him from Florence (addressed to Constance, to give him), on his father's orders and then from habit, and gives him the last, of March 1816, four lines long, which ends "The door is open". Julian sees that his arranging of other people is his aunt's burning of letters in a better coat. He writes to his mother at last, and writes his own erratum: the tenth Farmer letter, to be signed with his name. Agnes confesses, is forgiven in part, and brings Winifred Lovejoy's next stroke (a second Crier number on 28 September with the carter's affidavit). Verity Quarles arranges a loan from her father, Sir Ephraim, at six per cent against the Albion press, with no marquess in it.
9. *The Saturday number (24 to 28 September).* Mrs Keziah Rudge, the *Gazette*'s elderly Quaker proprietor, returns from Bath on the strength of Winifred's letter of 3 September, hears Winifred confess in full, strikes Clause 9 from the contract and orders the *Gazette*'s first erratum in nineteen years, to be set by Winifred and signed by her, and the Farmer's tenth letter beside it, and the *Gazette* to reach the streets an hour before the Crier. On the night of Friday 27 September the whole cast gathers at Hand and Pen Court. The roof lets the rain in again; the Albion's toggle collar cracks and Julian's signet ring is wedged in it to hold; Lovejoy arrives with a bailiff and a demand under his acceleration clause (the bailiff may not touch the press before sunrise; the stroke is delay), is paid in full in coin by Winifred out of the Quarles loan, and lingers only to tell Winifred her word-spacing is uneven. The cart goes at 4.15 with Winifred on the seat. At dawn Julian, having asked Barnaby's leave, asks Winifred properly, and she says yes.
10. *The proper way.* The banns are read three Sundays in St Vedast, Foster Lane, and St George's, Hanover Square. Lovejoy attends and gives Winifred a new cast capital W. Julian's mother arrives from Florence. They marry on Thursday 17 October. In the register the clerk spells her name "Pargiter". Julian reaches for the pen to correct it; Winifred says "Stet." One error stays on the record by choice.

## World and setting

**The year.** 1816, the "year without a summer", though no one in the book knows why. (Tambora erupted in April 1815; the characters say only that the weather is a scandal.) Constant cold rain from May through the autumn; sunsets turn a strange copper-red; the hay rots in the fields, the wheat comes in late and thin, the hops mould on the bine. Wheat stands at seventy-odd shillings the quarter and rising; a quartern loaf is a shilling or more. The town is still chewing over the Separation of Lord Byron, who left England in April, and over the Regent's follies; Princess Charlotte was married on 2 May; the Elgin Marbles are being bought for the nation; the income tax has just been abolished (March) and gentlemen are cheerful about it; Parliament rises on 2 July. Waterloo was a year ago, and its anniversary (18 June) is celebrated in the wet.

**Map in words.**

- *Paternoster Row* runs east to west just north of St Paul's Cathedral, a narrow street of booksellers, so dark with overhanging upper storeys that the shop lamps burn at noon in winter. Its west end opens onto *Ave Maria Lane*, which runs south to Ludgate Hill. *Hand and Pen Court* is a cobbled slot off the north side of the Row near the west end, entered through a brick arch eight feet wide, with a pump in the middle. At the end of the court stands **Pargeter's**, the printing house: three storeys of sooty brick under a slate roof with a bad slope.
- *Pargeter's, inside.* Ground floor front: the composing room (two rows of type frames with the cases on them, the imposing stone in the middle under the one skylight, the proofing press, the speaking-tube Barnaby has rigged up to his bedroom). Ground floor back: the press room, with the **Albion** (iron) and the **Old Lady** (wooden common press), a trough for wetting paper, ink slabs, stacks of damp paper under boards. First floor: Barnaby's bedroom and the parlour. Second floor: Winifred's room, Mrs Hobday's, the wet-paper loft with the drying lines. Basement: kitchen. The roof leaks over the imposing stone. The household calls the leak **the Drip**, and it has a place at every meal.
- *Ludgate Hill* runs down from the west front of St Paul's to Ludgate Circus and Fleet Street. It is steep enough that a loaded dray needs a drag chain.
- *Pomfret Yard* (fictional) lies off the north side of Fleet Street, about six-tenths of a mile from the court: the *Morning Gazette*'s office, its own press room, its counting-room. A cart from Hand and Pen Court takes twelve minutes, in rain twenty.
- *Ave Maria Lane*: Lovejoy's printing house, the Phoenix Press (fictional), a long building with a fine shopfront and a counting-room above. *The Town Crier* is printed there on Friday nights for Saturday morning.
- *Curzon Street, Mayfair*: **Stanmere House**, the Marquess's London house, tall, cold, correct. About two and a half miles from Paternoster Row: fifty minutes on foot, thirty-five by hackney in rain, longer in a crush.
- *Hertford Street*: Lady Constance's narrow house, three minutes' walk from Curzon Street.
- *Ormsgill House, Piccadilly*: the Dowager Duchess's great house with a ballroom roof that leaks.
- *Bankside, Southwark*: the Quarles Black Bull Brewery, across London Bridge. Smell of malt and horses.
- *Stanmere Court*, Kent: a red-brick Elizabethan house with a Georgian front, near Yalding, in the low Weald of the Medway, thirty-eight miles from London by the Maidstone road over Wrotham Hill. With the roads as they are that summer: eleven hours by private carriage with changes, longer with a loaded cart. The house stands on a slight rise; below it are the water meadows between the Medway and its tributary the Beult, then hop gardens and oast-houses (round brick kilns with white cowls), then the little church of St Peter and St Paul at Yalding with its long old bridge. Estate: eleven farms, a steward's office in the old brewhouse (where the press is set up), a great tithe barn.
- *St Vedast, Foster Lane* (Wren's church, a few minutes from the Row): the Pargeters' parish church and the wedding church. *St George's, Hanover Square*: the Marquess's parish.

**Rules of the trade.** (There is no magic. These are the rules.)

- *The* Morning Gazette *comes out Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday mornings*, four pages on one sheet, price sevenpence (fourpence of it stamp duty), circulation about eleven hundred. Its own press room prints the outer forme (pages one and four: news and market prices) the day before. Pargeter's prints the **inner forme** (pages two and three) the same night so that Fashionable Intelligence is fresh: copy arrives at Hand and Pen Court by five in the afternoon of the day before (Monday, Wednesday, Friday); composing runs from six to about midnight; the formes are locked up by one at the latest; printing runs from half past one to about four; the wet sheets are carted to Pomfret Yard at quarter past five for folding and dispatch by six; hawkers are on the streets by seven. The Albion pulls about 250 an hour with two men, the Old Lady about 150; together they do eleven hundred copies in under three hours.
- *The Albion.* The historical Albion press is of c. 1820; in this book **the Albion** is a one-off iron-framed hand press built in 1813 to Barnaby's design by his cousin Ebenezer Pargeter, a Clerkenwell smith, on the lines of Lord Stanhope's iron press, with a toggle-and-lever action that pulls easily. It is named after the shop sign Barnaby bought with it, the Albion's Head. Rival printers call it "Barnaby's folly". Never call it the first Albion; call it "the Albion" and let it be Barnaby's own.
- *The Old Lady.* Wooden common press, c. 1770, ink-ball inking, screw and bar, built like a gallows. She is pledged to Lovejoy by a bill of sale for £60 until Winifred redeems her (chapter 9).
- *Clause 9 of the Gazette contract.* "Any Error of the Press in the Gazette requiring a Correction to be printed in a later number shall render this Contract void and the Bond of £500 forfeit." The *Gazette* has never printed a correction in its nineteen years: Mr Underhill's pride, and the reason the clause exists.
- *Clause 6 of the Gazette contract.* "Delivery at Pomfret Yard by a quarter to six; a cart arriving after six shall be a breach, and a second breach in any quarter shall void this Contract at the Proprietor's option." The cart was late once, on 11 May, in the rain. Mr Underhill has been waiting for the second.
- *Lovejoy's notes.* £740 of Barnaby's paper (money borrowed 1814-15 to buy the Albion and paper), payable at Michaelmas, with a clause making the whole payable on demand if Barnaby loses the *Gazette* contract. Once triggered the clause stands, even if the contract is later restored. A creditor is not bound to accept payment before a note falls due, and Lovejoy declines it. In August Lovejoy buys a further £400 account from the paper-merchant Mr Trigg: £1,140 in all.
- *Libel and the imprint.* Every printed sheet must bear the printer's name and address. A false paragraph is a risk to the printer, not only the paper.
- *Banns.* Banns are read on three Sundays in the parishes where both parties live; the marriage follows, in the morning, between eight and noon. A special licence is for people who are in a hurry and can pay.
- *Engagement customs.* A lady does not say she is engaged until the gentleman has asked her father; a gentleman who announces his own engagement in a newspaper is vulgar; a lady who has been jilted is pitied, a gentleman who has been jilted by a printer's daughter is envied by no one but is, strangely, forgiven.
- *Forms of address.* Julian: "my lord" to servants and tradesmen, "Lord Stanmere" in society, "Stanmere" to men of his set, "Julian" only to Constance, Rupert, later Winifred. Constance is "Lady Constance" (a marquess's daughter) and "Aunt Constance" to Julian. Winifred is "Miss Pargeter", "Win" to her father only.
- *Anachronism guard.* Never write "fiancé" (say "betrothed", "intended"), "scientist", "okay", "boyfriend", "teenager", "weekend", "stressed", "lobbyist". Lighting is candle, rushlight, oil lamp and, in the better streets, gas (Pall Mall since 1807; not Hand and Pen Court). Rollers for ink exist (Barnaby uses them on the Albion) but the Old Lady is still inked with balls.

**Glossary of recurring terms, objects and customs.**

1. **Composing stick**: the hand-held tray in which a compositor sets lines. Winifred's is her late mother's, of brass, worn bright at the thumb-rest.
2. **Case, upper and lower**: the two sloping trays of type; capitals live in the upper case.
3. **Sort**: a single piece of type. "Out of sorts" is meant literally in the shop.
4. **Quad, em, en, space**: the blank pieces that set the width of lines and the gaps between words.
5. **Forme**: type locked in an iron frame (**chase**) with wedges (**quoins**), ready for the press.
6. **Page cord**: the string tied round a page of type so it can be moved. Toby's dropped page had a loose cord.
7. **Galley**: a long tray to hold set type.
8. **Pie / pi**: type spilled and mixed. "A page in pie."
9. **Imposing stone**: the flat slab on which formes are made up. The Drip falls on it.
10. **Bodkin**: the printer's awl for lifting a letter in a locked forme.
11. **Proof**: a trial impression for the reader to correct. **Reader**: the proofreader. The *Gazette*'s reader is Mr Ingram, ill that week.
12. **Dele** (take out), **stet** ("let it stand", the mark that cancels a correction), **erratum / errata** (a printed correction slip). The book's three key words.
13. **Imprint**: the printer's name at the foot of a sheet.
14. **Ink balls**: leather pads stuffed with wool, used to beat ink onto type on the Old Lady.
15. **Tympan and frisket**: the frames that carry the paper onto the type. **Platen**: the plate that presses. **Toggle**: the Albion's jointed lever gear. **Bar**: the lever handle on a hand press.
16. **Pull**: one impression. "Two hundred pulls an hour."
17. **Chapel**: the workmen's society in a printing house, with its own customs; its head is the **Father of the Chapel** (Mr Orme). Chapel business is not told outside.
18. **Printer's devil**: the apprentice (Toby), who carries things and is blamed for them.
19. **The Crooked W**: a capital W in the Pargeter fount with a nicked left foot, a small flaw that prints as a hairline gap. It is in Winifred's advertisement line and in the Misprint.
20. **The Drip**: the roof leak above the imposing stone.
21. **The Articles**: the seven Articles of Agreement, printed on a single foolscap sheet, two signed counterparts.
22. **Hand and Pen**: the court's name and the sign of Pargeter's trade, a hand holding a pen.
23. **A rout**: a large crowded party. **Almack's** and the Patronesses exist but are offstage.
24. **A quartern loaf**: the four-pound loaf, whose price is the nation's mood.
25. **The Quarter Day**: Michaelmas (29 September), when rents and notes fall due.
26. **Great Primer**: a large type size (about 18 point), used for the Erratum.
27. **The Farmer's letters**: the nine (later ten) signed "A Kentish Farmer".
28. **Forfeits**: parlour games in which a loser pays a silly penalty.
29. **Pattens**: iron-ringed overshoes for wet streets.
30. **"It is in the Gazette"**: Winifred's formula, not a joke but a principle; it becomes everyone's.

## Cast

**1. Winifred Pargeter** ("Miss Pargeter"; "Win" only to her father), 26, born February 1790. Compositor and de facto manager of Pargeter's.
- *Physical, never to change:* small and square-shouldered (five feet four), dark hair always pinned with a brass composing rule through the knot like a hairpin; a permanent blue-black stain on the nail and ball of her left thumb that gloves cannot hide; strong forearms and the quick light hands of someone who picks up small objects for a living. She smells faintly of lye (type is washed in it).
- *Speech:* quick, dry, short sentences; she counts and measures things aloud ("that is a long line, it wants breaking"); answers a hard question by stating a fact; never swears, says "Lord" and "Heavens" and, when angry, goes extremely polite. She will not lie aloud. *Example:* "I do not say it cannot be done. I say it cannot be done by Thursday."
- *Wants:* to keep her father's press and to be taken as what she is, a printer. *Fears:* that her quickness is a danger to the people she loves; that she is a person who ruins things and then makes a joke. *Secret:* she saw the misprint at 4.40 a.m., before the cart left, and chose not to stop it. *Arc:* from hiding behind her father's name and her own wit to putting her own name on the record, in Great Primer; from "dele" (cut it out and move on) to "stet" (let some things stand, including love and her own flaws).

**2. Julian Stanmere, 7th Marquess of Stanmere** ("my lord"; "Stanmere"; Julian to Constance, Rupert, Winifred), 31, born 1785. Inherited at 24.
- *Physical, never to change:* tall (six feet one) and slightly stooped from reading; sandy hair cut shorter than fashion; a nose broken at Harrow in a dispute over a Greek verb; a white notch in his left eyebrow from a cricket ball; pale grey eyes; long hands. He turns his heavy gold signet ring (the Stanmere griffin) on his finger when he is thinking.
- *Speech:* dry, understated, exact; answers vague questions with "Which?" and numbers; says "Quite" when he means "No"; corrects other people's signboards under his breath; humour so deadpan that servants take a minute to find it. *Example:* "I have never been engaged to anyone. I find I am not good at it, and I have been at it for a week."
- *Wants:* peace, his estate, and the Farmer's work heard. *Fears:* becoming his father (a marriage as a long quarrel conducted at dinner in front of a child); making a promise he cannot keep. *Secret:* he is "A Kentish Farmer"; and he has never admitted, even to himself, how much he minds that his mother (he believes) never wrote to him once in twenty-two years. *Arc:* from letting things stand (passivity: the *stet* of his whole life) to acting in the open (public erratum, his own name on the Farmer's letters, a wedding by banns); from arranging things for people to asking them.

**3. Lady Constance Dyer** ("Lady Constance"; "Aunt Constance"), 64, Julian's paternal aunt, widow of General Sir Hugh Dyer (d. 1811).
- *Physical:* tall, rigid, black bombazine always with a purple ribbon at the throat; an ear-trumpet of tortoiseshell she does not need but uses to avoid conversation; a lorgnette; a one-eyed pug called **Blücher** under her arm.
- *Speech:* pronouncements, not conversation: short, no hedging; refers to everything as "a question of arithmetic". *Example:* "I do not ask. I inform."
- *Wants:* Julian married, and the family name safe. *Fears:* dying having failed him. *Secret:* for twenty-one years (1795-1815) she burned the yearly birthday letter from Julian's mother Cecilia in Florence (first at the 6th Marquess's order, then from habit); the one of March 1816 she could not burn and keeps in her desk. *Arc:* from controller to confessor to ally; at last she gives Julian the letter and lets him choose.

**4. Barnaby Pargeter** ("Mr Pargeter"; "Barnaby"), 59. Master printer, Winifred's father.
- *Physical:* stout, bald with a white fringe, spectacles pushed up on his forehead and forgotten there, a gouty left foot wrapped in flannel and propped on a stool, ink in the creases of his hands.
- *Speech:* long cheerful digressive sentences full of trade jokes; calls everyone "my dear fellow", including the Marquess; breaks off mid-sentence when a thought about machinery arrives. *Example:* "My dear fellow, you are holding it like a bishop holds a hot potato; the thing wants a firmer grip and, I think, a little prayer."
- *Wants:* his daughter safe and the Albion running. *Fears:* the debtors' prison, and his wife's opinion of him. *Secret:* the debts to Lovejoy are worse than Winifred knows, and he signed the *Gazette* bond with the house as security. *Arc:* from letting his daughter carry him to carrying his own part; the one who asks the Marquess the proper question.

**5. Mr Hector Lovejoy**, 48. Printer of the *Town Crier*; owner of the Phoenix Press, Ave Maria Lane. Rival.
- *Physical:* small, neat, brown snuff-coloured coat, hands scrubbed pink, a magnifying glass on a ribbon at his waistcoat, two pairs of spectacles pushed up. He was Barnaby's apprentice from 1783 to 1790.
- *Speech:* soft, courteous, formal; "my dear sir", "madam"; counts out loud when handling money. *Example:* "I never print a lie, madam. I print a true thing in a place where it will do the most harm."
- *Wants:* the *Gazette* contract and respect from the trade, which he has never had. *Fears:* being thought a gutter printer. *Secret:* he has not made a typographical error in thirty years, and cannot forgive carelessness, least of all in himself. *Arc:* defeated cleanly; keeps his honour as a craftsman: corrects Winifred's word-spacing in the middle of his own downfall, and later sends her a new W.

**6. Lord Rupert Tremayne** ("Rupert", "Lord Rupert"), 29, second son of the Earl of Tremayne. Julian's schoolfellow at Harrow.
- *Physical:* lanky, russet-gold curls, a long amused mouth, a waistcoat always slightly too beautiful; he tosses a guinea when thinking.
- *Speech:* fast and light, "my dear girl", "my dear Stanmere", comparatives ("more sorry than I look and less sorry than I should be"). *Example:* "I have never been refused by anyone I liked. I find it extremely interesting."
- *Wants:* to be out of debt without working. *Fears:* being his father's joke. *Secret:* he owes £2,300 in all, £800 of it to Lovejoy, who has asked him for a favour; he does not give it. *Arc:* from charming parasite to a man who learns the brewery business; marries Verity on her terms.

**7. Miss Agnes Fell**, 41, companion to Lady Constance for twelve years; secretly "Peregrine Pry", columnist of the *Town Crier*.
- *Physical:* thin, long-necked, hair going grey in one white streak at the left temple, always in mittens (to hide an ink stain on her right middle finger and a callus from the pen); she keeps a night-candle burning until two.
- *Speech:* soft, apologetic: "I beg your pardon", "I may be quite wrong, but"; yet she writes like a razor. *Example:* "I beg your pardon, but I think the turtle is not dead."
- *Wants:* to be read under her own name; to keep her brother out of prison. *Fears:* poverty, being found out, her brother dying in the King's Bench. *Secret:* Pry; she copied the Articles and sold them. *Arc:* from invisibility through betrayal and confession to a signed column in the *Gazette*, "A. Fell".

**8. Miss Verity Quarles** ("Verity"), 22, daughter of Sir Ephraim Quarles, brewer, baronet.
- *Physical:* tall, freckled, large capable hands, spectacles on a chain for reading figures, a brown dress she wears too often.
- *Speech:* blunt, numerate, kind in a flat way. *Example:* "It's a perfectly good brewery. It wants a better man at the top, and I am not allowed to be one."
- *Wants:* to run the brewery. *Fears:* being married for it. *Secret:* she keeps the brewery's real books and found that Papa's partner Mr Halloran has been skimming. *Arc:* friend to Winifred; arranges the loan; marries Rupert on terms.

**9. Tobias "Toby" Mudge**, 13, printer's devil at Pargeter's.
- *Physical:* freckled, ears that stick out, always chewing; he wears a paper hat on press nights.
- *Speech:* blurts, non sequiturs, the truth at the wrong moment; calls the Marquess "your Marquessship". *Example:* "Is it true gentlemen don't eat pies? Only I've been told it and I can't make it hold together."
- *Wants:* pies, and to be a compositor. *Fears:* the cat, losing his place. *Secret:* he dropped the page. He has told no one but Brevier. *Arc:* tells Winifred; sets his first line in the Erratum.

**10. Mrs Dorcas Hobday**, 60, cook-housekeeper at Pargeter's. Methodist, wide, forearms like hams; speaks in half-quoted hymns and Scripture ("Behold, the Lord shall provide, and meanwhile there's cold mutton").

**11. Mr Absalom Orme**, 71, journeyman compositor, Father of the Chapel. Slightly deaf; reads everything backwards by long habit and sometimes forwards by mistake; sets a line like a metronome. *Example:* "Backwards, miss, it is perfectly plain."

**12. Mr Edward "Ned" Fairweather**, 40, journeyman pressman. Enormous arms; plays the fiddle badly and with devotion; says "Aye" and little else.

**13. Mr Ezekiel Underhill**, 55, editor of the *Gazette*. Large, pink, a beautiful speaking voice, in terror of libel; proud that the *Gazette* has never corrected itself. *Example:* "The *Gazette* does not retract, sir. It clarifies."

**14. Mrs Keziah Rudge**, 72, the *Gazette*'s proprietor, widow of the founder. A Quaker in grey with a white cap; speaks in the plain style ("thee has") and never says Mr, Mrs, Miss or "my lord" (she says "Ezekiel Underhill", "Julian Stanmere", "the ninth month"), which the Marquess finds restful; deaf on one side; reads every issue with a pencil in the corner of the counting-room where nobody notices her; the sharpest person in the book. *Example:* "A newspaper that cannot say it was wrong cannot be trusted when it says it is right."

**15. Mr Ambrose Minton**, 35, Julian's secretary. Anxious, immaculate, pocket-watch checked every four minutes, ink on his right forefinger from the quill; writes the denial, and carries the Farmer's manuscripts in plain covers to Mr Ingram at the *Gazette* without having been seen to open one. *Wants* to be useful; *fears* being blamed; *secret* he sketches caricatures of his employer; *arc*: becomes steady.

**16. Henrietta, Dowager Duchess of Ormsgill**, 79. The great hostess. Tiny, ringed, in a purple turban, eyes like pins; says wicked things at conversational volume and speaks of the dead as though they were in the room ("Ormsgill would have hated her, so I like her"). *Example:* "I have forgotten more engagements than you have attended."

**17. Sir Ephraim Quarles (60) and Lady Quarles (Augusta, 50).** He: broad, bald, a self-made man who says "Plain terms". She: a woman of fainting fits who hunts a marquess for her daughter.

**18. Others, briefly.** *Mr Gideon Wragg*, 58, steward of Stanmere Court, grey, suspicious of London. *Mrs Pickett*, housekeeper at Stanmere Court. *Gantry*, butler at Stanmere House. *Dr Orlando Pym*, 50s, a Kent clergyman-naturalist who blames lightning-rods for the rain and lectures at dinner. *Sir Marmaduke Crake, M.P.*, a bore on corn. *Mr Sowerby*, the *Gazette*'s carter. *Mr Gribble*, Lovejoy's bailiff. *Matthias Fell*, Agnes's brother, 38, half-pay lieutenant in the King's Bench with a £312 debt. *Cecilia, Marchioness of Stanmere*, 53, Julian's mother, in Florence since 1794. She left after a marriage conducted as a long quarrel, and could not take her son (the law and her husband forbade it); she wrote to him every year on his birthday, care of Constance. *Brevier* the shop cat (grey, one torn ear, named for a type size).

## Relationships and conflicts

- **Winifred and Julian.** Want: a quiet fix. Collide: she will not be rescued or lied to; he arranges things. The Articles are a joke that becomes a vow. The real obstacle: she will not be bought or saved, and he has never asked anyone for anything. The fix is not misunderstanding; it is a plain question properly asked, when she does not need the answer.
- **Winifred and Barnaby.** Love and guilt; she has carried the shop and he has let her.
- **Julian and Lady Constance.** She has protected him by controlling him; her burned letters explain his fear. He learns the pattern in himself.
- **Barnaby and Lovejoy.** Master and former apprentice. Lovejoy believes Barnaby never gave him credit; Barnaby never knew. Their quarrel is petty on both sides and ruinous.
- **Winifred and Lovejoy.** Two craftspeople who respect each other's work and oppose each other's aims.
- **Agnes and Constance.** Twelve years of near-silence, mutual dependence; Agnes betrays what she loves because she loves someone else more.
- **Rupert and Verity.** He needs money; she needs a man who will not take the brewery. She decides to buy his debts and charge him interest.
- **Julian and Rupert.** Old friendship with a rivalry of charm; Rupert's honesty with Winifred is the stronger one.
- **Winifred and Mrs Rudge.** The proprietor who reads with a pencil recognises a reader.

## Voice guide

- **Viewpoint.** An arch, amused, slightly omniscient narrator, third person, past tense, who may step forward and address "the reader" now and then (never more than two or three times in a chapter) and who sometimes knows what a character does not. The close perspective is mainly Winifred or Julian; a chapter may shift to Constance, Barnaby, Agnes, Lovejoy or the Duchess for a scene. The narrator is fond of everyone, including Lovejoy, and refuses to lie: when the sun does not come out at the wedding, it does not.
- **Tense and register.** Past tense. Period-accurate diction, no parody: the sentences of 1816 without the vocabulary of 1816's novelists copied out. Educated, quick, concrete.
- **Sentence rhythm.** Long, balanced, turning sentences interrupted by short ones; paragraphs of varied length; no two consecutive paragraphs opening alike. A joke is often in the second clause.
- **Humour.** Constant and warm: situational (the Drip, the pug, the pies), verbal (Winifred's literalism, Julian's deadpan, Barnaby's digressions) and arch (the narrator's asides). Never at the expense of real feeling; the funniest chapters have a pain in them.
- **Dialogue.** Double quotation marks (straight quotes in the files), new paragraph for each speaker, "said" mostly; a third to a half of most chapters is talk; every speaker has a distinct rhythm. Characters interrupt, dodge, answer the wrong question. People say less than they mean.
- **Detail.** Name the type size, the quartern loaf, the toll, the hackney's fare, the fabric. Printing detail is always accurate and always in service of a scene.
- **Romance.** Warm, closed-door. Kisses and hand-holding are fine; nothing further.

**Sample paragraph (voice only, not part of the novel).**

It will be objected, by those readers who have never been obliged to earn a shilling, that a woman of twenty-six ought not to have been standing in a printing house at three in the morning with her sleeves pushed above the elbow and a bodkin between her teeth. The reader is quite right. She ought to have been in bed, and so ought the cat. But the roof of Hand and Pen Court had admitted, since the first of May, a quantity of rain that would have satisfied the Deluge, and a person who sets type for her bread does not choose her hours so much as find them waiting for her, like creditors. Winifred Pargeter had not made the weather. She had merely been standing under it. That the weather would presently ruin her, and a marquess, and a very good paragraph in a very respectable newspaper, was a thing nobody could have told her; and if anybody had, she would have asked for the figures.

**Things this narrator would never write.**

1. Modern idiom or slang ("okay", "awesome", "no way", "chill").
2. "Fiancé" or "fiancée". (Betrothed, intended, engaged.)
3. Stock phrases of machine fiction ("a testament to", "tapestry", "shiver down her spine", "breath she didn't know she was holding", "palpable").
4. A joke that sneers at Winifred's trade or at the poor.
5. A speech in which a villain explains himself. Lovejoy tells people only what is true and useful.
6. The Marquess saying "I love you" in so many words before chapter 23; he says "Quite", "Which?" and "Stet".
7. A convenient letter or diary that delivers backstory. (Constance tells Julian aloud; Cecilia's letter is four lines.)
8. A coincidence that solves a problem. Every rescue is earned by someone's earlier choice.
9. Explicit sex, or anything past a kiss.
10. Winifred telling a flat lie aloud. She deflects, qualifies, quotes the *Gazette*.
11. Parody of Austen or any author's voice, or a character named from an existing novel.
12. A sentence that explains the joke.

## Motifs and recurring images

1. **Stet, dele, erratum.** The three proof words. *Stet*: chapter 3 (Julian's choice), 11 (Winifred leaves an error in Constance's menu cards), 12 (the midpoint), 24 (the register). *Dele*: Winifred's way with trouble; Julian's father's portrait. *Erratum*: the closing of chapter 18, 22, 23.
2. **The Crooked W.** In the Misprint (1), in Lovejoy's magnifying glass (6), on the Erratum proof (23), as a new W (24).
3. **Rain and the Drip.** The leak over the imposing stone: 1, 7 (a ballroom leak), 11 (kitchen flood), 13, 17, 23 (the roof gives way), 24 (a lull). Rain on every outing.
4. **Ink on the thumb.** Winifred's left thumb: gloves (4, 7), Julian's inky cuff (5, 9), Julian's own thumbprint on Article 6 (3), her thumb on the register (24).
5. **Julian's signet ring.** He turns it when thinking (2, 5, 10); it is used to shim the Albion's toggle (23); he wears it bent after (24).
6. **The brass composing stick.** Naomi Pargeter's: introduced (3), the Duchess's dinner-table touch (11), forfeits (16), the Erratum is set with it (23).
7. **Pies and Toby; Brevier the cat.** "A page in pie" (1), the pastrycook's pies (9), the flood washtub (17), the half pie he offers (18), the wedding pie (24).
8. **The red sky.** Copper sunsets: 10, 17, 24.
9. **Florence.** The pale rectangle on the library wall (10), the Florence-postmarked letter on Constance's desk (4), her confession (19), the letter (19), Cecilia at the door (24).
10. **"It is in the Gazette."** Winifred's formula: 3, 7, 13 (where it fails on a woman who cannot read), 16; used by Julian (7); finally turned: "It is in the register" (24).

## Ground rules that must stay consistent

**The Misprint, word for word.**
- *As printed (Thursday 6 June):* "MARRIAGE IN HIGH LIFE.—We are authorised to state that the engagement between the Marquess of Stanmere and Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row, is entirely well founded, and that his Lordship is at present engaged to the lady."
- *As intended:* "MARRIAGE IN HIGH LIFE.—We are authorised to state that the report of an engagement between the Marquess of Stanmere and Miss Quarles, daughter of Sir Ephraim Quarles, Bart., is entirely without foundation, and that his Lordship is at present engaged to no lady whatever."
- *How it happened:* rain through the slates blotted the copy; the page fell into pie; the line "Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row" (Winifred's own advertisement) and the denial's name-line were of one measure and lay together in the heap; the phrases "the report of an", "without foundation" and "no lady whatever" were scrambled. The Crooked W is in the printed "W." Nobody proofed it: the reader Mr Ingram was ill and Mr Underhill's rule is no proofs after midnight.

**The Erratum, as printed in Great Primer on Saturday 28 September (the wording is fixed; chapter 23 may quote it).** "ERRATUM. In our number of Thursday the 6th of June, in the paragraph headed Marriage in High Life, for 'the engagement between the Marquess of Stanmere and Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row, is entirely well founded, and that his Lordship is at present engaged to the lady', read 'the report of an engagement between the Marquess of Stanmere and Miss Quarles, daughter of Sir Ephraim Quarles, Bart., is entirely without foundation, and that his Lordship is at present engaged to no lady whatever.' The error was the compositor's. The compositor is W. Pargeter, who saw it before the sheets left her house and did not stop them. The Gazette begs the pardon of the Marquess of Stanmere, of Miss Quarles, and of its readers, and has never before had occasion to do so."

**The Articles of Agreement (printed on one foolscap sheet, two signed counterparts, one kept by Julian at Stanmere House, one by Winifred in her mother's brass stick-case).**
1. The announcement in the *Gazette* of 6 June to stand as printed, and neither party to contradict it until Michaelmas Day, 29 September 1816.
2. Miss Pargeter to appear in company with the Marquess on not fewer than six occasions, and at his table once; the Marquess to call at Hand and Pen Court twice in every week, at hours convenient to the press.
3. The Marquess shall not lend, give, advance or promise any money to Miss Pargeter or to Mr Pargeter, directly or by any third person.
4. Neither party shall be required to utter in their own voice any statement they know to be false; the *Gazette* shall be relied upon to speak for them.
5. At Michaelmas Miss Pargeter shall have the sole right to end the engagement, and the Marquess shall bear all the blame.
6. Neither party shall form an Attachment of the Affections.
7. The truth shall be known to no persons beyond Mr Pargeter, Mr Minton, and the chapel of Pargeter's.

**Who knows what, and when.**
- Thu 6 June: Winifred (error, and her secret); Barnaby (told that evening); Toby (he dropped the page); Underhill (thinks the engagement is real); Minton and Julian (know the paragraph is wrong); Constance (guesses by 9 a.m.).
- Fri 7 June: Orme and Fairweather (the chapel, by custom keeps it); Mrs Hobday guesses. Julian knows the error was Winifred's; he does not know she saw it at 4.40 until the midnight of 4 July.
- Mon 10 June: Agnes knows, from Constance. Fri 14 June: Lovejoy knows how the error happened and has the carter's story by August.
- Tue 18 June: Verity knows (guesses). Sat 22 June: Rupert knows (guesses, tells Winifred he knows).
- 4 July: Winifred learns Julian is the Farmer; Julian learns she let the cart go.
- 3 Aug: Agnes copies the Articles. 15 Aug: Agnes sends Lovejoy the copy. 31 Aug: the town knows. Julian's attempt to buy the notes: Tuesday 27 August.
- 1 Sept: Julian learns of the burned letters. 3 Sept: Winifred learns Agnes is Pry. 24 Sept: Mrs Rudge knows everything. The Duchess says she knew in a minute, and perhaps she did.

**Money and time.**
- A guinea is 21 shillings. £740 became £1,140 in August; Verity's loan is £1,200 at six per cent. The Old Lady's bill of sale: £60 plus Gribble's costs, paid by Winifred on 27 June out of the cash-box (£74 from three weeks of fashionable orders at ordinary rates).
- Julian never pays Winifred, her father or the shop a penny (Article 3) except for work at ordinary rates, through Minton, as any customer; the Farmer's pamphlets and the Weald Subscription are such work. He breaks the spirit of Article 3 once, in chapter 18, and it costs him her good opinion.
- *Distances:* Paternoster Row to Curzon Street about two and a half miles; to Pomfret Yard six-tenths of a mile; to Southwark across London Bridge a mile and a quarter; to Yalding thirty-eight miles. A hackney in rain: half an hour to the West End; the post-chaise to Kent: eleven hours on the roads of that summer.
- *Weather:* it rains almost every day of the book. Fires are lit in August. The sun is seen as a copper disc at sunset on three occasions only. It does not shine at the wedding.
- *Bells:* St Paul's strikes the hours. St Vedast rings for service on Sundays.
- *Julian's Farmer letters:* nine to May 1816; the tenth is printed on 28 September.
- *Toby's age is thirteen. The cat's name is Brevier.* The pug is Blücher. The Albion's maker is Ebenezer Pargeter, Barnaby's cousin.
