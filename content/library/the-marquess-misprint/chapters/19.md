# What Lady Constance Burned

At seven o'clock on the first of September the Marquess of Stanmere sat in a closed carriage in Hertford Street, with the blinds down, and did not get out of it.

The bells had begun. They begin early in Mayfair on a Sunday, for the convenience of persons who like to be reproached in good time: St George's had led off, and St James's, which would not be outdone, had come in a little flat. On the box Pegg turned up his collar and fixed his walnut face upon the horses' ears, as a man does who has driven his master to a number of doors at a number of hours and has formed no opinion of any of them. The rain was the fine, tired rain of the fifth month of a summer that had never begun.

Julian had not been to bed. He had come in from Cheapside at ten the night before, wet to the thighs, and Gantry had taken his coat without remark, which was Gantry's way of weeping; and he had sat in the library until the windows went grey, in the chair that faced the wall between them, where a pale oblong showed the shape of something that had hung there until he was nine. Somewhere about four he had understood that he was going to Hertford Street. He told himself it was to confess Tuesday to his aunt before the town did. This was half a lie. He was going to be told what to do. It was what Hertford Street was for. He was thirty-one, the master of eleven farms and the author of nine letters on the Corn Laws, and he sat with his hand on the carriage door like a boy who has been sent for, and was ashamed to find that it was a comfort.

At a quarter to eight he got out.

The butler, who had opened that door to him since he wore frocks, said that her ladyship was in the morning-room and that Miss Fell had gone to the early service, as she did every Sunday; and he said it to the middle distance, in the manner of a man letting a gentleman know that the house is empty of everyone but the person he has come to see.

The morning-room was as cold as it had been in June. The hundred and forty birds of the late General Sir Hugh Dyer looked down from their shelves with the glassy surprise of creatures that had been shot and not told why. Lady Constance sat by a fire the size of a hat, in a grey wrapper with the purple ribbon at the throat, which she wore with everything. On her knee lay the *Town Crier*. At her feet Blücher opened his one eye, found that it was only the Marquess, and shut it again.

"Sit down," said Lady Constance. "Not that chair. There is a nuthatch in it."

He sat in the other, under the heron.

"I have read it," she said. "Five times with the trumpet and a sixth without. You will not be able to tell me anything in it that is not so."

"There is one thing it does not say." He turned the signet on his finger. "On Tuesday I went to Ave Maria Lane."

He told her the whole of it, in figures, because figures were what he had. The hour: eleven. The sum: two thousand two hundred and eighty pounds, being twice the paper. The reasoning: that Article the Third forbade him to lend, give, advance or promise, and said not one word about buying, which he had found out at three in the morning and hated by four. The refusal. The Saturday, and Mr Lovejoy bowing to him in a composing room with a glass in his hand and the whole affair in his mouth like a sweet.

"Perfectly sound," said Lady Constance.

"It was not sound."

"It was exactly what I should have done." She took up the ear-trumpet, looked down the wrong end of it, and laid it in her lap. "I should have sent Gantry with the money in a purse and a story about a cousin's mortgage. You went yourself, which is more honest and a great deal more stupid. What did she say?"

"That a special licence is what a lord uses to make a mess disappear. That she would not be disappeared."

"Did you ask her?"

"I told her what I could arrange."

"Ah."

It was the smallest syllable he had ever had from her, and it went through the room like a door. Julian looked at the pug, who was asleep; at the vulture on the top shelf; at his own hands.

"You arrange people, Aunt," he said. He had meant it for a charge. It came out flat, as a fact does that has been kept two days from the post.

"Yes," said Lady Constance.

He had prepared a speech. He had made it at four that morning, with a good deal of syntax in it, and he found that nobody had ever agreed with him so promptly. It left a marquess with an armful of syntax and no one to throw it at.

"I have arranged you for twenty-two years," she said. "It is the one subject on which I have never hesitated." She took up the trumpet again and put it down, which in her was the equivalent of another person's sitting. "Blücher is blind in one eye and discreet in the other. I am going to tell you a sum."

"Which?"

"Twenty-one."

He waited. When she said nothing more he did what he always did with a number, which was to look for what it was the number of.

"Twenty-one what?"

"Letters. One a year, on the twelfth of March, from 'ninety-five to 'fifteen. They came from Florence in your mother's hand, with my name on the cover, because she knew that your father would not open a letter directed to his sister, and she trusted his sister to do what a sister does." She looked at the fire. "I did what a sister does. I put them in it."

The pug breathed. The clock on the chimney-piece, a gilt affair with a stuffed wren on top, struck the quarter, and neither of them looked at it.

"The first came in the spring of 'ninety-five," said Lady Constance. "Your father took it out of my hand in the dining-room. He read the direction. He said, 'The boy is a Stanmere, Constance,' and he put it on the fire himself, with the tongs. I was forty-three. I had been told since I was in frocks that this is how a family is kept."

"And the second?"

"He did not ask for the second. He said, 'You will know what to do.' I knew."

"And after he died."

"That is what I cannot explain to you." She said it exactly as she said everything, like a woman reading out a bill. "In 'nine there was nobody left to obey. You were twenty-four. You were the Marquess, and might have been in Florence by the next packet if you had a mind. I thought, if the boy goes I shall have no one. I thought, if he reads it he will not forgive me the rest. I thought a number of other things, which came to the same sum, and it was the twelfth of March, and I burned it. One does not decide such a thing, Julian. One does it once, and the second time is habit, and the eleventh time is Tuesday. It is not done with a great fire. It is done with a great many small reasons."

"Did you read them?"

"No. It was the one decent thing. I knew what I was burning and not what it said." She stood, with the pug sliding off her feet in protest, and went to the escritoire in the window. The purple ribbon at her throat, which he had always taken for an ornament, turned out to have a key on it. "Until this March. I read the last. I do not know why. I stood at the grate with the poker in my other hand, and it had an address in it, and I found that I burn sentiments, Julian, but I do not burn addresses. An address is a fact."

She unlocked the third drawer and took out a single sheet, folded in three, and held it out. Her hand was perfectly steady. It was the steadiness of a person who has resolved not to be seen shaking.

"Miss Pargeter read the direction of that across this room in June," said Lady Constance. "From nine yards. I turned it face down. She said nothing to anyone. I watched her say nothing for three months, and thought better of her every week, and did not know why until I had your *Crier* in my lap and nothing to do but think."

The paper was soft at the folds, as paper is that has been taken out and put back. The hand ran uphill. He knew it at once, which was absurd: he had last seen it in a nursery at Stanmere Court, on the flyleaf of an Aesop, *For Julian, who is to be good*, and had forgotten that he knew it, and his eyes remembered first.

> My dear Julian, you are thirty-one today, and I have not forgotten one of them. I do not know whether Constance gives you these; I have decided that she does. I am at fourteen Via dei Bardi. The door is open. C.

It was four lines long.

He read it twice. He read it as he read everything, with a pencil's attention and no pencil; and at the second sentence he stopped, and looked for some time at *I have decided that she does*, as a man looks at a sum that has come out right against all the odds and is therefore, on the whole, more frightening than one that has come out wrong.

He did not shout. The reader who has waited this long for a marquess to shout will be disappointed: Julian had never in his life raised his voice to an aunt, and did not know in which part of the house the voice was kept. What came was slower, and worse. For twenty-two years he had been a man whose mother did not write. He had built upon it as a house is built upon a fact: the shelf of errata, and the oblong on the library wall that he did not mention, and the boy of nine who resolved that he would not marry unless it were impossible not to. All of it rested on the plain sentence *she did not write*, and the sentence was a misprint. The copy had said something else. Somebody had read the copy, and seen, and let it go.

And there was another thing, which he could look at only sideways. He had gone to Ave Maria Lane with a cheque-book for the reason his aunt had stood at a grate with a poker. He had known what was best for a person, and had not asked her. It was the same fire. His had a better coat on, and a cheque-book where hers had tongs.

"I shall not ask you to forgive me," said Lady Constance. "I do not ask."

"No." He folded the letter along its three folds, which fitted. "I should like to be alone, Aunt."

"Go and be alone somewhere useful."

At the door he turned. "You kept the last."

"I kept the last," said Lady Constance. "I should like it noted. I am told it counts for something."

"Quite," said Julian, which was his word for No and, on this single occasion, for the other.

* * *

When he had gone she sat for some time without moving, as one sits after a tooth has been drawn, to find out whether it is true. Then she took up the *Crier* from the floor, where it had slid, and turned to the second page, to the column headed THE QUIZZING-GLASS, and read the first line of it for the seventh time.

*I may be quite wrong, but.*

She had heard that sentence at her own breakfast table, at the foot of it, over the toast, in a voice that apologised to the butter, every morning since 1804. She had not mentioned it to her nephew. A woman who has just emptied one drawer does not reach for another within the hour; and it was a habit of hers not to say a thing she knew until it would be useful. She had known this since Saturday, and had said nothing for the same reason she had said nothing for twenty-one years, and it had occurred to her, somewhere between the burning and the giving, that it had not been a reason then either.

She took a sheet and wrote, in the upright and uncompromising hand of a fence: *Agnes. You will come to the morning-room when you have read this. C.D.*

She did not send it. Miss Fell came in from church at nine, wet to the mittens, and took off her bonnet in the hall and said good morning to the pug; and Lady Constance, who in twelve years had not once looked at her companion's face across the toast, found that she could not look at it now. The note stayed in the pocket of the wrapper all day. It went up at ten that night, on the chocolate-tray, face upward, to lie on a washstand in an attic where a candle was burning.

* * *

The General Post Office stood in Lombard Street, in a house that had been built for a banker and had never entirely got over the descent. On a Sunday the foreign department kept a hatch open from ten until seven, for the convenience of persons who had spent the week finding a sentence.

Julian found a sloping desk with a chained pen and an inkwell the colour of the Thames. He took a sheet. He wrote *Madam,* and looked at it as he looked at a wrong signboard, and crossed it out. He wrote *Mother,* which he had not said aloud since he was nine, and found that it stood on the paper like a man in a doorway; and he put *Dear* before it, to give it something to lean on. Then he wrote *There has been a misprint.*

He sat twenty minutes with the pen above the next line. A blot formed on the nib, grew, and fell, and he watched it go. At the next desk a sailor in a pea-jacket, with his tongue between his teeth, wrote to somebody in six lines, kissed the sheet, and was gone; and Julian envied him with a ferocity that would have astonished the sailor. At last he folded the half page into his pocket-book, between two leaves of W.P.'s queries, and went out into Lombard Street without having got any further.

* * *

It turned out, as the afternoon wore on, that what he could do was correct himself in type. It was the one form of apology in which he had had any practice, having begun it at nine years old over his father's Gibbon.

At three he sat down in the library, in the chair that faced the oblong, with a sheet of foolscap, and wrote without stopping the tenth letter of A Kentish Farmer. The first nine had cost him a month apiece, with a dictionary and a quantity of reflection. This took two hours, and he did not blot it.

> To the Editor of the Morning Gazette. Sir,—In the nine letters which have appeared in your columns over the description A Kentish Farmer, for A Kentish Farmer read Julian, Marquess of Stanmere, who owns eleven farms in the Weald of Kent at rents he has not lowered. The figures, I am advised by an able reader, stand.

The rest was the arithmetic of bread, which he knew, and the arithmetic of a landlord, which he had never in his life set down; and the second was a good deal shorter than the first. At the foot he wrote his name and style in full, in the same hand as the figures, and sat looking at it. It was the first thing he had signed in twenty years that he had not read over.

Minton came in at four with the cold beef on a tray, like a man bringing a sandwich to a bomb.

"My lord, it is signed."

"Yes."

"With your name."

"That is generally what a signature is." Julian held out the sheet. "A fair copy, if you please, in a plain cover, to Mrs Rudge at Bath. Mr Ingram has the direction and will pretend that he has not."

"Not to Mr Underhill, my lord?"

"Mr Underhill would print it and call it a clarification."

"And the Row?" Minton did not look at him. "It is Miss Pargeter's type."

"No." He turned the signet once. "Sent from Curzon Street to Hand and Pen Court, it would look like a present, and she has refused presents since June. It will go to the one person in London who can print it or not as she pleases. What Miss Pargeter does with the consequences is Miss Pargeter's affair. I find that I am not to be consulted."

Minton took the sheet. He had read it already over the tray, as a secretary reads, without moving his head. He consulted his watch from long habit, and found it saying five minutes to three, as it had said since a ditch on Wrotham Hill, and put it away with the face of a man who has been caught agreeing with a clock.

"It is the best thing you have written, my lord."

"It is the only one in which I was wrong," said Julian. "I expect that is the same thing."

* * *

At six he went back to Lombard Street.

He did not take the carriage. He walked, east along Piccadilly and down Cheapside, and he noticed that nobody looked at him, which was the first mercy of the day. The hatch was still open. The sailor had been replaced by a clergyman, who wrote with his eyes shut.

The letter took eleven minutes. The tenth Farmer had taught him the form: one states what was printed, one states what should have been, and one signs. He wrote that he had been told for twenty-two years that she did not write, and that it appeared she had. He wrote that he had the last, of March, and should like the others, if any survived, and the rest of her, if the door were still open. He wrote that he would not come unasked, and that this was why he was asking. Then he wrote his name, and read it over, which he had said he would not do, and found nothing in it to correct.

The clerk at the hatch was a young man with a cold. He weighed the packet, turned it over, read the direction, *The Marchioness of Stanmere, 14 Via dei Bardi, Firenze*, and then looked at the griffin in the wax, and at the person on the other side of the brass.

"Florence, my lord?" said the clerk. "That will be two and sixpence. It is a long way."

"Yes," said the Marquess. "I know."
