# The Old Lady Runs Away

It is a peculiarity of Hand and Pen Court that nobody knocks. The street door of Pargeter's stood on the latch from five in the morning until the pressmen went home, and callers, from bishops to baker's boys, came in and shouted. So that when the Marquess of Stanmere walked under the arch at ten o'clock on the Thursday, with his umbrella furled like a sword and Minton behind him carrying the second one in the manner of a man carrying a spare soul, the first thing he heard was a knock, and the second thing he thought was that it was wrong.

It was three raps, evenly spaced, with the ferrule of a stick: the knock of a man who has stood at the doors of a great many houses and is in no hurry to be admitted to any of them.

In the middle of the Court beside the pump stood a dray with a skid laid to its tail and a large placid horse of the Suffolk sort between the shafts, which regarded the proceedings with the look of a horse asked to take a small part in a play it had not read. Two porters sat on the edge of the trough eating bread and bacon. On the step, with his stick still raised, stood a stout and civil man in a brown coat with brass buttons, who turned round at the sound of the Marquess's boots and took off his hat.

"How do you do," said Julian, who had rehearsed it in a hackney a fortnight before and found that it still came out.

"Gribble, sir," said the man. "For Mr Lovejoy. A bailiff, in a manner of speaking, though I call it collecting."

The door flew open. Toby looked out, in his shirtsleeves, with a pie in his hand; he took in the dray, the porters and the brown coat, and then, with deep and shining satisfaction, Julian. "It's a bailiff!" he cried, over his shoulder, into the shop. "A proper one, miss! With a stick!" And to the Marquess, kindly, as to a colleague who had done his best: "You aren't one, your Marquessship. Are you? Not today?"

"Not today," said Julian.

Winifred came to the door. Her sleeves were above the elbow, the brass rule was through her hair, and she had ink to the wrist; and she took the paper from Mr Gribble's hand with a politeness so extreme that Julian, who had known her three weeks, stepped back half a pace.

It was a bill of sale, dated the fourteenth of July, 1815, and it conveyed to Hector Lovejoy, printer, of Ave Maria Lane, as security for the sum of sixty pounds advanced to Barnaby Pargeter, one wooden common press, known as the Old Lady, of about the year 1770, with its bar, its tympan, its frisket, its ink-balls and its appurtenances, to be delivered upon demand after Midsummer Day. Midsummer Day had been Monday. It was signed by her father in the hand of a man who had been cheerful when he wrote it.

"There is a notice period," said Winifred. "Fourteen days. I remember the clause. My father read it to me aloud, with some pride."

"Notice was served by post upon Mr B. Pargeter on the tenth, miss. It was received. It was not, I understand, opened."

"Father," said Winifred, not turning her head.

From the speaking-tube in the composing room, which carried through the open door, a muffled and agonised voice said: "My dear, I took it for the coals."

"It was under the cat," said Toby.

"I shall have a great deal to say to the cat," said Winifred, very quietly. She turned the paper over. She had already begun to count. "Mr Gribble, I have in the cash-box fifty-four pounds, eleven shillings. If you will wait until three o'clock, the Bishop's chaplain is to pay nineteen pounds ten for five hundred sermons, and I shall have the sixty and the costs besides. If you will take the fifty-four now, I will give you my note for the rest."

"I couldn't, miss. A bill's a bill. Sixty and costs, paid to Mr Lovejoy at the Phoenix Press between the hours of eleven and four, and Mr Lovejoy, I understand, is at Pomfret Yard until five." He turned his hat in his hands, with real regret. "I am sent to take, not to receive. It's the sort of distinction a man in my line has to be careful of."

It was very neat. Julian, standing on the cobbles with the rain on his hat, saw all of it at once, as he sometimes saw the foot of a column before he had read the head. Friday's *Gazette* was to be run from half past one in the morning. Without the second press, the Albion would do two hundred and fifty an hour, for an hour, for men who could bear it. Winifred was saying the figures aloud, in a steady voice, as one reads out the symptoms of a patient who will not recover. "Eleven hundred, at two hundred and fifty. Four hours and twenty minutes, for men who cannot be borne. Say six. The cart must leave at a quarter past five." She stopped.

"Clause six," said Julian.

"You have been reading the contract."

"Minton has." Minton, behind his shoulder, found something wrong with the second umbrella. "It was the cart in May, was it not? I thought, Miss Pargeter, that a second breach—"

"A second breach in any quarter voids it at the Proprietor's option. Yes." She smiled at Mr Gribble, with the smile of a person at a funeral. "Mr Lovejoy is a very considerate man."

Julian's hand had gone to his breast, to the pocket in which, under the pocket-book, a cheque-book on Child's lay folded flat. It rested there as a hand rests on a pistol it has promised not to draw. "Article the Third," said Winifred, without looking round.

"I know what it says. I was present at the composing of it. I am merely reciting it to myself."

"You may recite it a little more quietly."

So he stood in the yard. He had never in his life been so entirely without employment. He was a man to whom the arrangement of other people's difficulties was a form of respiration; he had only to be told of a trouble to find that the means of removing it had already been put in his pocket. Sixty pounds. Sixty-three, with costs. It was less than a month's wine. He stood on the cobbles with it unspent in his pocket, a Marquess without a purse, and the effect of the situation upon his temper was so remarkable that he drew himself up to his whole six feet and one, and said, in the voice that his steward in Kent called *the old Lordship's*, "I am the Marquess of Stanmere."

"Are you, sir?" said Mr Gribble, with the greatest civility. "I have had two earls and a man who said he was the Archbishop of Canterbury, and I find the effect is much the same. It doesn't alter the writ."

The title, thus employed, frightened Mr Gribble about as much as a hat.

* * *

The Old Lady came out of the back room the way a gallows comes out of a village green: unwillingly, in sections, and with a great many persons giving advice. She was eight hundredweight of oak and iron, with a bar like a ship's tiller and a screw as thick as a man's wrist, and she had been inked and beaten and sworn at for forty-six years in a dozen shops, and had printed in her time a quantity of sermons, a victory, three libels and the banns of the Row. The porters slid her up the skid with a tackle and lashed her to the dray with cords that Mr Orme, standing at the corner of the shop in his apron with his composing stick held like a lance, looked at for some time with an expression of increasing contempt.

"Chapel!" cried Orme, raising the stick.

"Aye," said Fairweather, behind him, which was to say that the Chapel had been called and had answered; but Dick Sparrow, the second pressman, who might have been expected to be the second voice, stood by the pump with his hands in his pockets, whistling through the gap in his front teeth. He wore, Julian noticed, a very clean neckcloth. Fairweather looked at it, and then at Dick, in a manner which would have been a remark if Fairweather had been a man who made them.

"I'm going to guard her," announced Toby. He had climbed up, pie and all, on to the tail of the dray, and was sitting with his boots against the Old Lady's frame and his arms round her screw, looking pale and proud. "Somebody's got to. She's been in the Row since before I was born."

"Get down, you little—" began Mr Gribble.

At which point Brevier, who had been watching from the window-sill with the attitude of a cat that has no opinions, rose, stretched, descended to the cobbles, and made one fluid leap on to the tarpaulin over the Old Lady's bar, where she sat down and began to wash. The porters looked at the cat. Mr Gribble looked at the porters. And the dray, with the Old Lady, the boy and the cat, rolled out under the arch of Hand and Pen Court with an inch to spare on either side and a sound like a violin string being played by a drover, followed by a great fall of soot.

Winifred took off her apron. She did not say anything. She walked out of the arch behind the dray, with the rule in her hair and her pattens clacking on the stones, and the Marquess of Stanmere, who found that he was walking beside her and had no recollection of having decided to, said: "Where will he take it?"

"To the warehouse in Bridge Street, Blackfriars. Mr Lovejoy keeps his spare presses there."

"Then we are on the Hill."

"We are."

Ave Maria Lane was a short steep pinch of a street, and the rain was coming down it in a sheet; and at the bottom, where the Lane meets Ludgate Hill, the dray turned west and began, quite quietly, to go down. It is a very steep hill. It is steep enough that a loaded dray wants its drag chain shipped and its horse held back; and the porters, who had not done up the lashings with the care of men who expected to be watched, had also not shipped the drag.

It happened in the following order. A street organ beside the church of St Martin struck up *The Roast Beef of Old England* with a wheeze like a dying lung. A boy with two dogs, bringing a drove of sheep up the Hill the wrong way to Smithfield, met the dray head on. The Suffolk horse, who had borne an enormous number of things with an enormous composure, looked at the organ, looked at the sheep, and decided that its engagement was over. It went forward. The dray went forward after it. Somewhere in the rattle there was the sharp twang of a cord parting, and the Old Lady lurched on her skid, and Toby screamed, in a shrill voice that carried the whole length of Ludgate Hill, "Your Marquessship!"

Julian ran.

He was thirty-one, and had never run in a street in his life. He ran like a heron that has been startled, all knees and neck, with his coat flying; and Winifred ran after him, and her pattens, which were of iron, caught between two stones and tripped her; she kicked them off, one, two, over the heads of the crowd, and ran in her stockings through the rain. The dray had a lead of thirty yards and was gaining. Julian had a long stride. By the corner of the Old Bailey he had got within ten feet, and by the Belle Sauvage yard, where a woman screamed and a pot-boy let fall a tray, he had a hand on the tail-board and a foot on the drag chain, which was trailing behind the wheel like a snake. He took it in both hands. He sat down in the road.

The chain ran out. He went along the cobbles on the seat of his breeches, with the iron round his forearms, for twenty yards, at what felt to him a speed exceeding anything attempted by man; and the dray, which had one hind wheel half locked by this unexpected anchor, slewed from the middle of the Hill, took a lamp-post by the shoulder, cut across the pavement, and fetched up with a crash in the bow window of Mr Mutlow's pastrycook's shop, which had been prepared that morning for the Thursday trade.

Pies went up like a covey of birds. Mutton pies, beefsteak pies, eel pies in their trays, a tall cold goose pie from the window, forty-one of them in a single hot brown tide that broke over the Old Lady, the horse, the pavement, a passing curate and the Marquess of Stanmere. He sat in the road with his hair on end and a pie on his collar and a bar of the chain round his arm, and looked at the Old Lady, who had come to rest with her frame against Mr Mutlow's counter and her screw in the air, like a gallows that has just had a very good idea.

"Your Marquessship!" said Toby, from beneath a tarpaulin. "I held on! I held on to her, I did. Brevier's in the goose."

* * *

By the end of the quarter of an hour there were two hundred persons on the Hill, and a boy from Mr Treacher's shop in St James's, who had been in the Belle Sauvage yard by accident, was drawing in a book with the speed of an artist who sees fortune coming round the corner. News in the Row travels at the speed of an apprentice. They came down Ludgate Hill from every printing house in Paternoster Row, from Ave Maria Lane and Warwick Lane and Ivy Lane, boys and journeymen in paper caps and leather aprons, a hundred or more, hatless, in the rain; and they did not ask a question. They looked at the Old Lady, and at Mr Gribble, who stood in the ruin of Mutlow's shop with his hat in his hand and a pie on his stick, and at the lady in her stockings; and a boy from Longman's who had a voice like a bell said: "Chapel!"

They lifted her. It took forty of them, and they did it as a congregation lifts a coffin, under the bar, under the frame, with a hand under every beam, and set her on their shoulders. Somebody started to sing. It was a trade ditty, and had been sung in the Row at wakes and weddings and funerals since before anyone could say when, to a tune that bore no relation to any other tune in England:

*Pull the bar, boys, pull it down, ink the ball and beat the town; wooden Lady, oaken Dame, take her home and take no blame!*

They bore her back up the Hill in the rain, a hundred and twenty boys carrying eight hundredweight of oak, with Toby riding on the top, shrieking the chorus, and a cat on his shoulder, and a goose pie in the cat. Behind came Mr Orme, who had been brought down the Hill in the arms of two apprentices like a bishop on a litter, and who had risen in their arms to claim Chapel law. A stranger, he announced, who laid hands upon a press within the freedom of the Chapel without paying garnish to the Father, was liable to a solace of one gallon of porter. "Backwards, Mr Gribble," said Orme, "it is perfectly plain." And Mr Gribble, who had served writs upon four bishops and a harpist and been given tea by most of them, walked behind the procession with his hat in his hand, among forty apprentices, in the rain, like a man who has been taken prisoner by a very kind enemy.

Winifred walked with them, carrying her pattens. She had a pie on her shoulder. Her stockings were black to the knee. She had not said one word since the lamp-post. At the corner of Ave Maria Lane she turned her head, and saw, a little behind, the Marquess of Stanmere coming up the Hill with the chain still on his arm, and in his face, under the soot and the grease and the dripping hair, an expression she had never seen on it: of a man who has been in a fight and has no idea who won, and is very much inclined to go again.

* * *

Mr Gribble came back at six, as he had said he would, with the writ.

He came into the composing room with his hat held before him, in the manner of a man who calls upon a family after a bereavement and has not forgotten the cake. The Old Lady stood in her accustomed corner, gleaming with a certain smugness; she had been wiped by forty hands and oiled by Orme, and her bar was set in the position of one who has done her day's work. The cash-box stood open on the imposing stone. The Bishop's chaplain had come at three. Toby sat on a stool in the corner, consuming the surviving goose pie, and was in the happiest condition of his life.

"Mr Lovejoy's compliments, miss," said Mr Gribble, "and he regrets any inconvenience, and he begs me to say that the press must go to Bridge Street tonight or be redeemed."

"Quite," said Winifred. It was not her word, and Julian, standing against the far wall in a borrowed apron with soot in his hair and a pie on his collar, felt it go through him like a pin.

She began to count. She counted aloud, as she always did, in her quick dry voice, setting each coin on the stone in rows, so that every person in the room might see it done: fifty-four pounds eleven in the box in the morning; nineteen pounds ten from the Bishop's chaplain at three; seventy-four pounds and a shilling in all. Out of the seventy-four, sixty to Mr Lovejoy upon the bill. Fifty-seven guineas and three shillings, which is sixty pounds to the penny; she counted the guineas twice, and pushed them across the stone in a bright heap.

"And costs, miss. Three pounds four. There is the dray, and the porters, and the damage to Mr Mutlow's."

"The dray is Mr Lovejoy's own, and the damage to Mr Mutlow's was your lashings, which are his affair and not mine," said Winifred. She took the pencil from behind her ear and wrote the figures on the stone. "Your attendance, three days at a crown. The stamp on the writ, six shillings. The porters' hire, twelve. That is one pound thirteen. Call it two pounds, and I will not ask for the pies."

Mr Gribble looked at the figures for some time. He had the expression of a man who has been out-arithmeticked in a profession that sets great store by arithmetic. "Two pounds," he agreed, at last, and signed the receipt on his knee; and Winifred, who had not smiled since half past ten that morning, put the writ in her pocket, as one puts away a handkerchief that has been of use. "You may tell Mr Lovejoy," she said, "that the Old Lady is redeemed, and is grateful for the outing."

Mr Gribble bowed to the press, to the lady, and to the Marquess, in that order. He went out through the Court with his hat on, whistling something that was not far from *The Roast Beef of Old England*.

The door closed. The Drip fell into its pan with a small flat note.

"I did not pay it," said Julian.

She raised her eyes. They were dark and tired and rimmed with red from the rain, and she looked at him across the stone as one looks at a column of figures that has come out, at the last, to the penny.

"You did not," said Winifred. "Thank you."

It was, as it happened, the first time in thirty-one years that anyone had thanked the Marquess of Stanmere for doing nothing, and he found that he liked it a great deal more than he ought. He stood against the wall of a printing house with a pie upon his collar and a smear of ink from the Old Lady's ball across his nose, watching a small woman put sixty pounds, in coin, into a cash-box that was not his, and not once, in the whole of the operation, did he reach for his purse.
