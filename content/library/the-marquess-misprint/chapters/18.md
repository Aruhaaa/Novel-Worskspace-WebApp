# The Town Crier Cries

"Marquess's Misprint!" cried the hawkers in St Paul's Churchyard at seven o'clock on the morning of Saturday the thirty-first of August, in the rain, with armfuls of a damp sheet held against their chests like firewood. "The Articles of Agreement in full! Twopence! Read the Marquess's Misprint, sir, the Articles in full and the carter's tale!"

It had been, until that hour, a remarkably sweet time, or as near as made no difference. The last week in Kent had gone to the Subscription, which by the twenty-second had raised six hundred and eleven pounds from landlords who had read it over their breakfasts and, to their own astonishment, paid; the water had gone down in five days, leaving a mark on the tithe barn at the height of Dr Pym's chin; and on the Thursday the Pargeters had come home to London by the Maidstone road without once going into a ditch. Since then Julian had called at Hand and Pen Court on the two days Article 2 required, and not a minute over. He stood at the far end of the composing room and handed her proofs by the corner. Mr Orme had remarked that it was the most correct courtship he had seen in fifty-two years. Winifred had counted the days to Michaelmas aloud, to the Drip, and found that the figure had begun to frighten her for a new reason, which was that it was getting small.

On the Saturday morning at half past one, Dick Sparrow did not come in.

The Gazette's forme was locked up, the Albion was inked, and the second pressman's place at the bar stood empty. Fairweather, asked where he was, said "Aye," with unusual weight, and went on inking. So Winifred and Fairweather pulled the inner forme between them, two hands short, with Toby on the balls, and the sheets went to the cart at a quarter past five with eleven minutes to spare; and it was twenty past six, and she was sitting down for the first time with a heel of bread, when she heard the cry in the Row.

Toby came in at a quarter past seven with his paper hat over one eye and a sheet inside his jacket, which is where a boy keeps what he has been told not to read.

"Don't," said Winifred.

He read it aloud. It was a long column, and he began in a loud voice, and grew smaller as he went. It was headed *The Quizzing-Glass*, and was by Peregrine Pry, and it began:

> I may be quite wrong, but I have long thought that the most dangerous thing a printer can set is her own name. Readers of this paper will recall that on the sixth of June the *Gazette*, which had never been known to be mistaken, informed a delighted town that the Marquess of Stanmere was engaged to Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row. We are enabled this morning to lay before them the Articles by which that engagement was made, and kept, and, as it is hoped, ended: a document which for neatness of drafting has not been equalled since the Fall.

Then came the Articles, all seven, from the heading to the imprint, in a clear Long Primer; and the imprint at the foot, which Winifred read three times, had been altered. *Printed by H. Lovejoy, Phoenix Press, Ave Maria Lane.* They were word for word as she had set them. They were the same to the comma. Whoever had set the thing had had it before him, flat, in a good light, and had kept even the space before the sixth.

"Toby."

"There's a bit more, miss." He swallowed. "There's a carter."

> We have this further from the carter who for nineteen years has taken the inner forme from Hand and Pen Court to Pomfret Yard, and who has the plain honesty of his trade. He states that on the morning of the sixth of June, at a quarter past five, the young lady stood in the arch of the Court with a wet sheet in her hand and said nothing, and let the cart go. We make no remark. A compositor, it is said, does not read; she sees letters. The reader will decide for himself how many it takes to make a Marquess.

Toby stopped there. Winifred put out her hand and he gave her the sheet, and she folded it very small, and went to the table, where her mother's stick-case lay, and turned the key at her throat, and lifted the baize. The foolscap was where she had left it, flat, with the smudge at the foot of the sixth paragraph. She laid it open beside the Crier. They were alike in every line, and it was this, rather than anything the column said, that she looked at for some time. Her paper had not been taken. It had been looked at.

* * *

Mr Underhill came at nine, in a hackney, which he never did, pink and trembling, in a coat buttoned wrong, with the Crier crushed in his fist like something he had caught.

He stood in the door of the composing room with the water running off his hat. He had a beautiful voice, the best in Fleet Street, and it shook. "Mr Pargeter. Miss Pargeter. I have come to say what a gentleman must say in person. The *Gazette* has been made a laughing-stock. In the Strand this morning, madam, a boy sold me my own paper with the Crier folded round it. In nineteen years the *Gazette* has not once been shown to have printed what was not so."

"The paragraph was a misprint, Mr Underhill," said Winifred. "It is a thing that happens."

"It is a thing that has never happened to the *Gazette*."

"It happened to the *Gazette* on the sixth of June."

"Clause Nine." He drew himself up. "An error of the press *requiring* a correction. The Crier has shown that the paragraph of the sixth of June was an error of the press. It requires a correction."

"Then print it. It is a single paragraph. I will set it myself."

"The *Gazette* does not retract, madam."

"You might clarify."

"At this length," said Mr Underhill, "it is not clarity. It is confession." He swallowed. "The clause, madam, says *requiring*. It does not say *printing*. Mr Ingram, who read the contract at my request, is of the opinion that it is satisfied by the requirement. The contract is void. The bond is forfeit. Five hundred pounds, secured, I believe, upon this house."

"Secured upon—" said Winifred, and turned.

Her father had come down on his gouty foot, with his spectacles on his forehead, and was standing at the foot of the stair with a hand on the newel. He looked at his daughter with the face of a man who has been caught in a moment of his own.

"Win," said Barnaby. "I meant to tell you on the fourteenth of June. Mrs Hobday came in with the soup."

"Mr Underhill," said Winifred, after a time, in a voice that had gone extremely polite, "Mrs Rudge would wish to be told."

"Mrs Rudge is at Bath for her chest, madam, and I will not distress her. I take no pleasure in this. Nineteen years." He turned his ruined hat in his fingers and looked at no one. "I wish you good morning."

He went out. In the corner, at the case by the window, Mr Orme, who had set type every working day since the year 'sixty-four, put his stick down upon the ledge of the frame, and laid his hand flat on top of it, and did not pick it up again. It made no sound. It was the loudest thing in the room.

* * *

Mr Lovejoy came at ten. He was in his snuff-coloured coat, with his hands scrubbed pink and his hat in front of him, and the two pairs of spectacles pushed up on his forehead; and he bowed to her as a gentleman does who has come to a house in which there has been a death.

"Madam. I shall not detain you. I do not come to gloat; it is a vice of the untidy." He glanced at the Crier in her hand. "The press of this morning is mine, madam. The prose is another person's. I never print a lie. I print a true thing in a place where it will do the most harm, and I do not think that anything in it is untrue."

"No," said Winifred.

"That is what I thought." He turned his hat once. "I am obliged, in the circumstances, to mention the notes. Under the third clause the whole is payable on demand upon the loss of the *Gazette*. The sum, with Mr Trigg's account, is one thousand one hundred and forty pounds. I do not make the demand. I mention it, as one mentions a window that has been left open. In consideration of the long acquaintance between our houses I shall wait until Michaelmas."

"Hector," said Barnaby from the stair, in the voice of a man speaking across a long time.

"Mr Pargeter." Lovejoy inclined his head. He went, with great tact, to the far end of the room, to the stone, where the forme of Tuesday's *Gazette* was standing, and bent over it with his glass, as a man looks at a house he used to live in.

Julian came into the Court at a quarter past ten. He had walked from Curzon Street, two and a half miles in fifty minutes, without a hat. Gantry had brought him the Crier on the salver where in June there had been cards; he had read it standing; and he came into the composing room with the water running out of his hair, white, a tall man a little stooped, and saw Lovejoy at the far end of the room with his glass, and bowed to him, and turned to her.

"Miss Pargeter."

"My lord."

"I have read it." He was turning the signet. "I have come to say this. I can have a special licence from Doctors' Commons by noon on Monday. St George's, or St Vedast, or your own parlour if you prefer it. The Crier cannot touch a Marchioness. It will not dare. A wife is not a party to any Articles; she is a wife."

"A special licence."

"Yes."

"It is what a lord uses to make a mess disappear." She had not raised her voice; she had gone, as she did when angry, extremely polite. "I will not be disappeared, my lord."

"I did not—"

"You would correct the Misprint by making it true. It is a very handsome erratum. I am not an erratum to be corrected by matrimony."

He took it as one takes a blow in the dark: without a sound, and with the whole of the frame. At the end of the room the glass was lowered. Mr Lovejoy straightened, and put the glass away on its ribbon, and came forward with his hat in both hands, and bowed.

"I beg your pardon, madam. In so small a room I could not help hearing the end. I came only to thank his lordship for his call on Tuesday. It was handsomely meant."

Winifred did not move. "His call."

"At Ave Maria Lane, at eleven, in person. His lordship offered me twice the sum for Mr Pargeter's paper: two thousand two hundred and eighty pounds. I confess I admired the arithmetic. I declined." He bowed again. "I mention it, madam, since it is true, and since it may be useful. It is why the Crier appears today, and not at Michaelmas. A man who sees a marquess coming up the Lane with a cheque-book prints early. Good morning."

He went out through the arch. The rain shut behind him.

"Is it true?" said Winifred.

"Yes."

"You signed Article the Third."

"I signed it before I—"

"Before you what?"

He stood in the composing room of Pargeter's with the water running down his neck and his mouth open upon the word. It was a short one. He had never in his life said it aloud in front of a witness. It would not come. He had signed it, he might have said, before a barn, and a rope, and a sleeping girl's hand in the dark; before a great many things, none of which was in the Articles; and he stood there with it in his throat, like a sort in the wrong box, and she, who could not abide a gap in a line, looked at him, and let it stand.

"Of seven Articles I asked for one," said Winifred, "that was not for the *Gazette's* sake. It was the third."

"I know."

"Then you will understand that I shall keep them all, to Michaelmas, because I keep my word. And that I do not wish to see you before. You are released from the second." She picked up the stick from the ledge, and found that it was Orme's, and put it down. "Good morning, my lord."

He bowed. He turned his ring once on his finger and went out into the rain.

* * *

He walked the whole length of Cheapside. He did not choose it. He turned east out of the Churchyard because it was the way his feet were pointed, and he walked past the Poultry and the Mansion House and the shop windows, with the water running down his collar, among hawkers who cried the Marquess's Misprint in his face, and a man in a doorway who looked up, and recognised him, and looked down again with the tact of the English poor. He did not hear them. In his head a single sentence was going round, in the voice of a boy of nine: *I decided that I would not marry unless it were impossible not to.* He had said it to her on the thirtieth of June, in the library, with the sunset in the window. He had not said, because he had not known it, that the same boy would grow up to be a man who could not ask a question without first having arranged the answer.

At the end of Cheapside he found that he was not in Curzon Street, and had not been, for some time.

* * *

By four in the afternoon the composing room was empty but for the cat, the Drip, and Barnaby, who had been put to sleep in his chair by the fire with the Crier over his face, like a handkerchief, and his spectacles pushed up over it. Orme had gone home to his lodging in Warwick Lane. Fairweather had gone to the pump and stood there. The rain fell steadily on the skylight, and the Drip fell on the stone, every four seconds, into the copper pan, which nobody had emptied since the morning.

Winifred stood at her frame with her mother's brass stick in her hand. She had not set a line. She was holding it as she had held it at seventeen, the week after the funeral, when she had not known what it was for; and the thumb-rest, worn bright by a woman who set the Psalms, was cool against her thumb. She was conscious of the pan. She ought to empty it. She did not move.

A shuffle behind her. It was Toby, who had been sent to bed three times and had come back, and who now stood at her elbow in his paper hat, with his ears sticking out and his freckles very bright, chewing; and in his hand, held out with the care of a man handing a document across a table, was a pie. It was Mutlow's. It had been, a few hours ago, a whole one. There was a bite out of it, and a neat clean edge where he had broken it in two. He had never in his life been known to offer anyone food.

Toby held it out. It was, she saw, the larger half.
