# If Any of You Know Cause

On Michaelmas Day, which fell that year upon a Sunday and rained, the clerk of St Vedast, Foster Lane, stood up in his box under the pulpit, at the end of the second lesson, and read the banns for the first time.

The notice of them had been taken on the Saturday, at ten minutes past four, in the dining-room of the Reverend Mr Lathom, who was at that moment at his dinner, and had been delivered by a printer of fifty-nine with a gouty foot in flannel and a boy of thirteen with his mouth full, both of whom had arrived at the door at a run, which for the printer was an achievement. Mr Lathom had taken it, and put down his fork, and had not stopped smiling since.

"I publish the Banns of Marriage," said the clerk, in the voice of a man who has been reading them for thirty years and never until today been listened to, "between Julian, Marquess of Stanmere, of the parish of St George, Hanover Square, bachelor, and Winifred Pargeter, of this parish, spinster. If any of you know cause, or just impediment, why these two persons should not be joined together in holy Matrimony, ye are to declare it. This is the first time of asking."

There followed the silence that the Prayer Book provides for and that nobody has ever entirely believed in. It was a wet silence. It had the rain on the leads in it, and a gutter of Sir Christopher Wren's discharging into a bucket that had been put there for the purpose, and four hundred persons not coughing, which was three hundred and eighty more than St Vedast had held on a Michaelmas since the Peace. The reader, who knows of impediments enough to furnish a vestry, that the engagement was a mistake and was kept up as a lie and was ruined by a friend and mended by a Quaker, is requested to keep her seat. It is the one occasion in this book on which the reader's silence is required.

Nobody spoke. In the Pargeter pew Barnaby wept steadily into a handkerchief, with his foot on a hassock, and looked about him for someone to tell it to. Mrs Hobday sang under her breath. Toby sat with his ears pink and an air of having personally arranged the Church of England. Lady Constance, who had come in a closed carriage with her trumpet, took it from her lap and put it on the pew beside her, which was the nearest she had ever come to being at the mercy of a sermon. And in the last pew of all, alone, in a snuff-coloured coat, with his hat on his knee and the glass on its ribbon at his waistcoat, sat Mr Hector Lovejoy, who had walked there from Ave Maria Lane, a distance of two hundred yards, and was looking at nothing in particular with every appearance of being at a funeral he approved.

He was waiting in the porch when she came out. The rain had thinned to a drift, and the Churchyard was full of persons being looked at; but he stood by himself against a pillar, small and neat, and when she came out on her father's arm he stepped forward and gave her a flat parcel, wrapped in a half-sheet of the *Gazette*, about the size of a visiting card.

"Madam."

She undid the paper. Inside lay a single capital W, cast in best type-metal, still bright, with its face as sharp as a pin and both feet whole.

"The old one has a cracked foot," said Mr Lovejoy. "It is in the Pargeter fount. Your father cut the matrices in 'eighty-two, and I dressed that fount for seven years, and I had it by heart. I should not like to think of it going out into the world in a state." He bowed to her, and to Barnaby, who had stopped weeping in order to stare. "I wish you every happiness, Miss Pargeter. I have no doubt that you will have it. You have been exceedingly well set."

He went away down Foster Lane in the rain, and did not look back. He did not mention the notes, or the *Crier*, or the labels, or Saturday. He had said what was true and useful, and he took it with him, as he took everything, in clean hands.

* * *

Saturday's *Gazette* had sold out by nine, and been reprinted on the Monday, and by the day of the wedding its circulation stood at three thousand, a number at which Mr Underhill, who had once feared that the truth would ruin him, now looked with the haunted pride of a man who has been proved right by accident. The *Crier*'s account of Sowerby had been cried in the Churchyard at six, and nobody wanted it; the hawkers sold eleven, and one of those to Mr Lovejoy.

Mr Underhill had framed the Erratum in gilt, and hung it above the stove, and showed it to visitors with the air of a man who had always believed in it. Mrs Rudge, in the ninth month, had appointed Miss Agnes Fell to a weekly column under her own name, at a salary that would clear a lieutenant's hosiery in two years; and Miss Quarles, who had a ledger and a purpose, had in the meantime bought the hosiery from Mr Lovejoy at par, and Mr Lovejoy had sold it with an alacrity that surprised nobody who knew that he disliked being owed money by persons he might meet in church.

The Duchess's cards were on the stone. Dick Sparrow had come back on the Monday, in a clean neckcloth, and Fairweather had looked at the neckcloth for some time and said "Aye", in a manner that left Mr Sparrow in no doubt about the neckcloth. And the Farmer's name was now the Marquess's, in every county in England, and the Weald Relief Subscription stood at nineteen hundred pounds.

The banns were read on the same three Sundays, the twenty-ninth of September and the sixth and thirteenth of October, in St Vedast and in St George's, Hanover Square, to a silence of a different but equally expensive kind. On the fifteenth, which was a Tuesday, a post-chaise from Dover with the mud of three countries on it drew up at the door in Curzon Street, and a tall woman got out of it in a travelling-cloak.

He had been on the step since ten. Gantry had given up asking him to come in. She stood on the pavement with the rain on her hood, fifty-three years old, grey-eyed, with a mouth that had once said something to him and not been heard, and looked up at the house, and at the man on the step.

"You are taller," said Cecilia, Marchioness of Stanmere.

"I was nine, madam," said Julian. And then, because the word was on the paper somewhere in Lombard Street and he had crossed it out: "Mother."

She looked at his nose. "Who did that?"

"A Greek verb."

"Ah," she said, and came up the steps.

In the hall, at the foot of the stair, Lady Constance Dyer stood with Blücher in her arms and her eyes shut. She had stood so for twenty minutes, by the clock. The two women regarded one another across the black-and-white marble, the one with her eyes shut and the other with her hood back, and the pug, who knew a great occasion when he smelled one, turned his single eye from the one to the other and began, quite quietly, to wag.

"Constance."

Lady Constance opened her eyes. "Cecilia." And then, with the exactness of her whole life: "I gave him the last."

"You gave it to him."

"In the end."

"In the end will do," said Cecilia.

* * *

It did not shine. The reader will have wished for sun on the seventeenth of October, and the narrator would be glad to supply it, having promised it; but he has undertaken not to lie, and the morning came up the colour of dishwater over the roofs of Foster Lane, with a wind off the river and a smell of wet slate. It stopped raining, however, for the length of the vows, which in the year 1816 amounted to very nearly the same thing.

Winifred went up the aisle on her father's arm at eleven o'clock in a gown of dove-grey sarsenet, bought out of the Duchess's cards at four and sixpence the yard from a man in Cheapside, which was the fourth gown of her life that she had paid for herself. She had her mother's seed-pearl brooch at the throat and her mother's brass rule through her hair. She wore gloves on neither hand. She had meant to wear the left one and had left it, at the last, upon the hall table, and Lady Constance had seen it lying there and said nothing, in a tone that could be heard in the street. The thumb was as God and Fairweather's ink had made it, a clear blue-black to the ball. She carried it before her like a banner, and the church, which was full, had its first sight of a bride with a trade.

Barnaby had a new coat. He walked the length of the church with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, where for the first time in her recollection he did not forget them, and he gave her away in the tone of a man handing over a very fine press. "Her mother," he had said in the vestry, "would have said you'd improved the line." Toby walked before them with the ring on a composing-stick tray, lined with baize, in a paper hat he had been made to remove and a collar that he had not. The ring was Naomi Lamb's. It had been in Barnaby's waistcoat pocket since the March of 1807, thin at the inner edge from nineteen years of lye. Brevier, in a hamper, was in the vestry; the church had been told that she was a hat.

They were all there. Orme, with his hand cupped to his better ear, had set the wedding card, and had pronounced it, backwards, perfectly plain. Fairweather, in a blue coat, carried his fiddle in a green baize bag on his knee, like an infant, to be played at the breakfast, badly and with devotion. Verity sat beside Rupert, who had a drayman's arms and a gold signet on his own hand once more, as of Saturday the twelfth, on which day she had answered, to everyone's satisfaction but his own surprise. Agnes sat behind them, with her bonnet in her lap. Minton sat very straight, with the stopped watch in his pocket and the sketchbook in the other. Dr Pym, who had been in the porch a quarter of an hour with a rain-gauge, was observed to look at the leads when the rain left off, and was heard to say, "That will be the rods," and this time somebody laughed. It was the Dowager Duchess of Ormsgill, in a purple turban, in the front pew, who had been speaking to the late Duke throughout the service and had paused to enjoy it. "Ormsgill would have hated the sermon," she said, at the carrying pitch, "so I am enjoying it enormously."

Mrs Rudge was not in the church. Friends do not go into steeple-houses. She stood in the porch, under a large grey umbrella, in her coal-scuttle bonnet, with her better ear turned inward toward the aisle, and her pencil in her hand, and she heard every word of it. She would not have missed it. "Julian Stanmere," she said, when the Marquess came out, "thee has made a good beginning. I shall look for the rest in the number after next."

Julian said "I will." He said it to the vicar; he said it clearly, in the voice in which he had read figures to sixty tenants from a hay-cart; and it was observed by those who could see his hands that the signet he wore was no longer round. It had been bent to an oval in an iron collar, and the griffin on it was a smear; he had put it on the smallest finger of his right hand, where it just went, and the hand shook, a very little, upon the book.

* * *

The register was in the vestry. It lay on the table under the window, a huge book in a leather cover, with a bottle of ink and two pens and Mr Dibben, the clerk, standing behind them, a grey man in black, whose hand was a monument and whose spelling was not. He had entered the particulars beforehand, as the custom was. There were the names of the parties, and the parishes, and the date. *Julian, Marquess of Stanmere, bachelor, of the Parish of St George, Hanover Square. Winifred Pargiter, spinster, of this Parish.*

Julian saw it first. He saw it at the speed at which he saw all errors, which was before he had finished looking at the page. He had been correcting other people's signboards since he was eight; there was a pencil in his hand that was not there, and he could feel the weight of it. He reached for the pen.

"I believe, sir," he said, "that there is a—"

Winifred put her left thumb on the back of his hand.

It was the thumb that had held the stick since she was seventeen. It was the colour of a bruise, and a little tacky at the ball, for she had been at the case at six that morning setting the last of the Duchess's cards, and the lye had not taken all of it; and it left on the back of the Marquess's hand a blue-black print, the size of a shilling, as his had once been left on a sheet of foolscap in a printing house in June. She did not look at the register. She looked at him.

"Stet," said Winifred.

He stood with the pen in the air. He had said that word himself, once, under an archway, in the rain; it had been his word for the whole of his life, and he had never until that moment known what it was for. Then he put the pen down on the table, and took her hand, and said to the clerk, who was waiting with his mouth slightly open, "Mr Dibben, it is a printer's word. It means let it stand."

"Very good, my lord," said Mr Dibben.

She signed with the other pen, on the line beneath, and wrote Pargeter, with an *e*, in the small upright hand of the proof-margins, a quarter of an inch under the clerk's *i*. Then, when the ink was dry, she laid her thumb beside it and pressed, and took it away.

* * *

The breakfast was held in the printing house, because she would not have it elsewhere. Orme's cases had been pushed back against the wall, and the imposing stone was laid for forty under a white cloth that Mrs Hobday had been starching since the Peace, with a wedding pie from Mutlow's in the middle of it the size of a drum. Fairweather played the fiddle. The Albion was hung with ivy, and the Old Lady with a bishop's lawn, lent by a bishop's chaplain, who had not yet been told. Cecilia and Constance sat side by side by the stove, not speaking, with the pug between them, with the distance of two women who have a great deal to say and twenty-two years in which to say it. Mrs Rudge, who would not enter a church, entered the printing house, which she regarded as a place of business, and read the wedding card through with her pencil and found a comma and left it.

"Are you really married to our Win?" said Toby, from under the table, where he had retired with the better half of the pie.

"I am assured that it is in the register," said Julian.

Winifred, who was cutting the pie, looked up at him. It was her sentence. She had said it on the staircase at Ormsgill House with a straight face, and in a drawing-room in Kent with a crooked one, and a woman of eighty had told her in a lane that it was a lie, because she could not read it. Now it was true, and could be looked up. She smiled; it was a smile she had not used before, and the shop took it as it had taken the Misprint, with a little silence and then a great noise.

It was sometime after four, when the Duchess had said at the carrying pitch that she had forgotten more weddings than anybody present had attended and this was the one she should remember, and Rupert had been seen to kiss the top of Verity's head in a passage, that Winifred went and stood by the stone. The Drip had been mended. A slater from Cripplegate had come in the week of the banns, and taken eleven pounds, and the water had stopped on the fourth of October at ten minutes to three, and the household had stood about the stone and observed a silence, and Mrs Hobday had said grace over the absence. It rained on the roof now and not through it, which is the difference between a house and a hope. There was no pan on the stone. The slab was dry, and the light fell on it from the skylight in a long grey square.

She found that she missed it.

She stood there with her thumb against the cold stone, and listened to the rain going over the new slates, and the fiddle, and the voices of the people she had been given, in the order in which they had happened. Julian came and stood by her without arranging anything. After a time he put his hand over hers, the ruined one, with the print of her thumb upon it still.

* * *

The register of St Vedast, Foster Lane, for the year 1816, may be seen on application to the incumbent. Under the date of the seventeenth of October there is an entry in the hand of Mr Dibben, whose penmanship was admired by three bishops, and the name of the bride is spelled *Pargiter*. The signature beneath it is *Pargeter*. No one has ever corrected the entry. There is no note in the margin. Beside the signature, if the page is held to the light, there is the print of a thumb, the colour of a bruise, which the clerk took for a blot. It is not a blot. It is what a proof-reader puts in the margin against the things that are worth leaving as they are, and the word for it is *stet*.
