# High Water

At a quarter past ten that night the Medway came up the lawn at Stanmere Court in the light of forty lanterns, black and wide and in no hurry at all. It had taken the Ham meadows at dusk, and the lower orchard and the ha-ha before nine; and it advanced now across the grass toward the terrace with the leisurely thoroughness of a bailiff taking an inventory, noting the garden seat, the sundial, the gardener's barrow, and the first of the stone steps.

The Marquess of Stanmere had asked, as his first act, how many head of cattle were on the Ham; and as his second, how many persons lived below the hundred-foot line; and Mr Wragg, who knew everything about the estate except until lately the spelling of his own name, had answered both to within a dozen. From these two numbers everything followed. Grooms went down to the Ham with lanterns and halters and brought the cattle off in the dark, lowing and unwilling, up the cart-track to the home paddock. A rider went to Yalding to have the church bell rung. The tithe barn was thrown open, and Mrs Pickett's girls set to baking.

Lady Constance commanded the kitchen like an admiral from the head of the scullery stairs, with the pug under one arm and the trumpet under the other, issuing orders in a voice that carried to the bakehouse without the assistance of the trumpet; and Verity Quarles took her station at the barn door with her ledger and her spectacles on their chain, and counted heads as they came in, and by midnight had forty-three and a dog.

Dr Pym, to his own surprise, was useful. There was a tide-gauge in the portmanteau, which was what had clanked from Wrotham to Yalding: a brass staff, graduated in inches, with a float and a bell. He planted it at the foot of the terrace steps and read it aloud every ten minutes in a voice of mounting joy, which was a mistake in a man whom the weather was vindicating, but a comfort to everyone else; a river that is being measured seems less likely to do something unprecedented.

Winifred had watched the water from the brewhouse window for as long as it took to understand it, and then had done what she did with every emergency, which was to ask what wanted setting. She found Julian on the terrace in his shirtsleeves with a lantern.

"Which of the cottages have a clock?"

"None."

"Can they read?"

"A third."

"And can they hear the bell?"

"Not through this."

"A bell says *come*," said Winifred. "It does not say why. Give me a quarter of an hour."

It took her six minutes to set, in Great Primer, three lines of four words, a thing she had been practising for thirteen years without knowing it. She set it twice on the stone, two-up, so that every pull gave two bills, and a hundred pulls would give two hundred; and Barnaby was carried in, chair and all, by two Johns, and put at the ink with his foot on the bucket, and Toby beat the balls, and Winifred took the bar. Miss Fell sat at the table by the lantern with a pair of scissors and cut the sheets across, one after another, without being asked, and without looking up.

She pulled two hundred in forty minutes. Print was not a thing that did anything quickly at Yalding. It came on Thursdays in somebody's cousin's pocket and was eleven weeks old. Now a page of it reached a cottage while the water was still on the lane. Toby took the bills to every door on the low ground, in a washtub found in the scullery, paddling with a coal shovel, since the lane had already ceased to be a lane; and Brevier sat in the bow with an expression of complete resignation. And at the last cottage but one, a boy of ten named Jem Fryer, who had been taught his letters by the curate on Sundays, read the bill aloud to his grandmother in the kitchen with his finger under the words; and Mrs Fryer, who was eighty and could not read, put down her knitting and said, "Well, if it says so," and put on her coat.

They went out into the dark. The lane was already a stream to the knee, and the only thing left to do was to climb the highest thing within a quarter of a mile, which was Fryer's kiln.

* * *

It stood a hundred yards from the terrace, across what had been the ford of the Beult: a round brick oast with a conical roof and a white cowl, and on the roof, in the light of forty lanterns, an old woman and two children. Rupert and Wragg launched the hay-boat to reach them. The current took it at the hedge, turned it twice like a leaf, and drove it down the lane; and it fetched up against a pollard willow, with Rupert in the water to his chest and his coat gone, and Wragg, grey and silent, hauling him out by the belt. They had got a line to the kiln, but it was the one thing they had accomplished, and Wragg had left a boot.

Barnaby had it drawn on his knee with a wet finger. He sat in his chair on the terrace with his foot up and an oilskin over his head, designing, in the light of a lantern held by a groom; and his daughter, coming from the press with ink to her elbows, found him in a state she had seen only at the birth of the Albion.

"My dear girl, it is the simplest thing in the world. A line from the oak at the end of the ha-ha to the kiln, which they have, and hauled taut with the Old Lady's block and fall. A traveller on the line. A sling made of her own girth-strap. The people come across above the water like Sunday linen on a line." He looked up. "The only difficulty is that somebody must go out."

"I'll go," said Julian.

He had come up behind them. He was bareheaded and wet to the skin, in his shirt and breeches, with his boots off, and a bowline had already been put about his chest by a groom who knew the knot. There was a short silence on the terrace, in which the river said what it had to say, and Winifred opened her mouth, and found that she had nothing to put in it. She had given him no leave, and he had not asked it; it was the first thing he had ever done in her presence that he had not arranged. "Mind the traveller," she said, which was not what she meant.

"Quite," said Julian.

He went out along the line, hand over hand, with the traveller hauled after him; and for twenty yards he was a tall man crossing a river in lantern-light, and for the rest of it, where the current had the line, he was a pale shape, going down by inches and coming up. The men on the fall paid out the rope. At the kiln he got the children into the sling, one after the other, the girl Patience first, and the fall brought them in, and Wragg handed them out on the bank to a dozen hands. He came back for Mrs Fryer.

"I'll not be put in a basket like a cheese," said Mrs Fryer, from the roof, at the top of her voice.

"Then you shall be put in my arms," said the Marquess.

He took her down in the sling with himself. Barnaby's rig was beautiful, and had one fault, which is the fault of all beautiful things: it assumed that the rope would not stretch. A wet rope stretches. With two persons on the traveller the line sagged in the middle until the sling trailed in the current; and the current, which had been waiting for something to do, took the Marquess of Stanmere by the legs.

They saw the sling come in. It came up the line, jerking, with the men hauling on the fall like a team at a tug-of-war, and in it, held by the shoulders in a thing like a bag, was an old woman in a man's coat with her mouth shut tight. There was nobody else in it.

"Julian!" cried Winifred.

She had not said it before. It is a short name, and it carried a great way. She was in the water before the sentence was done, up to the waist and then past it, with the line in both hands, which she had taken for the traveller's rope, and she was aware of Wragg beside her, who had called her the printer woman for a week and now took hold of the rope behind her without being asked. The slack was coming in, wet and heavy. It had a weight at the end of it. She hauled, hand over hand, with the cold going up her like a ruler, and something came up out of the water on the end of the rope that was a man's shoulders and the collar of a shirt; and she took him by the collar in both fists and Wragg took him by the belt, and between them, with the river pulling, they dragged him in on the stones.

He was on his hands and knees. He coughed up, so far as an observer could judge, the greater part of the Weald. When he could speak he looked up at her, with the water running out of his sandy hair and the white notch in his eyebrow filled with river, and said:

"Quite."

* * *

At three in the morning, in the tithe barn, the Fryers slept on straw under horse-blankets: Jem and Patience, and Mrs Fryer in the midst with her stick across her knees and a limp wet handbill clenched in her fist, which she would not give up. "I can't read it," she had told Winifred, who had sat by her with tea, "but Jem read it me. It said the water was up. It was." It is a notice that a printer might have framed.

In the far corner, by a bale, under a blanket that smelled of horse, the Marquess sat with his back to the wall and his hair drying in spikes, and Winifred sat beside him with her hands in her lap. They did not talk. After a time one of her hands was in his, under the blanket, in the dark, as if it had been left there, and he held it as one holds a thing one has been told not to drop. Nothing was said about Michaelmas. Forty-five days, she thought, with her head against a bale; and then she was asleep.

Barnaby came across the barn at half past three with two bowls of Mrs Pickett's broth, limping, with the gouty foot in a flannel boot and a lantern in his teeth. He put one bowl in the Marquess's hands and sat down upon the next bale with a grunt, and looked at his daughter, asleep, with her mouth a little open and her mother's brass rule coming out of her hair.

"My dear fellow," said Barnaby, low. "You will have observed that I have a daughter."

"I have observed."

"She carries things. She has carried me eleven years, and the house, and a quantity of Psalms. I have sat in a chair with my foot up and let her, and called it the gout." He stirred his broth. "I have been thinking it over this evening with my leg in the air, and I find that I would very much sooner ask the Marquess than let my girl carry it alone. The notes, I mean. It is a matter of some eleven hundred pounds. I do not say it is a fine thing to ask. I say that I would rather."

Julian did not move. The signet turned once on his finger, in the dark, and stopped.

"I am bound, sir," he said, "by Article the Third."

"Quite right." Barnaby nodded slowly. "Quite right. I printed them, I recall. No, she printed them; I merely wept." He drank his broth. "They are wiser than they look, my dear fellow, those Articles. They will keep a proud girl from being obliged to you until she can afford the obligation. It is the only way, I find, to give her anything. I should know. I gave her a printing house."

* * *

Toward dawn, Julian went out to the barn door with the blanket about his shoulders to see what the water had done. It had stopped. It had drawn back in the night as far as the ha-ha, and stood there brown and shining, with Fryer's kiln in the middle of it like a rook on a ploughed field; and above the oasts in the east the sky had turned the colour of an old penny. It was the first hour of that sort of light in a month. It lay in a thin copper line between the cloud and the drowned meadows, no more than a pencil-stroke, and every pool on the lawn took it up and held it.

Behind him, at the other door, Miss Agnes Fell stood in her shawl. She had not slept. At the gate to the avenue, on a pony the colour of wet bread, in a sack for a coat, sat the Yalding post-boy, fourteen years old and blue with cold, who had come up by the high road to say that the post-office was under three feet of the Medway and that the mail would go from Wateringbury at seven if anybody had anything for it.

Miss Fell had something. She had it in the pocket of her wrapper: a single sheet of foolscap, folded into a cover and sealed with a wafer, and directed in a round law-stationer's hand to *H. Lovejoy, Esq., Ave Maria Lane, London*. She had written the direction in the night, between the second hour and the third, at the kitchen table, while the household baled the scullery about her. Nobody had looked at her. Nobody ever did. In the course of that night she had boiled the kettle eleven times, and cut four hundred slices of bread and butter, and held a child with a cut hand while Mrs Pickett sewed it, and by her own computation she had done more good between ten o'clock and six than in the whole of the previous twelve years put together. She crossed the wet gravel and put the packet into the cold hand of the post-boy, and a shilling after it; and it seemed to her that a little more sin would hardly show.
