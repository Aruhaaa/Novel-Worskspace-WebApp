# A Dinner Afloat

At four o'clock on the afternoon of Thursday the fourth of July the kitchen of Lady Constance Dyer's house in Hertford Street stood eleven inches deep in water, and the water was still arriving.

A gutter above the area door, designed by a man who had never seen it rain, had overflowed into an area drain that had not been cleared since the death of General Dyer in 1811; and the area, having nowhere else to put what it was given, had sent it down the steps and under the door and into the kitchen, where it had risen among the legs of the table, the dresser and the coal-scuttle, and was now lapping at the bottom bar of the range. Sixteen persons were to sit down to dinner at eight. The Dowager Duchess of Ormsgill, who had not arrived anywhere before the appointed hour in thirty years, had sent word that she would be in Hertford Street at half past seven. And on the stairs, a little above the water-line, with her trumpet held out before her like a rod and the one-eyed Blücher under her arm, stood Lady Constance, regarding the flood as Moses regarded the sea, in the expectation that it would see reason.

It did not. At the foot of the area steps a French cook of enormous moustaches, lent for the evening from the Ormsgill kitchens, had just thrown a copper pan at the wall, and stood with his arms folded among the debris saying "C'est impossible" to the ceiling, which had not asked him. Mrs Bunting, who had cooked in the house for thirty years and never wept at a bill, was weeping into a roller towel over the turtle. It lay in a tub by the stove, a creature of thirty-eight pounds, the Duchess's own gift, three guineas' worth of Jamaica, with its chin in the water and its eyes shut. "He was lively at noon," said Mrs Bunting, "he were lively as a lamb. And the Duchess coming. And the General's own copper, and a flood—"

"I beg your pardon," said a thin dry voice by the stove, addressed to no one in particular, "but I think the turtle is not dead."

Winifred, passing at that moment down the plank of the scullery door with a bucket in either hand, looked round. It was Miss Fell, in her grey gown and her mittens, standing ankle-deep with her skirts held up in one hand and her eyes upon the tub, with the fixed polite attention she gave to everything, as though it might presently apologise. Nobody else had heard. Mrs Bunting was in her towel, Monsieur Gaspard in his apostrophe; Lady Constance, on the stair, was occupied with the flood. Winifred put down the buckets, rolled up a sleeve, and put one finger into the tub, and the turtle opened an eye, and shut it with the weary contempt of a magistrate.

"It is not dead," said Winifred.

She was a good deal amused. She had come at four o'clock, summoned by a note to be coached in the matter of forks, with a band-box in her arms and a printer's instinct for the exact moment of disaster; and she had stood at the top of the area steps and recognised it at once. It was a forme gone to pie. There were a great many things, none of them in the right place, and one of them was water, and the water was the Drip. One did not argue with the Drip. One worked round it.

"Mrs Bunting," she said. "What is wanted first?"

"Miss?"

"What must be on the table first, and at what hour?"

"The soup, miss, at eight. But there's no soup, there's a turtle, and the range is—"

"Then the soup is a quarter to eight, and the rest can go on the housekeeper's hearth." She took the pencil from behind her ear and wrote on the back of a bill of fare which had been floating by the dresser. "Who has the key of the coal-hole? There will be planks in a house with a sedan chair. I want six, the length of the kitchen, and a chain of buckets to the area steps, and everyone who can lift one. Monsieur."

"Mademoiselle," said Monsieur Gaspard, with a bow of enormous irony, "it is impossible."

"Il est possible," said Winifred, in the French she had learned from forty menus, "mais il faut déplacer le feu." She pointed up the stair. "The range is a fixture, like the stone. The saucepans go on the hearth in the housekeeper's room, and the copper in the laundry will do for the turbot." She paused, as he drew breath. "I do not know anything about cooking," said Winifred, "but I know a great deal about being late."

He looked at her for some seconds. Then, in the manner of a man who has found, in an unexpected quarter, a soldier, he unfolded his arms, and shouted, in English, for the knife-boy.

* * *

By six o'clock the thing was in the way of being done, on a system that Hertford Street had never seen and did not recognise as one. Planks ran across the water from the scullery door to the range like the bridge to a fortress. Buckets went up the area steps hand to hand in a chain of two footmen, the knife-boy, the scullery-maid and the butler in his shirtsleeves, with Verity Quarles, who had arrived at half past four in her brown dress as she had promised, at the head of the line, counting. "Eleven inches over three hundred and fifty square feet of open floor is two thousand gallons," Miss Quarles had announced. "A bucket holds two, and a chain of six at twelve a minute will have us clear in an hour and a half." They were clear in eighty-one minutes. She was a little sorry that it was not eighty.

The soup, such as it was, stood on the housekeeper's hearth above stairs in a copper that Monsieur Gaspard regarded as a personal insult, and the pastry had gone to the first-floor pantry on a tray carried by the butler like a sacrament. The box of place-cards, which had been left on the pantry floor, had become a pudding; so Winifred, on the kitchen table, in ten minutes, with the butler's ink and a hat-box lid cut into sixteen squares, lettered new ones in a plain Roman hand. She set the table by shape. A dinner of sixteen is nothing but a forme: the heavy matter must go to the edges, the quarrelsome must be locked apart, and the thin must be set between the stout so that the whole will carry. She put Sir Marmaduke Crake between two long ladies, and Lady Quarles, who was apt to faint, beside the colonel with one arm, who was apt to catch; she put Verity next to Lord Rupert, because the one had a head for figures and the other an urgent need of them.

At twenty past seven a voice at the top of the area steps said: "I understood there was a flood."

The Marquess of Stanmere had come in his evening coat, sent by Constance, or by Minton, or by something in himself that he had not examined, to see whether anything remained of the house. He stood on the bottom step with his hat in his hand and the water at his boot-heels, and looked about him at the planks, the buckets, the Frenchman and the printer in the centre of them with a ladle for a baton; and Winifred, who had not heard him come, looked up, and her face did a thing that she afterwards put down to the heat.

"Good," she said. "Hold this."

She had stooped, as she spoke, to the tub by the stove, and lifted out of it the dripping and extremely indignant turtle, and put it into his arms. It was green, with a yellow belly like a Dutch cheese, and it looked at the Marquess with an expression of the profoundest disapproval.

"What is it?"

"A turtle. It is not dead."

"I am relieved to hear it."

"It is to go up to the dry pantry on the first floor, and be put on a chair with a cushion, and nobody is to say anything to it. Mind the third plank. It is not nailed."

He minded the third plank. He carried the turtle across the kitchen with his head up and his back straight, in a black coat that had cost forty guineas, as another man might have carried a sleeping duchess, and mounted the area steps and went through the baize door and across the black and white of the hall at precisely twenty-nine minutes past seven, at which instant the footman, ignorant of everything, threw open the street door upon the Dowager Duchess of Ormsgill.

She stood on the threshold in purple satin, with her turban and her stick and her tiny glittering ruined face, and nobody behind her but a page. She looked at the Marquess, and at the turtle, and at the Marquess.

"Julian," said the Duchess. "That is a turtle."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Is it dead?"

"I am assured not."

"How very inconvenient for the soup." She advanced into the hall, as an army advances into a conquered city. "Take it round by the back. I have not been greeted with a turtle since the Peace of Amiens, and then it was only the Prince."

* * *

She dressed in four minutes, in the housekeeper's room, with the housekeeper holding the candle and the knife-boy holding the shoes; and came to the drawing-room at eight with her hair damp under the rule, her cheeks very pink with the heat of the hearth, and her left glove drawn up over a thumb that no ordinary soap would have shifted in a week. Nobody in the room perceived that she had come by way of the coal-hole.

The dining-room was of the greatest austerity: fifty-two degrees by the thermometer on the wall, as it had been on every day of every month since the General died, the fire being lit only for the departure of the guests. His cases of stuffed birds had been moved into it for the occasion, to add to the company, and a heron the height of a boy stood on the sideboard with a fish in its bill and an air of having been invited. Barnaby had been asked, and had declined, on the ground that someone must stay with the Drip.

Lady Constance sat at the head, with her trumpet laid beside her fork. The Duchess had the place of honour, with Winifred on her left and Mr Underhill, the editor of the *Morning Gazette*, on Winifred's other hand; Julian sat directly across, so that Lady Constance might give him the pleasure of watching. Beyond them were Sir Marmaduke Crake, M.P., and his lady; Sir Ephraim Quarles, who said "Plain terms" to the footman, and Lady Quarles, who looked about her for a sofa; Verity; Lord Rupert; Mrs Underhill, who spoke in a whisper and ate in a hurry; two bishops' widows; a Colonel Fenn with one arm; and Miss Fell, at the foot, in her grey gown, whom Lady Constance had asked to make up the number as one sets a salt-cellar to level a table.

Mr Underhill was large and pink, with a voice so beautiful that it seemed a waste to have put it to the service of libel law. He had been invited to keep the *Gazette* sweet. He seized upon Winifred before the soup was down, and turned the whole organ of it upon her. "Pargeter's! My printer! My dear young lady, I cannot express to you what a pleasure. The *Gazette* has been a matchmaker!"

"It was the *Gazette* that said so, Mr Underhill," said Winifred.

"Exactly! Precisely so! It was in the paper and so it was. I do not call it chance, Lady Constance," said Mr Underhill, to the head of the table. "The *Gazette* does not retract, madam. It clarifies. And in this case I venture to say that it has clarified a marriage."

Winifred put her hand flat on the table, beside her plate. She did not say anything. It is no light matter to be congratulated on one's greatest crime by the person whom it has most nearly ruined, and she found, on examination, that the best course was to look at the soup.

It was a very good soup. It was a calf's head, with a quantity of green things, passed in the candlelight as turtle by a cook who had been told that it must be so passed; and it was received with a murmur. "A great deal better than I looked for," said the Duchess, to the table at large. "I should not have thought a turtle capable of so much."

Sir Marmaduke Crake, M.P., a broad man who had been waiting for the soup to be removed in order to deliver himself of an opinion, delivered it with the fish. Wheat, he said, stood at seventy-four shillings the quarter and was rising, and he called that prosperity. Where the farmer throve, the nation throve, and he would answer for it before any man.

"I beg your pardon, Sir Marmaduke," said Winifred.

The table, which had been talking, ceased to talk.

"A quartern loaf was thirteenpence in the Row on Tuesday. A labourer in the Weald earns nine shillings in the week, and a household of five eats seven quartern loaves in seven days. That is seven shillings and sevenpence in bread alone, and leaves one and fivepence for his rent, his fuel, his shoes and his tea. Where the farmer thrives," said Winifred, "the labourer has one and fivepence."

There was a pause such as follows the dropping of a plate. Sir Marmaduke had turned the colour of the lobster sauce. "Madam," he said, "who is your authority?"

"A Kentish farmer. I set his type."

Across the table the Marquess of Stanmere, who had been engaged with a wing of pheasant, put his napkin to his mouth and made a noise like a man swallowing a coin. It was a larded pheasant, three months from lawful, sent up from the ice-house at Ormsgill Park with a note that said only *Eat them*, the Duchess regarding the Game Act as an opinion; and a small bone of it had entered his windpipe at the exact moment when the Farmer was named. He choked. Rupert thumped him on the back with the energy of a man who has been waiting years for the opportunity. Lady Quarles, finding the thing too much, laid the back of her hand to her forehead and slid slowly from her chair into the arms of the colonel with one arm, who caught her with the practised grace of a man who had done it before; and for a minute and a half the dinner at Hertford Street was, in the Duchess's own words, as good as a play.

"You have been reading the Farmer," said Verity Quarles, round the lady in the colonel's arms, with her spectacles on, in the tone of one who has seen a ledger come right. "The figures are perfectly sound. I checked the seventh against the Corn Returns."

"I did not doubt it," said Winifred. Across the table, with the bone gone and his eyes streaming, the Marquess of Stanmere looked at her over his napkin and found that he could trust neither his windpipe nor his ears.

* * *

The menu cards stood by each plate in their silver holders. They had been ordered by Lady Constance from Mr Pratt of Bond Street, who was stationer to the nobility and, in her view, to nobody else, and they had been delivered at six that evening by a boy straight to the dining-room, dry. Winifred had not looked at hers. A person who sets type for her bread does not read a card in the ordinary way; it is borne in upon her, as a smell is, from across a room. She picked it up, looking for the date, and the card told her, in four places, in the voice of a small shrill relation at a wedding, everything that was wrong.

> *At Lady Constance Dyer's, Hartford Street. Turtle Soup,. Turbot with Lobster Sauce. Saddle of Mutton. Pheasent, larded. Sallad.*

There was the comma stranded after the soup like a hair on a plate. There was *Pheasent*. There was *Sallad*. And at the head, over the whole, in a copperplate of the greatest elegance, every one of sixteen cards had the street wrong: *Hartford*, with an *a*, and not Hertford with an *e*, the name of the street in which Lady Constance Dyer had lived since 1787.

Her thumb went out of its own accord. It was the old proof-reader's gesture, and she had made it ten thousand times: the little loop that says *dele*, take out, strike. It passed across the *a* in *Hartford*, and the blue-black thumb, in its bareness, lay on the cream pasteboard beside the offending letter. And stayed. She looked at Lady Constance at the head of the table, upright in her bombazine, with a high colour under the powder, who had gone to a stationer in Bond Street rather than a printer in the Row because a Dyer did not take her cards from the people she was obliged to; and who had been so proud of them that she had not read them.

She lifted the thumb. She made no mark.

"You saw the street," said the Duchess, at her elbow, in the voice in which she said everything, which carried to the saucepans.

"I saw it."

"And?"

"It is her house," said Winifred.

The pins in the Duchess's face went out of focus, and then came back very bright. She looked at the girl beside her for some seconds with an expression that no one in Hertford Street, or Piccadilly, or the whole of the *Peerage*, had seen on it since 1786; and then, without turning her head, she addressed the length of the table. "Constance!"

"Ma'am?" said Lady Constance, behind the trumpet.

"You have overdone the turtle."

There was a rush of laughter that might have been heard in the Mews. Lady Constance Dyer sat bolt upright for a moment, in a state of fury so complete that it was indistinguishable from joy; and then, with a movement of great dignity, raised her trumpet to her ear, as though she had not heard, and was heard by those nearest her to say, in a low firm voice, "The turtle was exact."

It was the second time in her life that Winifred had seen an error in print and let it lie. It was the first time that she had done it out of kindness. The narrator mentions it only because the reader may be keeping count.

* * *

The moulded ice came with the dessert, in the shape of a pineapple, on a silver dish; and it stood in the middle of the cloth for eleven minutes, and did not melt. It was the first ice in the history of Hertford Street to complete a dinner in the shape in which it had begun, the dining-room being, as has been stated, fifty-two degrees; and the Duchess, who had eaten a quantity of it, put down her spoon and announced, to the cloth, that she would send her carriage to the Row in the morning to engage Pargeter's for her cards. "All of them," said the Duchess. "I have six hundred. And the funerals."

Mr Underhill said, in his organ voice, that he was gratified beyond expression.

"I was not speaking to you," said the Duchess.

When the last of the guests had gone, a little after eleven, and the street was a clatter of wheels in the wet, Winifred stood in the hall in her cloak, waiting for the Stanmere carriage to be brought round. Lady Constance had settled the matter in a sentence: the Marquess would see his betrothed to the Row, since the *Gazette* had declared them as good as married, and the distance was two and a half miles, which was thirty-five minutes in the rain, and a question of arithmetic. He was finding his hat. The drawing-room door stood ajar behind her. Through it, in the light of one candle, sat Lady Constance and the Duchess of Ormsgill at either side of the fire that had at last been lit, the pug between them on a footstool. Lady Constance had taken off her ear-trumpet. It was the first time that Winifred had seen her without it, and it made her look smaller, and her ears, which were perfectly sound, stood out.

"Well," said Lady Constance.

"Well," said the Duchess. She said it at the pitch at which she said everything. "Which of them knows?"

There was a silence in the drawing-room. The fire settled.

"Neither," said Constance.

"Good. It is much the best way."

Winifred stood with a hand on the newel. She had no notion what they meant. There was the engagement, which each of them knew to be a lie, or there was something else, and she could not tell which of the two she was afraid of. She put it away, in the place where one puts a proof that has come back with a mark one does not understand; and she would find it again before the night was out, at the instant when she least expected it, and had least armour.

The footman announced the carriage. She put on her gloves, the right and then the left, drawing the kid over the thumb that was still, in the lamplight, a faint blue, as it always would be, as it had been since she was seventeen; and she discovered, standing in the hall of a house in which she had been cold and wet and ridiculous and, for the first time in her life, wanted, that she did not mind in the least who saw.
