# A Red Sky Over Curzon Street

At a quarter to nine on the evening of Sunday the thirtieth of June the sun, which had not been seen in London since the eleventh of May, was seen.

It stood on the rim of the world behind the chimneys of Hampstead in a smear of copper, the colour of a new penny held up to a candle, and the whole of the west took fire from it by degrees: first the slates, then the cloud-bank above the slates, then the long wet length of Curzon Street, which lay down under it like a ribbon dipped in rust. The rain, as though from a sense of occasion, had stopped. By ten minutes to the hour every step in Mayfair had its household on it. Footmen stood bareheaded, housemaids held up their aprons, an old gentleman in the Square had been brought out in his chair by two grandsons and sat with his mouth slightly open, like a man at a play he does not follow and has been told is very fine; and at the top of the steps of Stanmere House, in his black coat, with his hands behind his back, stood Gantry, who was sixty and had been in the family for forty-four years, and had seen a Prince drunk and a Prime Minister sober and was moved.

"It is an omen, my lord."

"It is dust," said Julian, from the doorway.

"Of what quantity, my lord?"

"Considerable."

"Very good, my lord," said Gantry, who went on believing in the omen.

Julian had been at the library window since eight, with a pencil in his hand and nothing to mark. He had been trying to see his house as she would see it. It was an exercise he did not recommend. Stanmere House was tall, cold and correct; it had been built by a man who disapproved of comfort and had the means to enforce his views, and its stair carpet was the exact grey of a clergyman's opinion. Minton had put out the place-cards, and taken them in, and put them out again. Gantry had been asked for a plain dinner and, in the course of the afternoon, had translated *plain* into five removes. A fire was lit in the library grate, in the last day of June, because Gantry held that a fire was a form of welcome and that the library, without one, resembled a tomb in which somebody had been so careless as to leave the books. And the Marquess of Stanmere, who had given dinners to the Cabinet and the Corn Committee and the Dean of Westminster without feeling anything more than a mild wish to be elsewhere, found that his hands were damp, and wiped them on his coat, and was ashamed.

At ten minutes to nine a hired hackney came along Curzon Street at the walk, with its lamps unlit and its driver standing up on the box to see the sky, and stopped; and Mr Barnaby Pargeter was lifted out of it by a footman and the secretary, with his foot in its flannel and his spectacles on his forehead, like a monarch being conveyed to a coronation he had been dreading.

* * *

Of the dinner there is nothing to be said that a short paragraph will not cover, which is the case with most dinners and the whole of the reason that novelists make so little of them. There was Gantry's soup. There was a boiled fowl, a tongue, a saddle of mutton and two removes of which nobody afterwards could say what they had been. There was a footman, whose name was William but who was called John, as were all the footmen at Stanmere House, the sixth Marquess having found names tiresome; and this footman held a tureen for twenty minutes at Mr Pargeter's right hand, unable to put it down, while Mr Pargeter explained the toggle-and-lever action of the Albion to him with the assistance of two forks, a salt-cellar and a spoon. There was Minton, who ate three courses with his watch open beside his plate and said nothing whatever, and who removed himself with the dessert as a man withdraws from the scene of a pleasant crime. And there was, throughout, a sort of gap at the table, which all three of the guests of the house of Pargeter felt, between the courses, as a tongue feels the place where a tooth has been: the absence of a small flat note, falling every four seconds into a copper pan.

Article the Second, which required that Miss Pargeter dine at his table once, was satisfied at the fish. The Marquess did not notice it go by.

It was a little after ten when the cloth was drawn. By then the copper had gone out of the sky behind the house and something deeper was coming in. Barnaby, who had been walked by degrees into the library, passed through it like a man in a cathedral: slowly, with his hat in his hand though he had no hat, and his head tipped back, and his lips moving. "My dear fellow," he said, at the foot of the gallery, "I have never seen so many books that were not for sale." And he sank, with a sigh like a bellows, into the best chair, which was a great wing-backed leather affair by the fire, and laid his foot on the stool that Gantry had set for it, and said, "I shall close my eyes for a moment and consider the folio sizes," and was asleep.

"He has been up since four," said Winifred. "There was a leak."

"There is always a leak."

"In this house?"

"I should be surprised if there were not one in the roof of my own mind," said Julian. "But I do not think there is one in the slates."

She had taken off her gloves. It was the first time he had seen her do it in company, and she did it with an air of having decided on the stairs: the right, then the left, drawn finger by finger and laid on the arm of a chair, so that the left hand lay on the dark leather in the firelight with the thumb uppermost, blue-black to the ball, like a bruise that had been taught to read. He looked at it and then, with an effort, at the shelf.

* * *

The shelf stood in the east alcove at the height of a man's elbow, and Winifred, who had not asked what was on it, had walked to it as a needle goes to the north.

He kept there what he called, when pressed, his ephemera. There were ballads, a pile of them, the *Last Dying Speech* of a quantity of gentlemen whose last speeches had been set up in type before the occasion; an eighty-year run of almanacs; a tavern bill for the year of Culloden; a playbill from Drury Lane with a hole burned in it; and in a frame of black wood, under glass, a single leaf from a great folio Bible.

"The Vinegar Bible," said Winifred, bending, with her hands behind her back so as not to touch. "Oxford. Mr Baskett's folio of 1717. The head of Luke the twentieth: the Parable of the Vinegar."

"For *Vineyard*."

"A basket-ful of printer's errors, the Row called it, Baskett being his name. Which is just the sort of thing the Row says when it is jealous." She straightened. She looked at the tavern bill and the burnt playbill and the ballads, and at the last, lying by itself on a velvet cloth, the Last Dying Speech of one Josiah Tapp, with its black border, and a line at the foot in a different fount. "'The above Person has since been Reprieved.'"

"I bought it for a shilling in the Mint. I think it the finest erratum in the language."

"It is a masterpiece." She laughed, a low startled sound. "Do you collect them?"

He took down from the shelf a japanned box, the size of a prayer-book, and opened it. It was full of slips of paper, long and narrow, yellowed, thick as a pack of cards: *For* Antioch *read* Antium. *Page 86, line 11, for* the *read* then. *For* Gothic *read* Gaulish. Two hundred and eleven of them, each cut from the back of a book or bought by the dozen from a stationer in Holywell Street who thought him mad, and each marked on the back, in his small even hand, with the date.

"As a boy," said Julian, "I thought that a thing printed was true. I do not mean the opinions. I mean true in the way that a brick is hard. And then I found one of these pasted in at the back of my father's Gibbon." He had the slip in his fingers: *For* Gothic *read* Gaulish. He was not looking at her. "And understood that print could say it was sorry."

"How old were you?"

"Nine."

She said nothing. He noticed it, because it was the thing about her that he could least account for: a woman who answered every question by stating a fact, and who could, when the occasion wanted, leave a question lying on the floor between them like a dropped glove, without picking it up, as though a space were a thing she knew how to set. She was looking at the wall. She was looking, with the most perfect indifference, at the wall between the two windows, where the green silk, five feet by three, was a shade paler than the silk about it, as though a window had once been there and been boarded up; and a ghost of a nail stood in it, at the top, in the middle.

She did not ask; and Julian, who had never in his life been spared a question by anyone, discovered that he could not bear it.

"There was a picture," he said.

"Yes."

"My mother." He put the slip in the box and shut the lid, and found that he had the signet in his fingers, turning. "She lives in Florence. She went there in the autumn of 1794, when I was nine, and my father had the portrait taken down the same week. It has been in a crate in the cellar for twenty-two years. I have not had the wall done over. It seemed an expense to no purpose."

"Is she living?"

"She is, I am told, well."

"Told by whom?"

"By my aunt. At intervals. In the manner of a weather report."

"Does she write?"

He had been asked a great many questions in his life by a great many people, with every degree of delicacy, and had met them all with a *Which?* and a number. "No," said Julian.

It fell between them. Over the fireplace, in a gilt frame of enormous size, the sixth Marquess of Stanmere regarded the room from beneath a plum-coloured coat and an expression of having just been contradicted at dinner and being resolved to win; and Julian, who had been under that eye for thirty-one years, observed to his surprise that it did not look at him but at the pale rectangle, steadily, as though the portrait and the place had been put there to quarrel.

"My parents' marriage," he said, "was a long quarrel conducted at dinner, in the best manners, in front of a child. I was the child. It is very instructive. One learns to pass the salt." He heard his own voice, level as a rule. "I decided, at nine, that I would not marry unless it were impossible not to."

"And is it impossible?"

"It is in the *Gazette*," said Julian.

She laughed. It was not a success; it came out of her chest like something carried too far, and she put the back of her bare hand against her mouth, and over it her eyes were very bright. He found that he wished to say something. It was a very peculiar sensation, since he did not know what it was; he had the sentence ready, and none of the words.

She said it for him, or said something. She had taken the rule out of her hair. The hair fell down on one side, which she did not appear to notice, and she laid the brass strip in her hand, in the firelight, long and flat and warm, and turned it over to show him a worn place along the edge. "This was my mother's. The stick is the other half of the set; it is at home. She was a compositor's daughter and she set the Psalms in Long Primer at nineteen, in a back room over a chandler's in Warwick Lane, in four months. It is bright where it is bright because she had a thumb like mine." The thumb lay on the brass. "She died in March of 1807. It was a consumption. I was seventeen."

"I am sorry."

"The Row came to the funeral. That is the thing I remember. My father couldn't do anything, he sat in the parlour and looked at the floor; and the night before, a compositor from every house in the Row came to our door, with his stick, and each of them set a verse of a hymn in his own house's fount, and we pulled it at six in the morning, on a single sheet, on the Old Lady. It was in eleven different founts. It looked like a sampler." She smiled, not at him. "It was the ugliest sheet ever printed in London and I have it still in my Bible. I took the stick the week after."

"It is not ugly," said Julian.

"No. It is the opposite of ugly. I haven't a word."

He had one, or the beginning of one, and let it go by. It would be a long time before he said it, and when he did, it would be in a church. He said instead, after a pause that he could not have measured, "Thank you."

"For what?"

"For telling me which hymn."

"I did not tell you which hymn."

"No," said Julian. "I find it was not necessary."

* * *

They discussed Lord Rupert's courtship for something under half a minute, with great restraint on both sides, as two persons might discuss a dog that has been left in the hall.

"He has called three times," said Winifred.

"I am glad he is attentive."

"He brought pinks."

"I know. Your father told me, at length." The corner of his mouth went. "I now know the Latin name, the price at Covent Garden in three seasons, and that a pink is a carnation which has not been given the chance. He meant it, I think, as a character reference."

"It was a very good one," said Winifred, and Julian said "Quite," and Barnaby, in the best chair, with his chin on his chest, turned over, and said clearly in his sleep, "Not on the tympan, you fool," and was quiet again. They looked at each other and, in the firelight, did not laugh, which was an achievement.

The window had gone from copper to red, and from red to something darker, a slow rich colour like the inside of a shell or the shadow in a glass of port. It lay over the roofs of Mayfair in a long smoulder. The street below was silent. Julian went to the glass, and she came with him, bareheaded, with her hair on her shoulder and the rule in her hand, and they stood side by side with a foot of clean air between them, looking at the end of the world. Out in the Square somebody's footman was weeping, or laughing; one could not tell at that distance.

"It will be in the Farmer, I suppose," said Winifred.

"I beg your pardon?"

"He ought to write about it. He has written on everything else." She had put her forehead nearly to the glass. "Weather, rents, bread, the poor-rate. He cannot let this go by."

His mouth was dry. "What would he say?"

"That nobody can explain it," said Winifred, "and everybody is pretending to."

He did not answer. It was precisely, to the syllable, what he had been going to write; he had composed it in his head at eight o'clock that evening, standing at this window with the pencil, and had not set it down. He stood very still. His right hand, with a will of its own, had begun to turn the ring.

"Is it a good thing to say?" said Winifred. "I thought it a rather good one."

"It is exactly the thing."

"He will think it his own, then. They generally do." She laughed softly. "I never knew a writer who did not think everything good had been taken out of his head."

* * *

At half past ten the Pargeters departed in the hackney, with Barnaby carried down the steps by two Johns and a secretary, awake and saying he had never in his life shut his eyes and that the folio sizes had been entirely satisfactory. Winifred went last, with her hair up again and the rule through it, done without a glass in four movements. She put on her gloves on the step, the left over the thumb, and looked up at the sky. "It is going," she said; and it was. The red had ebbed into the black behind the roofs. A first thin rain, blowing along Curzon Street out of the north-west, had begun again, as though the sky had only stepped out for a moment and come back. "Thank you for the dinner. It was very pleasant. I never in my life had so many forks."

"I will tell Gantry. He will put in for a medal."

"Good night, my lord."

"Good night, Miss Pargeter."

The hackney went off at the walk, with its lamps lit now, and its wheels hissing on the wet. Julian remained where he was, on the top step, in his evening coat, with the rain coming at his face and a last long bar of crimson across the roofs behind him, so that he threw a shadow down the steps as long as the street. Gantry had gone in. The old gentleman in the Square had been taken home. He stood, a tall and slightly stooped man of one-and-thirty, with a pencil still in his hand, knowing that one person in London now knew what was in the crate in his cellar, and that he knew in return what had been set in eleven founts on the Old Lady in March of 1807; and that the exchange had been made without a receipt.

He remembered that he had signed Article 6.

He had signed it with a tolerable laugh. He tried the laugh now, on the step, in the rain, and found it had been used up.
