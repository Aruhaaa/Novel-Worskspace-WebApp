# A Brewer's Terms

The dray-horse in the yard of the Black Bull Brewery was the size of a small church, and had much the same expression of settled disapproval. He stood in his shafts with his head down, a Suffolk the colour of wet bread, while two draymen in leather aprons rolled hogsheads up a plank behind him, and he regarded Winifred Pargeter as she came in through the gate as though she were a person who had not paid her pew-rent.

The smell had reached her a quarter of an hour before the brewery did. It had met her on London Bridge, and by the time she turned in at Bankside it was a hot brown sweetness of malt and wet horse and spent hops, which took hold of the back of the throat and invited it to stay for supper. It was the twelfth of September, a Thursday. It was drizzling, as a matter of course.

"That is Bishop," said Verity Quarles, at the head of the counting-house stair. "He will not hurt you. He disapproves of everyone equally, and it is restful."

The counting-house was a glass box at the head of the yard, with a stove, a high desk, and four walls of ledgers lettered on their backs in a hand like a row of barrels. Verity wore her brown dress. It had been to three routs and a flood, and had never, Winifred thought, been so well employed as it was now, with a pencil laid along the sleeve and the spectacles down on their chain. She took Winifred straight to the desk, without a word about the weather, as a good cook takes a visitor to the larder.

"Look," she said. "Hops, Lady Day to Midsummer. Bought, forty-one pockets. Brewed, thirty-three. The other eight go out of the back gate on a cart to a gentleman in Deptford, who sells them back to the hop-factor at a third under, and gives Mr Halloran a commission for the introduction. Every month since February of last year. I make it eleven hundred pounds."

Winifred counted down the column with one finger. She could not help it; a column of figures on a page was a line of type to her, and it had a measure. "The seventh entry is wrong."

"The seventh entry is where he began to be careful. I have not told him." Verity took off her spectacles. "I have told his clerk that I have mislaid a column. Mr Halloran has been so kind as to offer to help me find it."

Winifred laughed, which she had not done in a fortnight, and found that it hurt in a place she had been keeping shut.

"Papa will deal with him in the spring," said Verity. "I should like to be there. But that is not why you are here. You are here for plain terms, and Papa has been looking forward to it since Monday, and here he is."

Sir Ephraim Quarles filled the door as a cask fills a cellar. He was broad and bald and sixty, with a face like a well-baked loaf and a voice that had been used to cross yards, and he took a stool, and set a mug of small beer on the ledger, and looked at Winifred without any appearance of being in a hurry. She counted four minutes by the counting-house clock, in which neither of them said anything much; and at the end of them she saw that she had been liked.

"Plain terms, Miss Pargeter. What do you want, how much, how long, and what do I get?"

"Twelve hundred pounds, Sir Ephraim, for three years, at six per cent."

"Security?"

"The Albion, the stock, and my word."

"I'd put 'em the other way about."

"So would I. But my word is not in the inventory."

"Hah." He drank. "I'll tell you what I get, then, and you tell me if it's fair. You print every label this house sends out, for three years: Entire, Porter, Double Stout, the Christmas Ale. Handbills. Dray-tickets. At a fair price. I'll not have it said I kept a printer out of charity. I keep a printer to print."

"Mr Lovejoy has had them fifteen years."

"He has. He sets a handsome label. I shall miss the bull." Sir Ephraim set down the mug. "But I do not employ a man who printed my wife's fainting fits in a gossip sheet. I've seen what the *Crier* said of Lady Quarles at the Waterloo rout, and I would not mind it if it were wrong. It was perfectly right. A man does not like to see his own opinion in the hands of Hector Lovejoy." He leaned forward. "Is there a Marquess in this?"

"There is not."

"Good. I distrust 'em. A man who has never asked a price is a man who has never been told one." He took a paper from his waistcoat, a banker's draft, already written, and blotted it on his sleeve. "Twelve hundred. It's my own money, mind, not the brewery's. The brewery is Verity's in nine years, and I'll not have Halloran's fingers in it."

"Papa," said Verity.

"I do not say a thing I have not said to his face."

"You have said nothing to his face. You have not noticed anything."

"I've noticed that you've been unusually kind to him," said Sir Ephraim, "and I've taken it for what it is." He looked at his daughter with an expression that was very nearly the only soft one the counting-house had ever seen. Then the door opened.

Lady Quarles came in with a vinaigrette in one hand and a gown the colour of a fine claret, which had not been made for Bankside. She was fifty, and she came into the counting-house of her husband's brewery as a swan comes into a stable-yard. "Ephraim, the carriage has been standing forty minutes in the smell. Miss Pargeter! My dear, I had no notion that you were come upon business."

"Mamma," said Verity, "Miss Pargeter is going to print our labels."

"Labels?"

"Trade, my dear," said Sir Ephraim. "Trade. Augusta. Trade."

Lady Quarles went down gracefully, with her hand to her brow, upon the third ledger of the year 1811. The clerk, a Mr Pike, who had evidently been through this before, fanned her with a price list.

"She'll come round at the sound of the kettle," said Sir Ephraim, regarding his wife with tranquil approval. "She likes to be left. It's her one accomplishment, and I'd not interrupt a woman at the head of her profession."

It was at this point, as is the way of comedy, that someone knocked.

* * *

He came in with his hat in his hand, in an old brown coat with leather at the elbows, such as is worn by persons who buy hay; and he wore no waistcoat at all, which on Lord Rupert Tremayne amounted to a moral position. He looked at the party in the counting-house: at Sir Ephraim, at Winifred, at the lady on the floor with the price list going over her like a banner. "I have come at a bad moment," he said. "I generally do. It is the only way I know of arriving at a good one."

"Rupert," said Verity.

"Miss Quarles." He bowed. Then he looked for something to do with his hands, and tossed no guinea, there being none to toss. "I came to propose to you, and I find I do not know how it is done without a waistcoat."

"Plain terms," said Sir Ephraim, from his stool. "Have you a penny?"

"None, sir."

"Good. It saves time."

"Papa," said Verity, "I will do this." She put down her pencil. She had Rupert's signet on her thumb, the gold one she had won from him at forfeits in Kent, and she turned it round, as Julian turned his, which Winifred noticed and said nothing about. "Say it, then."

He said it, to his credit, standing quite still. "I owe two thousand three hundred pounds to eleven men and a glover. I can ride, dance, pick a horse, and tell you which of three will break its leg. I have been bored for nine years and find you the only thing that has stopped it. I do not want your brewery. I should be extremely bad at it. I should like to marry you, and I should rather like to be told what to do."

"Eleven men and a glover," said Verity.

"I forgot the glover. I wished to be honest."

"I shall buy the paper. All of it, from the whole twelve, at five per cent. You will owe me instead of them, which is a convenience to your creditors and a great deal worse for you." She was counting on her fingers. "And you will go on the drays."

"The—"

"A month. Fourteen shillings a week, which is what Josh has, and Josh has a wife. I will not marry a man who believes that beer comes in barrels by Providence. If you are on the dray on the twelfth of October you may ask me again, and I will answer."

"Am I not to be told the answer?"

"No."

"Splendid." Rupert went to the glass and looked out at the yard. "My dear girl. That is not a horse. That is a cathedral chapter."

"His name is Bishop."

"Of course it is," said Rupert, and went out to be introduced.

When he had gone down the stair, with a cheer from the draymen at the bottom of it, Verity sat for a moment looking at her hands. "He will never forgive me for the barrels," she said. "That is the foundation of a happy marriage. I read it in a book of Papa's. It was about the Navy."

* * *

Winifred came out into the yard with the draft in her glove at a quarter to twelve. It had stopped raining, which meant that it was merely dripping, and the whole brewery steamed gently like a horse. She was crossing to the gate, in the shadow of the weighing-room, when she saw who stood in its door.

Mr Lovejoy had his hat in front of him. He wore the snuff-coloured coat, and the glass on its ribbon, and a flat parcel of proofs under one arm, and his hands were scrubbed to the colour of boiled ham. He had printed the Quarles labels for fifteen years. He had come, she understood, for the second time that week, to be told by Sir Ephraim a thing he had been told on Monday, because he was a man who liked to hear a sentence twice, to see whether it kept.

"Miss Pargeter."

"Mr Lovejoy."

"I came with the proof of the Christmas Ale. A handsome bull. I cut the horns myself." He looked at the parcel, and not at her. "Sir Ephraim was good enough to explain with great clearness that it will be set elsewhere."

"I am sorry for it."

"You are not, madam, and it would be a poor compliment to you if you were." He bowed. "I allowed for Curzon Street. I did not think of Bankside. It is an oversight, and I make so few that I find I notice it."

"I did not go to Curzon Street," said Winifred.

"No. I perceive that."

"There is still the matter of the carter."

"I know about the carter, Mr Lovejoy. I shall be telling it myself."

He did not answer at once. He looked at her as he looked at a page that had been given him to lay out, with his head a little on one side, working out what could be taken away and what must stay. It was not a look she had had from him before. She had had the other, the one that fell upon a nicked sort. It lasted about as long as it takes to say *Great Primer*.

"That is unusual," he said at last.

"Yes."

"I have not a rule for it."

"I can discharge the notes tomorrow," said Winifred. "In full, in coin, if you will name the hour. I should be glad to."

"I do not doubt it." He inclined his head, with real courtesy. "But notes payable at Michaelmas are payable at Michaelmas, madam. I could not think of taking yours a day sooner. It would be a discourtesy to the instrument."

It was his right. It was the only unkindness he permitted himself, and she saw that he knew it was one, and that he took no pleasure in it, and that he would do it all the same, as a man dresses a wound he has himself been obliged to make. He put on his hat and went out through the gate, small and neat, with the proofs under his arm, past the church-sized horse, who did not look at him either.

* * *

They walked back over London Bridge, she and Verity, to the bankers' in Lombard Street, with the river going out under the nineteen arches like a long brown thought. Winifred counted the arches. She always did.

"You miss him," said Verity.

"Like a tooth."

"I thought so. Teeth are a nuisance. One keeps putting one's tongue in the place." Verity walked as she did everything, in a straight line. "He sent me a note on Monday. One line. *Is she well? J.* I sent it back by the same boy with *Ask her* written on the bottom."

"I told him not to come."

"Then it is hardly surprising that he is not coming."

"I am not surprised," said Winifred. "That is the tooth."

Messrs Garland and Pell counted twelve hundred pounds into a strongbox lent for the purpose by Sir Ephraim, an oak-and-iron affair with a brass bull's head on the lid and a lock like a bishop's. Winifred had asked for it in gold, in a form that could be counted aloud on a stone, and the clerk had said that gold was dear. Verity had said that so was a good name. At three the box went home to Hand and Pen Court upon the Black Bull dray, behind Bishop, with two draymen sitting on the lid and Verity on the shaft; the great horse leaned into Ludgate Hill like a church being moved, and the whole of the Row came to its windows.

Fairweather carried it up to the second floor in his arms, like an infant, and said "Aye." Mrs Hobday had it put under her bed beside her hymn-book. "Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth," she said, "where moth and rust doth corrupt. And meanwhile, under the bed."

When they had all gone down, Winifred stood in the housekeeper's room a minute with her hand on the lid. Twelve hundred pounds. She had not been given it. She had been given the chance to earn it back at a fair price, which was better, and she had gone and done the bargaining herself, and not one word of it had been in a Marquess's voice. She was as nearly happy as she had been since the thirty-first of August, and she wanted, with a violence that surprised her, to tell someone.

She knew with perfect exactness who. He was at that hour in Curzon Street, two and a half miles off, thirty-five minutes by hackney in the rain; and she had asked him not to come.
