# Lady Constance Inspects the Goods

The morning-room at Hertford Street was so cold, and so full of stuffed birds, that Winifred Pargeter, who had been admitted by a butler with the manner of a man conducting her to the scaffold, felt she had been shown into an expensive larder. There were one hundred and forty birds. The late General Sir Hugh Dyer had shot them in four countries and had them stuffed in all of them, and they stood upon every shelf and bracket and in a great glass case by the window: kites, bustards, a griffon vulture with the expression of a landlord, two flamingoes that had been pink in Portugal and had since gone the colour of old soap. A fire was lit in the grate and gave out, as far as she could judge, the sentiments of a candle in a cathedral.

It was a quarter past eleven on Monday the tenth of June. She had been sent for. The note had said, in a hand like a row of railings, *Lady Constance Dyer will receive Miss Pargeter at eleven on Monday. She is not asked whether she is free*; and Winifred, who had read the note on the stone, upside down and from a distance of nine feet, had replied that she would be free until three, when the copy came.

"Sit," said Lady Constance.

Winifred sat. A pug the size of a muff came across the carpet, regarded her left ankle with its one eye, and set his chin upon it, where he stayed. "That is Blücher," said Lady Constance. "He is judging you. He has judged a bishop and found him wanting. Fell, the tea."

A thin woman in grey rose from the shadow of the vulture and poured. She was past forty, long in the neck, with her hair going to silver in one white streak at the left temple, and she wore mittens, though it was June, indoors, and the pot was no hotter than the room. She handed Winifred a cup with a small stoop, and then, as the pot gave a dribble, said to it in a soft voice, "I beg your pardon," and set it down.

"Miss Fell," said Lady Constance, "has been in this house twelve years and has never repeated anything, because nobody has ever asked her anything. You may speak before her. Now. Do you dance?"

"I am able to do country dances, my lady. My aunt in Hackney had a fiddle."

"Which dances?"

"Sir Roger de Coverley. The Dashing White Sergeant. Another, which she called Ruth, I think, in honour of a former tenant."

"French?"

"I read it. I set a menu in French once, for a Mr Beauchamp of Cheapside who wished to impress his brother-in-law. I corrected his accents, and he did not speak to me for a month."

"Horse?"

"No."

"Music?"

"The Old Hundredth," said Winifred, "and a good deal of the Chapel's songs, which I should not sing in the presence of your birds."

"Do you read?"

"My lady, I read for my living. Upside down, as a rule, and backwards, and when I am rested I can do it in the proper direction." She added, because the old woman had brought up the lorgnette and was staring at her through it like an inspector of cattle, "I will show you if you like. Anything. At a distance."

"The desk," said Lady Constance.

It stood by the window across the room, a writing desk of walnut with a leather top, and on its slope, among the pens and the pounce-pot and a heap of tradesmen's accounts, lay a letter with the seal broken and the cover turned towards the room. It was a good nine yards. Winifred looked at it as she looked at all print, in pieces, and let the pieces come. "The top bill is from a poulterer in Leadenhall Market. It is two pounds, eleven, for a goose and a hen pheasant, and it has been sent twice. The one under it is the sweep. And a cover with a Tuscan postmark, a ring with FIRENZE round the edge of it, and the direction in a lady's hand, running uphill: *Lady Constance Dyer, Hertford Street*, and then a line beneath that begins, *My dear*—"

Lady Constance's hand went out, with no hurry at all, and turned the letter face down.

It made no noise. A letter being turned over seldom does. But the room, which had been cold, became suddenly a degree colder, as if one of the flamingoes had opened a window; and from somewhere behind her Winifred heard Miss Fell's cup go gently into its saucer; and she knew, by some trade instinct older than manners, that she had read something that was not hers to read, and that the Row's rule, which is *set what you are given and do not read it*, had been a good rule after all.

"That will do," said Lady Constance. "Very good. Where were you taught?"

"By my mother, my lady. She was a compositor's daughter. She is dead."

"I am sorry for it." She was not looking at the desk. She was looking at the fire. "I have known since eight o'clock on Thursday morning," she said, "that my nephew is not engaged to you."

Winifred put down her cup.

"I knew it because he did not tell me first. I knew it because no Dyer has ever announced anything in a newspaper, or been announced in one; our births have been known, and our deaths, and the rest has been the business of the family. I knew it because Julian could no more have found a printer's daughter than he could have found Timbuktu without a map and a sponsor. And I knew it because he sat for ten minutes with a pencil on the page and did nothing, which is the way his father did all his worst things. I do not know what has been agreed. I do not ask. Julian has not told me, and has not denied it, which with a Dyer is a deposition. I shall not ask you. I inform you that it will be done well."

"My lady, I have not said that it is not true."

"No. You have said very little. I observe that you do not lie aloud."

"I do not."

"What do you say," said Lady Constance, "when you are asked if you are engaged?"

It was a fair question and Winifred had thought about it for three days, between the proofing of a draper's bill and the casting off of a sermon on the Deluge, and had not got further than a certain tightness in the chest. She heard her own voice say, for the first time out loud and in a cold room, "It is in the *Gazette.*"

The ear-trumpet came up, and went down. "Again."

"It is in the *Gazette.*"

"A little pause before the *Gazette,* as though it were a bishop. Again."

"It is in the—*Gazette.*"

"Better. It will be taken for reserve. Reserve is the only fashionable thing a woman can do for nothing. It is also, I observe, perfectly true, which is more than can be said for most of what is said in drawing-rooms." Lady Constance regarded her niece-to-be, so to speak, as a banker regards a draft on a house in whose solvency he does not entirely believe, but whose handwriting he admires. "You will want gowns."

"I have a grey merino and a black silk."

"You will want eight."

"My lady, I thank you. I will not take them."

"I did not say I should give them."

"You were about to. I could see it in the ribbon."

Miss Fell gave a very small sound which in any other person would have been a laugh and in her was an apology for one. Lady Constance turned her whole head. "Fell," she said. "Go on pouring."

"I will not wear a silk bought with the Marquess's money," said Winifred. "It is in the Articles. I mean—" She stopped; it was a thing she had not meant to say. "It is a principle. I will earn what I wear, my lady, or I will wear the grey."

Lady Constance looked at her for rather longer than was comfortable. Then she took from the desk, without rising, a printed sheet in a small close type, which she held out at arm's length, as a tradesman's bill is held. "The Society for the Relief of Distressed Gentlewomen," she said, "of which I am a patroness, and which wishes to circulate this appeal to fifteen hundred persons of property. I have been to Bond Street for it, and the man there is a fool and has misspelt *distressed.* The work is to be done at ordinary rates by a printer who can spell. You will put your price upon it. It will come to six pounds ten, I imagine, with the paper. And what you do with six pounds ten, once you have earned it, is no concern of mine." She swung the lorgnette upon its ribbon. "If you were to spend it upon a gown, I should not even hear of it."

"I could do it for six pounds."

"Six pounds ten. I will not have a tradesman who undersells. It is vulgar and it deranges the trade." She said it with an air of such exquisite annoyance, and was so evidently delighted, that Winifred understood, as one understands a thing in a language in which one has no vocabulary, that she had just been admitted.

The lessons followed. They were four, and they were given in a voice like a drill-sergeant's. The first was on gloves. "They exist," said Lady Constance. "I observe your left hand. I do not ask what it is. It is blue. You will wear gloves, at all times and in all company, and you will take them off at table when a lady takes off hers and not before, and you will keep the hand at your side. You will not hide it. A hidden hand is looked at. A gloved one is merely a hand."

The second was on the word *Marchioness*. "You will not use it. Not in jest, not in reverie, not in the dark of your own bed. It is the one word in the language that tells everyone what you hope, and what you hope you must never say. If you are asked, you will say that you have not thought of titles. If you are pressed you will say it as one speaks of a distant relation's disease."

The third was on dancing: "You will not." The fourth was on being looked at. "A woman who is looked at must have something to do with her face. Choose a point and rest your eyes on it. Look at the person with whom you are about to disagree. Do not smile at anyone, until you mean it. They will take that for a weapon. They will be right."

"My lady?" said Winifred, rising at last, with the pug's chin gently removed from her boot. "Why are you doing this?"

"Because I am sixty-four," said Lady Constance, "and I have been asked to nothing worth attending in twenty years. And because he will behave like a man who intends to be jilted." She said it flatly. "Do not let him enjoy it."

---

It had left off raining, which was an event, and she walked.

She took the long way, down through the wet green of the Park railings and into St James's Street, because the pavement there was clean and the clubs had their shutters back, and because the rain had stopped and that happened so seldom in that June that it seemed to require an answer. She had on her good grey, and the glove two sizes too small, and the six pounds ten shillings in her mind, laid out already as silk, a plain blue-grey at four and six the yard from a man in Cheapside she knew. She was almost cheerful. At the bottom of the street, where the shops begin to run to prints and seals, there was a small crowd on the pavement, five or six persons and a boy, standing before a window with their heads together and the look of people who have found a dead mouse in a drawer and are not sorry.

It was Mr Treacher's. She had set his handbills. She stopped behind a clergyman and looked over his hat.

The print was hand-coloured and very good. It showed a long, lean Marquess with a face like a heron, in a coat the colour of a wet pavement, correcting the figure of Venus on a pedestal with a pencil the length of a walking-stick; and riding at him from the left of the plate, on a gigantic copy of the *Morning Gazette* that had been drawn as a sort of flying carpet, came a short, broad girl in an apron and a pair of sabots, with a brass composing stick couched in her arm like a lance. Her hair was down. It had the look of a mop. The Marquess, looking round, had an expression of mild alarm, and in a balloon over his head, as Mr Treacher's artist had it, were the words, *My dear Madam, a semicolon!* The title was across the foot, in an inscription of cheerful capitals: THE MISPRINT. Beneath, a smaller line read, *Entirely Well Founded. Price One Shilling Plain, Two Coloured.*

She laughed. It rose up in her before she had decided, a short helpless snort, at the pencil and at the sabots, which she had never worn, and the clergyman turned his head and looked at her as one looks at a person who has laughed in church. She laughed again. It was extremely good. It had caught the Marquess's stoop to a hair. It had not caught the thumb; the artist had not known there was a thumb, and somebody had given her sabots instead; and she was standing in the middle of St James's Street laughing at herself when the boy at the front, a lad of twelve with a tray of lavender, said clearly, "That's her. That's the one. Look at her hair."

She stopped laughing. She did not stop all at once; it ran out of her, like the last of the water out of the pan, and what was left was a sensation which she identified, after a moment, as the difference between being a private joke and a public one, which was about three hundred persons. She took her glove from her left hand, and looked at the thumb, and put the glove back on. Then she walked on to the corner, with her chin up, and her hand at the rule in her hair, and a sort of burning behind the ears that she did not at all identify as pride.

---

At Hertford Street, that night, the house was quiet.

Lady Constance had gone to bed at ten, with the pug and a hot brick and a lecture to the housemaid on the drawing of curtains. The stuffed birds stood in the dark. In the attic, at the top of the last flight, which she climbed with her hand on the wall and her stockinged feet, a thin woman of forty-one let herself into the narrow room that had been hers for twelve years and shut the door.

Miss Agnes Fell had heard everything said in the morning-room that day. Being asked by nobody for her opinion of any of it, she had formed one, which she kept, as she kept a good many things, in a place where it could not be found. She was a person who, in a company of three, was apt to be taken for a piece of furniture, and in a company of twelve for two. She stood at the back of every room. She carried the shawl. She poured, and was sorry for the pot; and when she spoke, in her soft voice, with her mittened hands folded, the speaker nearest her would turn his head to see who had coughed. She had come to know to a nicety what nobody looked at, and she had a great deal of leisure in which to look at it.

She lit the night-candle from the stump on the washstand, and put it down on the table by the bed, where it would burn, as it always burned, until two. She took off the right mitten. The finger beneath it was stained blue-black to the second joint, and the ball of it, beside the nail, was hard with an old pale callus; she looked at it as one looks at an old acquaintance, without love and without surprise. From the drawer she took a penknife and a goose quill and a sheet of paper, and sat, and bent over the candle, and began to cut.

She had a way of sharpening a pen as though she were apologising to it.
