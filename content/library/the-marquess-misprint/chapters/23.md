# The Saturday Number

At two o'clock on the afternoon of Friday the twenty-seventh of September, three hours before it was due and thirteen before it was wanted, the copy arrived at Hand and Pen Court in a pile, and was laid on the imposing stone like the pieces of a puzzle that has been kept too long in its box.

Mrs Rudge had ordered it early. A newspaper that meant to be early in the streets, she had said, had best begin by being early in the shop. There was the news, in a clerk's fair hand, grudging as a bill. There was Mr Underhill's own notice, a paragraph of three lines on a correction which the *Gazette* "had been advised to make", which Winifred set aside with the interest of a naturalist for an unusual beetle. There was a column headed A. FELL, on the Weald and the Subscription, which every margin in the shop would have known for a razor's; the proprietor had sent for the writer in three words, *Send the woman*. There was the Farmer, in Minton's fair copy, with a covering line in the Marquess's own hand: *The commas are mine. Please do not correct them. J.S.* And there was the Erratum. Mrs Rudge had drafted it in pencil on a half-sheet, and her draft had stopped at *The error was the compositor's*. Winifred had added the rest in the margin, the name, and what the name had seen, and what it had done about it; and against the addition the proprietor had written, in the same pencil, a single word: *True.*

The rain had come back at noon, as a creditor comes back. Barnaby had hung a bucket from a nail in the beam, directly under the broken slate, and was explaining to it that this was the last night.

By six the shop was as full as it had been on the night of the Misprint, and a good deal fuller of the right people. Orme sat in his corner, deaf and perfect, composing the Farmer in Pica with the rhythm of a metronome that has been told a secret. Fairweather stood by the Albion. Mrs Hobday came and went with the tea, saying "In the night-watches" to the kettle, and Toby sat on an upturned pail in his paper hat with a fresh pie in his lap, which he was keeping, he said, for later, in the tone of a man laying down wine.

At five the Marquess had come in with his hat in his hand.

"My lord," Winifred had said.

"Miss Pargeter."

It was the whole conversation, and the shop, which had its ears out like a field of hares, found it adequate. She had put a stick in his hand. "Set your name," she said. "I will not set it for you." And Julian, in Barnaby's old apron, had gone to the case beside Orme's, and got the J into the stick in the first half-minute, the right way up, which is to say the wrong way round, as he had not managed on the thirteenth of June. Orme looked across, without turning his head.

"Clerkenwell?"

"Harrow."

"Hm." The stick ticked on. "Someone's been teaching you to hate a river."

* * *

The Erratum was set between eight and ten. Winifred set the body of it with her mother's brass stick, the thumb-rest bright and warm; Orme set the *Gazette*'s part; and at half past nine Toby, who had been given a line, stood at the upper case with a quad in his teeth and said to the type, so low that she had to lean to hear, "Miss. It were me."

"What was, Toby?"

"The page." His ears were scarlet. "I had a pie. Brevier come under, and I trod, and it went. I never said. I told her." He jerked his head at the cat, who was washing herself on the Albion with the air of a person who has heard it before.

"I know," said Winifred.

"You know?"

"I have known since the Thursday. You did not eat for two days, and you looked at the cat as though she had robbed you." She did not stop setting. "The cord was mine to look to. A thing told is worth three that are found out. Set your line."

He set it. It was the last, *and has never before had occasion to do so*, in nine words, and it took him eleven minutes and a good deal of nose; and when it was in the stick Winifred read it backwards and found it right, and said nothing, and he went on with his face wet and a more than ordinary dignity.

The company came in as the night went on. Verity arrived at ten with a hamper of porter and a ledger, to witness whatever Mr Lovejoy might bring, and sat down on a ream of paper with the ledger on her knee like a woman in a theatre who has paid for her seat. Rupert carried paper. He had been carrying barrels for fifteen days, and he came and went with a ream under each arm and the expression of a man to whom labour has been revealed by a miracle. At eleven two footmen of Ormsgill House, in claret and silver, with their calves in the rain, presented themselves in the court with a card on a salver: *My footmen are idle. Send them to the corners. O.* Mrs Hobday gave them tea in the scullery and a hymn. And at midnight Lady Constance Dyer arrived in a closed carriage with Blücher under her arm, and took the chair by the fire without a word, and sat in it like a monument to the principle of being present.

Agnes read the proofs. She sat at the proofing press with a pencil, her mittens off since ten, and her right hand blue to the second joint and quite plain to see. Lady Constance looked at it once, from the chair, and looked away. Agnes found a turned *n* in the Farmer and a comma in the second line of Mr Underhill, and she held up the proof of the Erratum to the lamp, and said, "Miss Pargeter. The W."

It was there, in the signature, in Great Primer, in W. PARGETER: the hairline gap in the left foot, as plain as a crack in a cup. She had meant to replace it since March. A new sort was fourpence.

"I can go to the foundry in the morning," said Agnes. "It would be a quarter of an hour."

"It is the same W," said Winifred. "It was in the Misprint. Let it stand."

* * *

At ten minutes past one, with the formes locked and lying on the stone for their last look, a slate went.

It went with a sound like a cough, and then there was no longer a Drip; there was a rope of water the thickness of a wrist, falling from the roof onto the stone and the formes upon it. Winifred was across the room before the second quart. She had the oilcloth from the proofing press in her arms, and Julian, who had been at her elbow, took the other end without being told, and they spread it over the type and lay on it, as one lies over a child in a crowd, with their backs to the roof and their arms out to the corners, while the water drummed on their shoulders. It ran down her neck. It ran down his. He said nothing. His cheek was an inch from hers, on the wet oilcloth, and he stayed there, and did not arrange anything.

Two sorts had gone with the first rush: a Great Primer *e* and a full stop, in the third column. She found the holes with her bodkin under the oilcloth by the light of Toby's lantern, and reset them by feel, one and then the other, as a person puts a stopper in a bottle in the dark. The water was in her ears. When she had done, she said "Quoins," and he knew where they were.

At half past one the two presses began.

* * *

The Albion's toggle collar cracked at two.

It cracked with a note like a plate. The bar went slack in Julian's hands, and the whole iron frame took on a look of a man who has heard bad news. Barnaby was on his knees in front of it before anyone else had moved, with his spectacles on his forehead and his gouty foot out behind him, feeling the collar with fingers that knew it better than his own. "Cracked through," he said. "It's the cold. My cousin Ebenezer's iron never liked a wet autumn. I want a collar of the right metal, and there's nothing in the shop of the right metal, and the Old Lady cannot do the run alone. At a hundred and fifty an hour—"

"It cannot be done," said Orme, from the corner, to the room.

Julian had already taken off his signet ring.

He did not do it as a gesture. He did it as a man takes off a glove to pick up something small. He turned it once on his finger, as he had turned it all his life when he was thinking, and drew it over the knuckle, and held it out to Barnaby, and said, "Will this do?" It was the Stanmere griffin, in eighteen-carat gold, that had been on the hand of six marquesses.

Barnaby weighed it in his palm. "My dear fellow, it's soft. It'll flatten. It ought to last two thousand pulls if you're kind to it." He fitted it into the gap with a hammer and a prayer. The griffin went into the iron like a coin into a slot; and the bar came taut again. "A very good ring," said Barnaby, "for a very bad purpose."

"Pull," said Winifred.

He pulled.

* * *

Mr Lovejoy came in at half past two, with an umbrella furled in his hand like a rolled proof, and Mr Gribble behind him in the brown coat.

"Madam. Mr Pargeter. I regret the hour." He did not look as though he regretted it. He looked as he had looked in June, small and clean, in the snuff-coloured coat, with the glass on its ribbon swinging against his waistcoat. "I have here a demand in form under the third clause. The sum of eleven hundred and forty pounds, with interest to this day, is payable on demand upon the loss of the *Gazette* contract, and I now demand it. I have not reckoned the interest. I thought it civil that we should do it together."

"The law, Miss Pargeter," said Mr Gribble, apologetically, to the oilcloth, "don't allow me to lay a finger on the press before sunrise. I'd not wish it thought I'd tried."

It was a very good stroke. It cost nothing and it took time. A demand must be received; the interest must be reckoned to the day; a receipt must be written; a signature, the signature of an old man with ink in the creases of his hands, must be fetched from the Albion, where it was inking. Every minute of it was a minute that the run did not run. The cart went at a quarter past four. At a hundred and fifty an hour, with the Old Lady, and the Albion held by a ring, it wanted every minute it had.

"I have reckoned it," said Winifred.

"I beg your pardon?"

"One hundred and eighty-seven days at six per cent on seven hundred and forty pounds. Twenty-two pounds fifteen shillings." She had turned and was speaking to the dark of the stair. "Rupert."

Lord Rupert Tremayne came down with the Black Bull strongbox on his shoulder, like a man carrying a lamb, and set it on the oilcloth that covered the stone. Winifred took the key from the ribbon at her neck. She counted the money out in piles of twenty guineas, aloud, and Mr Lovejoy, who counted aloud when he handled money, counted it back, a pile behind her; so that for eleven minutes the composing room resounded with two voices, one dry and one soft, going up the scale together like a pair of sparrows on a stair. Nobody in the room had ever heard so much silence made out of numbers.

"One thousand, one hundred and sixty-two pounds, fifteen shillings," said Winifred.

"I make it the same to a farthing, madam. You may keep the farthing."

Verity wrote the receipt in her square upright hand. It was brought to Barnaby, who signed it with a hand that left its own thumb-mark in the margin, and then held the pen in the air.

"Hector," he said, "my dear fellow. You had the best hands I ever had in the shop. I don't believe I ever said."

"No, sir." Mr Lovejoy's face did not move. The glass on its ribbon swung once. "You did not."

"It's a pity to say it at half past two in the morning, over a receipt."

"I find it comes out when it comes, Mr Pargeter," said Mr Lovejoy. He took the three cancelled notes and folded them, and put on his hat.

At the door he stopped. He stood there with his hand on the latch, looking back, with his head a little on one side, as a craftsman sizes a page; and the room, which had been about to breathe, did not. Then he came back across the floor to the line where the first sheets hung to dry, and lifted one with the tips of his fingers, and put up the glass.

"Madam. The word-spacing in your third column is uneven. At the ninth line there is a thick space where there should be a middle. No reader will notice it." He lowered the glass. "I cannot bear it."

Winifred looked at him. Then she stopped the Albion. She slackened the quoins at the corner, and opened line nine with a bodkin, and made it right, and locked it again; and the seven minutes it took were the most expensive in the history of the house, and nobody complained, and Fairweather said "Aye." When she looked up Mr Lovejoy was nodding, once, as a man nods at a sum that has come out. He bowed to Barnaby, and to Mrs Hobday, and to the pug. He went out into the court. The rain shut behind him.

* * *

It is time to say what was on the sheet that he had held, since it was the object of all this and has not yet been seen. It had been headed, in Great Primer capitals, centred, with a rule beneath it, and it began at the top of the second page, where the Fashionable Intelligence usually was.

> ERRATUM. In our number of Thursday the 6th of June, in the paragraph headed Marriage in High Life, for 'the engagement between the Marquess of Stanmere and Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row, is entirely well founded, and that his Lordship is at present engaged to the lady', read 'the report of an engagement between the Marquess of Stanmere and Miss Quarles, daughter of Sir Ephraim Quarles, Bart., is entirely without foundation, and that his Lordship is at present engaged to no lady whatever.' The error was the compositor's. The compositor is W. Pargeter, who saw it before the sheets left her house and did not stop them. The Gazette begs the pardon of the Marquess of Stanmere, of Miss Quarles, and of its readers, and has never before had occasion to do so.

The *Gazette* of that Saturday was the only newspaper in London in which the Marquess of Stanmere was, at the hour of printing, correctly engaged to no lady whatever. By half past six that would have changed.

* * *

The last sheet came off at ten past four. The cart was in the arch. Sowerby was on the box in his oilskin, with Jehu's ears laid back and his face like an old saddle left out in the rain; and Winifred, who had a clean apron and nothing else to change, climbed up beside him with the first copy folded on her knees.

He did not look at her. He was red to the ears.

"Miss. I only told the clerk what I seen. I wish to God I'd kept my tongue and my quart."

"You said what was true," said Winifred. "That is why it has been so difficult."

He clicked to the horse. The cart went out under the arch, past the pump, into Paternoster Row, and this time she went with it.

* * *

The sun was a pale disc over the dome when she came back on foot from Pomfret Yard. It was not copper. It was merely there, the colour of a sixpence that has been in a pocket, and it lit the wet street without any warmth. The *Gazette* had been in the streets since half past five. At the corner of Ludgate Hill stood a footman of Ormsgill House in claret and silver, with his calves in the rain, crying "Erratum! The *Gazette*! Erratum!" in the voice in which he was accustomed to announce Her Grace's carriage, and a crossing-sweeper had got a copy and was reading it aloud to a hackney-man, with his finger under the words. "She says she saw it and let it go." "Well," said the hackney-man. "She says it." At six the *Crier*'s hawkers began to come into the Churchyard with their armfuls, and found the street full of people reading something else.

Hand and Pen Court was quiet. The lamps had burned down. Lady Constance slept upright in the chair by the fire with the pug on her knees and her trumpet on the floor; Mrs Hobday slept with the hymn-book on her chest; Brevier slept on the Albion, under the bar. In the kitchen, over the dregs of the tea, with the door shut and Mrs Hobday's ear missing nothing from the scullery, the Marquess of Stanmere had already asked Barnaby Pargeter the question which that gentleman had been waiting to be asked since a bowl of broth in a tithe barn on the fifteenth of August; and had got his answer, which had been *Ask her properly, my dear fellow, she's had a great deal of the other thing*.

She found him on the stairs that led up to the parlour. He sat with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, ink to the shoulder, a night's grey on his face, with a bent ring in his hand. It had come out of the collar as an oval, with the griffin flattened to a smear. It would not go back on the finger it had come from.

"You have asked my father."

"I asked his leave. He gave it." He stood. "It is the twenty-eighth. Michaelmas is tomorrow. I counted fifty-five days in a hop garden, and there is one left, and I find I cannot do the last."

"You are a day early."

"In a man who has been three months late it seemed excusable. You said I was to ask you when you did not need me. You paid Mr Lovejoy out of your own box at half past two, and did not look round once to see whether I was there. I took that to be what you meant." He held the ring. "I signed Article the Third before I loved you. I broke it afterwards. That is the whole of the defence, and I do not offer it as one."

She stood quite still, with the Gazette's grey light behind her.

"Miss Pargeter, will you marry me? By banns, at your church and at mine, on three Sundays, in the open, with your father to give you away, and nobody paying for anything but the people in it. I have drawn up no Articles. I could not think of a seventh that you would let me have."

"It is the first time," said Winifred, "that anyone has asked me, instead of arranging. I wished to have noticed it."

"And?" said Julian. "I should like it noted that I do not at present know."

"Yes," said Winifred.
