# The Weald Relief Subscription

At six o'clock on the morning of Wednesday the thirty-first of July the great tithe barn at Stanmere Court held sixty persons in wet clothes, steaming gently, and a Marquess standing on a hay-cart reading aloud from a table of figures.

It was a barn of the kind built by men who expect to be paid in kind: oak and stone, a hundred and twenty feet long, roofed like the nave of a church and a good deal better kept, with the grey light coming in at the gable and lying along the threshing floor in a cold bar. The farmers sat in front on bales, their hats upon their knees. Behind them stood the labourers, and behind the labourers the women; and at the back, on a bale that had been covered with a shawl in her honour, sat Lady Constance Dyer with her trumpet raised, Miss Fell beside her with the umbrella, and Winifred Pargeter at the end of the row, with her hands in her lap and her left thumb in plain view, since nobody in Kent had yet thought to look at it.

Julian read the table in his dry flat voice, which was not an orator's, and had never in thirty-one years been raised, and which carried to the back of the barn for that reason. He had written a speech at five that morning and burned it at a quarter past, because it contained a metaphor.

"Wheat, at Maidstone on Saturday, seventy-eight shillings the quarter. The quartern loaf, thirteen pence." He turned the paper. "The wage of a labourer in the three parishes, nine shillings the week. A family of five eats seven quartern loaves in a week, which is seven shillings and sevenpence. That leaves one shilling and fivepence for rent, fuel, shoes and a shirt." He looked up. "I have done the sum six times. It does not come out."

Somebody in the third row laughed, not unkindly, as a man laughs at a joke that is not one, and the laugh went round the barn and died. Of the eleven farms, Julian went on, nine had no hops worth the picking; the bine had mouldered on the pole in July; and the duty on a crop that did not exist was a matter he proposed to write to the Chancellor about, and expect no answer. At his elbow Mr Gideon Wragg, in a grey coat the colour of his hair, supplied in a voice like a gate the figures for the barns. "Eleven farms, my lord. Not a third full."

Julian was aware, without turning his head, of a small square person at the end of the back row who had read every one of these figures nine times in a clerk's fair hand and queried the commas. It was an odd sensation, to be heard by the single person present who knew whose they were. He found that he was reading rather more slowly.

"What's to be done, my lord?"

It was a man in the third row, with his hat upon his knee and hands like roots: Amos Pankhurst, of Hope Farm, who had asked Julian's father the same question in the year 'ninety-eight about a different failure, and been told. Julian had been asked it in one form or another all his life, and his habit was to answer it by arranging something before the question was finished.

"I do not know yet," said the Marquess of Stanmere.

It is a thing a man may say once or twice in a lifetime, and he said it to sixty people and a dairymaid. "I can tell you what I shall not do. I shall not tell you it will be well. I wished you to have the figures from me, and not from the overseer, who has them wrong. You shall have the rest when I have it."

Nobody cheered. This was Kent. Pankhurst nodded slowly, twice, as at a price that was fair if disagreeable, and put on his hat; and at the back Winifred, who had expected an orator and been given a man reading out his own account, found that she was looking very hard at the toe of her shoe.

* * *

It was Lady Constance who found the use for him. She did it over cold ham, rapping on the table with her trumpet like an auctioneer.

"A subscription."

"Aunt?"

"It is a question of arithmetic. Kent has twelve hundred landowners and no reason to give. It will give if it is asked in print, with a table, by a man with a title. Every reader of the *Gazette* in the county, and every name in the Directory." She turned the lorgnette upon the far end of the table, where Winifred was dividing a roll with the economy of one who knew to a farthing what it cost. "You have a press."

"We have a press," said Julian, and looked at Winifred.

"We have a common press, my lord, one case of Great Primer and no damp paper," said Winifred. "I can give you five hundred broadsides in a week and a thousand in ten days, if the Medway permits."

"The Medway," said Mr Wragg from the doorway, where he had come with a ledger and the face of one who regards breakfast as a luxury of the idle, "will do as it pleases."

She designed it that afternoon at the steward's high desk, with a pencil, Julian's table at her left hand and her father in a chair at her right with his foot on a bucket, offering views. A broadside is a small thing and the hardest of all to set, since it has to be read by someone walking past; it must say everything in the time it takes to cross a yard. She cut the appeal to four sentences and the Farmer's adjectives to none, and set the heading in Great Primer, and ruled the table with brass, and on the Friday evening, with the first proof damp upon the stone, she showed it to the Marquess.

> THE WEALD OF KENT. A SUBSCRIPTION for the Relief of the Labouring Poor of Yalding, Nettlestead and Hunton, where the hops have failed and the wheat is dear.

> The quartern loaf, thirteen pence. A labourer's wage, nine shillings the week. Seven loaves for a family of five, seven shillings and sevenpence. Remaining for rent, fuel and clothing, one shilling and fivepence.

> "The price of bread is the one arithmetic from which the poor are never excused." A KENTISH FARMER.

> Subscriptions are received by Mr Wragg, Steward, Stanmere Court, Yalding, and by the Overseers of the three parishes. Printed at Stanmere Court by B. Pargeter, Hand and Pen Court, Paternoster Row.

He read it standing at the high desk with the proof flat beneath his long hands. "The last line but one," he said. "I did not give you that."

"Letter the Fifth. Second paragraph. I queried *excused*; I thought *spared* was cleaner. You said no."

"I was right."

"You were. It is a better sentence than I could write, and it ought to be on every barn door in Kent." She wiped her hands on her apron. Her father slept in his chair with his spectacles on his forehead, and Toby was at the pump, and she spoke low. "It ought to have your name under it."

"Quite."

"That is your word for no."

"It is." He took off the pencil from behind his ear, which he had not known was there. "A peer on corn is a landlord pleading for his rents. A farmer on corn is an honest man in a bad hat. The Farmer has been listened to for a year because he is nobody."

"He has been listened to because he is right."

"He has been listened to because he is nobody who is right. It is an uncommon combination, and I should not like to spoil it." But he was smiling, a little, at the proof. "You told me once you wished to hear him say he was wrong."

"I wished to hear him say it in his own name. Mr Underhill would print a Marquess before he would print a kitchen. The Weald would pay for it."

"The Weald will pay for the table."

"My lord, you are the most obstinate person I ever set type for."

"It is a family trait," said Julian, and was sorry at once, for the last time he had used the phrase it had been about something else; and she did not smile, and he put down the pencil. "No. Not yet. I will think of it." It was not yes. She did not tell. She had promised, and she had a habit of keeping what she had promised, and it was a better habit than most.

* * *

The notice on the tithe-barn door had been wrong for eleven years. She had seen it the first morning, and had not said so, which was a thing she was learning to do. It was a printed sheet in Great Primer, nailed up in the year 'five by a Maidstone printer, and it read: TITHES AND RENTS DUE AT THIS BARN AT LADY DAY AND MICHAELMAS. NO ABATEMENT. BY ORDER, G. RAGG, STEWARD. The W of Wragg was gone. For eleven years the steward of Stanmere Court had gone about his master's farms as Mr Ragg, and not a man in Kent had had the courage to tell him, or, which was more likely, the wish.

At Hertford Street, three weeks earlier, she had learned to let an error stand. It was among the most useful things she had ever learned, and she had been rather proud of it. But the error at Hertford Street had been a friend's, and had cost no one anything; this one was costing a man his name, in public, every Lady Day. There is a difference, which a compositor knows in the thumb, between an error that is a friend's and an error that is a wound; and on the Thursday morning she set the notice afresh in the corner of the forme, with a W at the head of Wragg as clean as a new pin, and nailed it up herself, over the old one, from a ladder, in the rain, while the steward watched from the barn with his arms folded.

He read it for some time. He read it as a man reads his own epitaph, to see whether they have got it right.

"That is my name," said Mr Wragg.

"Yes, Mr Wragg."

"It's been Ragg these eleven years. I never—" He stopped. "A man doesn't like to ask."

"No," said Winifred, with feeling.

"Ah," said Mr Wragg, and went away; and sulked, in the manner of a person who has been done a kindness without his leave, until dusk, when he met her crossing the yard and touched his hat to her without a word. In the morning it was Miss Pargeter.

The post came that afternoon, by a boy on a pony, with a letter in a hand like type: each letter standing by itself, and none touching. It was from Mr Orme.

> Miss,—Mr Trigg's young man called Monday with a paper. Mr Trigg has made over his account against this house, being four hundred pounds for paper, to Mr H. Lovejoy, who will take it at Michaelmas with the rest. The paper is stamped. Mr Lovejoy's boy waited for a receipt and was given a glass of water. Sparrow is punctual and clean, which I do not like. The *Gazette* went to the cart at a quarter before five on Tuesday with a Bishop on the second page. The Drip is steady. I have read your last letter backwards as well as forwards and find no difference. Your obedient, A. Orme.

She read it twice, which for her was a form of weeping. "Seven hundred and forty," said Winifred aloud. "Four hundred. One thousand one hundred and forty pounds."

"Trigg has been very kind to us these eleven years, Win," said her father, brightly.

"He has now been very kind to Mr Lovejoy."

"A paper merchant, my dear girl, is a man who sells what is shortly to be worth less. He is accustomed to disappointment." He took his spectacles from his forehead, found them in his hand, and put them upon his nose, from which he regarded her face. "Don't tell Stanmere."

"Article the Third."

"Quite right. Nobody ought to be the poorer for being fond of us."

* * *

On the Saturday, Mrs Pickett having at last dried enough wood in the bakehouse oven to light a fire in the dining-room, the whole party dined for the first time in a warm room, which made them garrulous. Dr Pym had held the table since the soup. He had reached the electrical fluid at the fish and the dome of St Paul's at the saddle of mutton, and he was explaining, with a pear in his hand for the cathedral, that the rods drew off the discharge of the clouds and left the rain with nowhere to go but the Weald, when Miss Quarles took off her spectacles, which hung on a chain, and put the question that had been forming behind them for some days.

"How many rods were there on St Paul's in 1810, Dr Pym? And how much did it rain?"

"One, madam. And the rain was—" The Doctor paused. "I believe the usual."

"One rod and the usual rain. And in this year one rod, and the unusual." She put the spectacles on again and looked at him through them with her flat kind eye. "I should say the rod was a constant."

"It is the accumulation of the effect, madam."

"Ah," said Verity.

"Plain terms, Doctor," said Sir Ephraim from the other end of the table. "She thinks you've chosen the wrong sum."

"I think he is an honest man," said Verity, "and has taken a great deal of trouble over it." Rupert, who throughout the Doctor's discourse had looked at her as at an interesting piece of pastry, asked her afterwards to dance in the hall, where the carpets were up on account of the damp. She said that she did not. He said that neither did he; he merely walked about in time. They went off to do so, with Miss Fell at the pianoforte, who played what she was asked and said in the whole of the evening eleven words.

At midnight, in the old brewhouse, Julian stood on one side of the stone and discovered that he had not known a hand-press could be so quiet.

He was there as a customer, which is to say on sufferance. Barnaby sat on a stool with his foot on a bucket; Toby, in a paper hat, stood by the ink slab with the leather balls; Verity, who had come to see a thing done properly, sat upon the copper's edge with her hands on her knees. The forme had been locked at half past eleven, and Winifred did not leave a locked forme overnight. She stood at the Old Lady in her apron, her sleeves above the elbow, and she did not look at him; and that, he was beginning to learn, was her way of looking.

Earlier in the evening, while Toby ran for the ink and Barnaby was admiring the lock-up, she had gone to the table at the wall, where her mother's brass stick-case lay open, and taken out of its lid the folded foolscap that lay there, and carried it to the lantern. He had seen her at it from the corner of his eye: a short square woman looking, under a light, at the foot of the sixth paragraph, where a thumb had left a smudge of ink. She had looked at it for about the time a person takes to read a clause. Then Barnaby had cried, "Win! The quoin!" and she had laid the sheet down by the case and gone back to the stone. He did not know what it was she had looked at. He found that he could very easily guess.

"Ink," said Winifred. Toby beat the forme, left and right, with a sound like a man patting a horse. The damp sheet went down on the tympan; the tympan folded; the bed ran in. She took the bar in both hands.

The Old Lady is not the Albion. The Albion sighs; the Old Lady groans. The screw goes down, and the great oak platen comes to rest upon the sheet, and the whole frame takes the strain with a creak like a ship at anchor; and there is a pause, such as there is at the top of a swing; and then the bar is released and the thing lets go its breath, slowly, and the bed runs out. It is a sound with a rhythm in it, but the rhythm is slow, slower than a man's. It is the sound of a very large and very patient heart. She pulled once, and ran the bed out, and lifted the tympan, and drew the sheet off the type, and held it to the lantern; and there was a hush, such as there is in a church when the last hymn is done and the sexton has not yet begun to cough.

"Plain," said Verity, from the copper. "And correct."

"One comma," said Julian.

"Where?"

He looked. He read it through twice, the loaf, the wage, the seven shillings and sevenpence, the line from the Fifth Letter. "I appear to have been mistaken," he said.

"Then I must look again," said Winifred, and did not look at him.

Barnaby was taken off to bed on the arm of a John, with the air of a man who has seen a launch. Verity went after them with her hands in her pockets, having said "Plain terms, good night." Toby lay down on the settle among the sacks, and Brevier climbed upon his feet, and he was asleep before the cat had settled. And then there were two people either side of a stone, in a stone room, with a damp proof lying between them like a treaty, and about three feet of oak; and they did not touch. It was not, the narrator is bound to say, that either of them did not wish to. It was that neither of them could think how it was done.

"It makes a sound like a heart," said Julian.

"It is the only sound in the world I would trust to count by." She took up her lantern. "Good night, my lord."

"Good night."

He took his own, and went out into the rain. Winifred, with her mind on other things, left the brewhouse a little after, and pulled the door to behind her on the settle and the sleeper, and the lamp on the high desk burning low.

The door opened four inches, and a candle came in at it, in a hand wearing a mitten. Miss Agnes Fell, in her grey wrapper and her shawl, who could not sleep and had a habit of walking the house with a night-candle till two, stood on the threshold and did what she did with every room she entered, which was to count it. Toby, on the settle, did not stir. She was very good at counting, and the narrator will leave it at that. She counted the proofs upon the stone, which were nine; she counted the lines on the top one; and then, for no reason she could have given, she counted the lines on the paper that lay beside it, under the lantern, out of its case.
