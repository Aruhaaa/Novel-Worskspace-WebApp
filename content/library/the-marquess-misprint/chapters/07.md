# A Rout in the Rain

"Say it again," said Barnaby, "as though I were a duchess."

"It is in the *Gazette*."

"Too much *Gazette*. You sound as if you were selling it. A duchess will want to be told, my dear, not offered a subscription. Lower. Slower. Let the word come at the end like the imprint, where it belongs and where nobody looks."

They were in a hackney on Piccadilly, in a line of carriages that had not moved in eleven minutes, and the rain was coming down upon the roof with the steady insistence of a creditor. Barnaby sat wedged into the corner with his gouty foot, in its flannel boot the size of a loaf, laid across the opposite seat, and his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, wearing the coat in which he had been married in 1788 and which had since been let out twice. He had refused to be left at home. He had also refused, with the dignity of a man who has been quoted the price of dress shoes, to go in; he was to see her as far as the carriage step, as a bishop sees a bride as far as the altar rail, and then be taken back to the Row.

Winifred sat very upright, because the silk allowed nothing else. It was blue-grey, four and sixpence the yard from a man in Cheapside, and in the three days since it had passed into the hands of Lady Constance's dressmaker it had been cut, turned, taken in, let out and given what that lady called a line, which Winifred, who set lines for her bread, privately thought she had always possessed. At her throat was the seed-pearl brooch that had been her mother's. Her hair was dressed higher than it had ever been dressed, and through the knot of it, because she had declined with a politeness that made the ear-trumpet tremble to be pinned with anything else, was the brass composing rule. "A pin," Lady Constance had said at last, "is a pin," and had let it pass. On her right hand she wore a white kid glove that fitted. On her left she wore another, two sizes too small, as a matter of policy: a glove that fits shows a thumb, and a glove that does not fit shows only a glove.

"It is in the *Gazette*," said Winifred, lower and slower.

"Better. A little more disdain."

"I have no disdain. I have a blister."

"Then use the blister." Barnaby considered her in the lamplight with his head on one side. "You are uncommonly like your mother, Win. I should mention that it makes me nervous. Her father asked me what I earned a week before he asked my name, and I said forty-two shillings and a good heart, and he said he could not eat the second."

Somewhere beyond the roofs to the west a gun went off with a flat wet cough. Then, after an interval, another.

"What in the world—"

"The Park, Father. They are firing the salute. It is the anniversary."

"It sounds like a sack of coals going down a stair."

"The powder has been rained on since Sunday." She counted the third. "Nine seconds apart. Fairweather pulls faster than that, and quieter."

The hackney moved four lengths and stopped again. Ormsgill House stood back from the road behind a wall and a double row of flambeaux that the rain had reduced to sullen orange smears, and footmen in the Ormsgill claret were conveying ladies from carriage to awning under umbrellas the size of tents. The awning was striped, and leaked in stripes. When the hackney at last drew up at the step, the footman who opened the door took in at a glance the hackney, the flannel boot, the old man in the married coat and the lady in blue-grey, and produced the blank face of a man who had once served a Prince and did not intend to be surprised by anything again.

Barnaby leaned across the straw. "Go and be looked at, Win," he said. "It is what a title-page is for." And he caught her left hand, the small-gloved one, and held it a moment, and said to the thumb in a different voice, "Your mother would have given a month's wages for a ticket." Then he let go, and sat back, and said, "Mind the roof," and the footman, who had heard him, looked at the sky.

* * *

There were forty-one steps in the great staircase of Ormsgill House, white marble with a gilt rail, and she counted them, as one does, to have something to do with her face. Lady Constance had met her in the hall, in black bombazine and the purple ribbon, with Miss Fell a pace behind carrying the shawl, and had said, "You are four minutes late. I allowed three. It is a question of arithmetic: four minutes is the interval at which a person is noticed arriving and has not yet been missed." She had then taken the stair as a dray takes a cask, slowly, braced, and without conversation.

Winifred had read a great many people in her time, from the far side of a case. She had never before been proofed. It was done on the stairs, by eye alone, between the first landing and the second: a hundred faces leaned over the rail in postures of the most careless interest, and the whisper ran down ahead of her like a wet finger down a column.

"That is the printer."

"She is a great deal smaller than the print."

"I understood she carried a lance."

"Is that a pin in her hair?"

"It is a rule, I believe. A printer's rule. For measuring."

"How very exact."

"She has her own hair. One can tell."

She had her own hair, and a rule through it, and a thumb in a glove that did not fit. She kept her eyes on a point at the head of the stairs, as she had been taught, and found that the point was a tall man in black, slightly stooped, who had been standing there for twenty minutes, which for a man who loathed routs was a form of courage and for a man who loathed waiting was a form of lunacy.

"Good evening," said Julian. He looked at her for rather longer than the words required. There was a notch in his left eyebrow, white against the sandy brow, and it lifted. "You look as though you were about to be set in type."

"Imposed, I think the word is."

"I had the word *justified* ready and it seemed too hopeful."

"Locked up in a chase," said Winifred, "and wedged with a quantity of furniture."

"And planed down."

"Planed, certainly. It is how one gets the level." She felt it begin in the ribs, where the stays were, and could do nothing about it; and he, with his hand on the gilt rail and the faintest of lines at the corner of that grave mouth, appeared to be doing nothing about it either. They laughed. It was the first time they had done so together, and rather more of the head of the stairs turned round than either of them had counted upon.

"Come," said Julian, composing his face. "I am to present you to everybody. It will take a quarter of an hour, and you will not remember a name. It does not matter. They will remember yours."

* * *

It will be known to the reader that there is no custom in English society so firm as the question, and none so easily answered by a person of nerve, who need only decline the question and reply to something else. Winifred did not know it. She had a formula, and a formula is a form of nerve.

The first to ask was a gentleman with a stick and a face like a disappointed ham, who put himself in front of her at the foot of the saloon and demanded without preamble whether it was true that she was to be Marchioness of Stanmere. "It is in the *Gazette*," said Winifred. The gentleman stared at her, and she at him, and his colour altered by degrees from ham to something nearer salmon. "Ha," he said. "Very proper. Very proper indeed." And he went away to tell a friend that she was the soul of discretion.

The second was a Mrs Chalmers, a very broad lady in puce, who wore on her bosom, pinned like a brooch, the Waterloo medal of a Colonel Chalmers who had been in the battle for forty minutes and had not recovered, and who asked after the settlements. "It is in the *Gazette*," said Winifred, and Mrs Chalmers said she was sure it was, and went on to tell her about a cook in Cheltenham for nine minutes without drawing breath.

The third was a pale girl of seventeen in white muslin, who said, with her hands clasped and her eyes enormous, "Oh, is it *true*?" and received her answer, and sighed, and said, "How romantic." It was the first time in Winifred's life that anyone had called her romantic, and it was for declining to say anything at all. She did not know whether to be flattered or to be afraid.

She heard Julian, ten feet off, asked by a bald earl whether the thing were settled. "I am assured that it is in the *Gazette*," he said, in the voice of a man reading from a bill of lading, and the earl looked at him sharply, and then at her, and then, in a way that she found very instructive, almost with envy. By ten o'clock the saloon was saying that the Marquess and Miss Pargeter had a delicate reticence. By eleven it was a famous one.

"Ah," said somebody at her elbow, in a voice like warm gravy. "So this is the young person."

It was a lady of fifty, in raspberry silk, stout and rustling and tightly laced, with a fan she was using as a weapon and a pair of eyes that went over Winifred as a shopman's go over a counterfeit shilling: front, back, edge. Lady Quarles. The lady swayed. She laid the back of her hand against her forehead, in the manner of a woman about to expire upon a sofa, and looked about for the sofa; but she had fainted at four routs already that Season and the footmen had become expert at not being near. After a moment she recovered with a great sigh, and went away, trailing raspberry, in the direction of the supper-room.

"That," said a voice from behind the nearest pillar, "was Mama. She does it at the sight of any person who has not been introduced at the proper moment. You may come round here. It is quite safe. I have been standing here for forty minutes and nobody has noticed."

Winifred went round the pillar. She found a tall young woman with a great many freckles, large capable hands and a brown dress that had plainly been to a great many routs before this one, and had a pair of spectacles on a chain round her neck, which she put on to look at Winifred and took off again.

"I'm the previous engagement," said Miss Quarles. "Verity Quarles. I only wished to say thank you, because Mama has been trying to sell me to your Marquess since March and I should have had to refuse him, which is awkward when it is a Marquess, and I should have been shut in my room. And to say that if you are ever in want of a dull and honest friend, I am available. I keep books."

"What sort of books?"

"Brewery." She said it as another girl might have said *Italian*. "Papa's. It is a perfectly good brewery and it wants a better man at the top, and I am not allowed to be one. But I can add up, and I can tell you that your Marquess's waistcoat cost eleven guineas, and that everybody in this room is in debt but the two of us. It is very restful, standing behind a pillar with someone who is not."

"I am in debt," said Winifred, before she could think better of it.

"Are you? How much?"

"Seven hundred and forty pounds."

"Heavens," said Verity Quarles, with real respect, and took her hand and shook it. "Then you are better company than I thought. I shall call."

* * *

Lord Rupert Tremayne came across the floor at a pace that suggested he had been heading elsewhere and had changed his mind at the sight of her. He was a lanky young man with russet-gold curls, a long amused mouth and a waistcoat the colour of a peach in a good year, and he carried a quizzing glass on a ribbon, which he did not need. He raised it. He looked at her through it. He lowered it.

"Miss Pargeter. Rupert Tremayne. Stanmere's oldest friend, which is to say the man who has lent him least. I shall not ask you whether you are engaged to him, because I have just heard you answer that three times, and I think the answer excellent."

"It is in the—"

"*Gazette*. Yes. Allow me, we might say it together. No? Then I shall tell you instead." He bent a little, and spoke in a light quick murmur that carried exactly as far as her ear. "You are not engaged to him. He is not engaged to you. Somebody in Fleet Street has put a W where a Q ought to have been, and the pair of you have found it more amusing than a retraction. I should have done the same, with less to gain."

Winifred looked at him. She had a great number of faces for such an occasion, none of which she had been taught, and she found that the most useful was the one she wore when a customer disputed a bill. "It is in the *Gazette*," she said.

"Quite so." He bowed to her, beautifully, from the waist. "My dear girl, I am enchanted. I have never been refused by anyone I liked, and I shall be very interested to find out how it is done. I intend to court you, you know. Honestly, as a rival, with all my cards on the table, so that when I become tiresome you may say you were warned."

"Then you will leave your hat on the table," said Winifred. "In the Row, a man who keeps his hat in his hand is a man who means to go."

Rupert Tremayne's mouth went up at one corner. "They took it at the door."

"Then you have saved yourself an argument."

"I think," said Lord Rupert slowly, turning the quizzing glass over in his fingers, "that this will be the most diverting summer of my life." And he went away, across the floor, tossing a guinea as he went, as though he were scattering it for the poor.

* * *

The Dowager Duchess of Ormsgill was enthroned at the farther end of the saloon on a sofa the size of a small boat, upon cushions arranged to raise her to a height consistent with her opinion of herself. She was seventy-nine. She was perhaps five feet tall, in a purple turban with a brooch the size of a pigeon's egg, and she wore rings on every finger including both thumbs, and her eyes, which were black and very small, came to rest upon Winifred as two pins come to rest on a map.

"Come here. No, there, where I can see you. Sit. Constance, go away."

Lady Constance did not go away. She stood a pace off with her trumpet at the port, like a ship refusing to be dismissed from a harbour. Miss Quarles had drifted to the arm of the sofa, and Lord Rupert was leaning against its other end in an attitude of the deepest unconcern; and at the back, a pace behind and a little to the left, in her grey gown and her mittens, holding Lady Constance's shawl as she held it everywhere, and as little regarded as the shawl, was Miss Fell.

"You set type," said the Duchess.

"Yes, Your Grace."

"Do you read it backwards?"

"Forwards, backwards and upside down, at about the same rate."

"A most disagreeable talent. Ormsgill had one like it; he could read a letter across a table, and he never forgave me for it." She unhooked a gold pencil from her girdle, took a card from her reticule, and wrote upon the back of it, shielding it with a ringed hand. Then she laid it on her knee, written side up, and slid it with one finger across the satin until it lay precisely before Winifred, who sat at the end of the sofa, across the width of a pillow, and had it upside down. "Read that."

Winifred read it. It took no time at all. A compositor does not read, as the narrator has said; she sees letters, as a baker sees flour, and she saw these with a clearness that was nearly rude.

"Aloud," said the Duchess.

"Mrs Chalmers," said Winifred, quietly, "is a bore."

It went no further than the arm of the sofa. The sofa heard it: Lady Constance, whose trumpet flinched; Miss Quarles, who coughed; Lord Rupert, who made a noise like a man swallowing a hiccup; and the Duchess herself, who gave a laugh like a drawer of cutlery being dropped, and said, at the volume at which she said everything, as Mrs Chalmers went by with the Colonel's medal on her bosom: "She has been a bore since Trafalgar, and tonight she is a bore with a medal."

Mrs Chalmers went on past, unhearing, in her puce, towards the supper-room.

"Are you to marry that long young man?" said the Duchess.

"It is in the *Gazette*, Your Grace."

"I have forgotten more engagements than you have attended. That is a very good answer. Do it again at supper." The pins moved to Winifred's hair, and rested on the rule. "Ormsgill would have hated her, so I like her, Constance. She will do. You will give a dinner. I wish to see her eat."

"Ma'am," said Lady Constance.

"Thursday fortnight. The fourth. I shall be dead by the eleventh."

"It is the fourth of July, ma'am, and—"

"Then it is the fourth. I shall come at half past seven, and you may look at me as you like." She waved the hand that had the thumb-ring. "Go and be looked at, child. Take Quarles with you, she is wasted on me."

* * *

It was about this time that the roof of Ormsgill House, which had stood since 1752 and had been described by its architect as proof against anything but the Day of Judgement, declared an interest in the proceedings. The ballroom ceiling was painted with the gods of Olympus, among clouds, and from the largest of the clouds, at intervals of just under four seconds, there began to fall a single clear drop of water upon the parquet. A footman, with the air of a man who had performed the office before, came forward with a silver basin from the Ormsgill plate and set it down.

*Plink.*

Winifred stopped where she stood, in the middle of the floor, with Verity Quarles at one elbow and the Duchess's blessing on her head. She counted. Three seconds and a bit. The Drip, in a gilded ballroom, being served with a silver basin by a man in claret.

"Four seconds," she said, to nobody.

She began to laugh. She tried to stop, and a thing like that, once begun, is a printing-house matter: it spread from the ribs to the throat and out of the mouth in a series of small helpless breaths, until Miss Quarles was looking at her with a deep, delighted, perplexed expression, and the nearest dozen guests had paused with their glasses to see what it was that the printer had found so droll. It was not a ladylike laugh. It was a laugh that had been waiting twelve days for a place to be let out, and it carried.

Across the width of the room, past two hundred shoulders and a quantity of ostrich plumes, Julian heard it. He did not look round at once. He was talking to the bald earl, about the Corn Returns, and had been for some minutes, and he went on doing so with every appearance of attention. But the earl, who was not a perceptive man, remarked afterwards that the Marquess of Stanmere had been turning his signet ring on his finger the whole time, round and round, like a man winding a very small clock; and that he did not appear to know that he was doing it.
