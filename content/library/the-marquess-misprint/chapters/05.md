# A Marquess in the Composing Room

"How do you do," said the Marquess of Stanmere to the roof of a hackney coach. "How do you do. How do you *do*."

"That was the nineteenth, my lord," said Mr Minton, from the opposite seat.

"Has it improved?"

Minton looked at his watch, as though the answer might be kept in it. "It has become a different question each time. At the sixth it was a physician's. At the twelfth, if I may say so, it was a magistrate's. The last was the question of a man asking to be shown the door."

"That was the one I was aiming at."

They were toiling up Ludgate Hill in the rain, behind two horses of the sort that hackney proprietors buy at Smithfield for their opinions, and on the seat between the gentlemen, wrapped in a cloth and a spare sheet of the Corn Returns, lay a York ham of fourteen pounds. It was there because of a conclusion the Marquess had reached at one that morning, by candle, with the Articles of Agreement spread before him. Article the Third forbade him to lend, give, advance or promise money. It said money. It said nothing of swine. He had read it six times, as an honest man reads a contract in which he suspects himself of being outwitted, and had decided that a gentleman forbidden to be useful to a household might at least be permitted to feed it.

"Minton. Is a ham money?"

"It has a market price, my lord. Elevenpence the pound, at Leadenhall."

"Money is what a price is paid in. A ham is what one gets for it. I have been over the ground."

"Yes, my lord." Minton looked at his watch again. "I only observe that Miss Pargeter set the Article."

"I am aware who set the Article."

This was his second call under the Second. The first, on Tuesday, had been paid in the manner of calls upon the dying: eleven minutes, a chair, the Drip, and Mr Pargeter's foot discussed with great tenderness by everyone but its owner. "You will excuse me, my lord, I have a bishop," Miss Pargeter had said, and gone on setting type, and he had stood in her composing room like a man who has brought the wrong gift to a christening. This time he proposed to be useful, in a manner not forbidden, and to begin by being received, which he understood to be the harder part. Minton's inquiries had meanwhile established that the house was founded in 1781, kept two presses and a Methodist cook who sang, and had a roof that leaked. Of its debts Minton had learned nothing at all, which in his experience was where information usually stopped, and no sign that there were none.

The hackney would not enter the court. It put them down at the mouth of Paternoster Row, in a gutter that carried the opinions of every roof in the City, and went away with the air of a conveyance making a point. Over the arch hung the trade's sign, a painted hand holding a painted pen, and the Marquess, who could not pass a signboard without examining its credentials, counted the fingers twice and was obliged to concede that the painter had gone on rather than stop. He said "How do you do" to the pump, once, for the sake of the practice.

The street door was opened before he touched it by a wide woman of sixty with a ladle, who looked past him, and past Minton, and at the ham. She looked at it as one looks at an idol carried in on a litter: with a deep and wary courtesy, and an unalterable intention of using it.

"How do you do," said the Marquess. It was the twentieth, and the worst.

"'Cast thy bread upon the waters,'" said Mrs Hobday, weighing the ham in both arms. "It does not say it shall come back pork. But I never knew the Lord to be particular." She received it as the pagan receives a sacrifice, gravely and without thanks, as a thing owing to the god for some time, and bore it down the cellar stair, whence presently arose *Praise God from whom all blessings flow* in the cheerful tone of a woman sharpening a knife.

Toby Mudge came out of the composing room with a pie in one hand and the pail in the other, saw the Marquess, and stopped as though shot. "Your Marquessship! Mr Fairweather says I'm to say my lord, and Miss says I'm not to say your honour on account of the bailiffs, so I've settled on Your Marquessship, which is both. Miss says gentlemen eat on alternate Thursdays. Is this one?"

"I shall have to consult an almanac."

* * *

Winifred was at her frame under the best of the lamps, with her sleeves above the elbow and her mother's stick in her left hand, and she did not turn round.

"You are early."

"By the clock of St Paul's I am ten minutes late."

"By the clock of the press you are early. The press sleeps on a Thursday until nine and wakes at ten, and you have chosen a convenient hour, which I take for a compliment. I have a bishop and a circular and nobody to hold the cord. You have brought a ham."

"I have read the Article six times."

"Seven, I think. You have the look. It says money. It is silent on swine."

"I had hoped you would find that."

"I found it on Friday. I have been wondering what you would do with it." She turned then, and looked him up and down, from the broken nose to the wet boots, as she might have looked at a sheet of poor paper she was nevertheless obliged to print upon. "Take off your coat. You will be in my light, my lord, and a man who stands in my light is a man I must find work for."

The apron she took from its nail was her father's, of leather, scarred and stiff and cut for a man of twice the Marquess's girth; it went round him with a good deal to spare, and the pockets held a bodkin, a stub of pencil and the crust of a pie of great antiquity. She set him at the next frame and in six sentences explained the world.

"This is the case. The small letters live in the lower, under your hand; the capitals in the upper, because they are used less and may be farther off. The e has the biggest box because it is the commonest, and the j has a cupboard because nobody wants it. Every sort has a nick on its shank, and the nick faces out. If it does not, you will go wrong in a manner a pressman will describe to you at length." She put the stick into his left hand. It was heavier than it looked, and warm from hers, and his thumb found the hollow worn in the brass and came out again at once, as a man steps out of another's pew. "Mind it. It was my mother's. Set your name."

Julian, whose whole life had been an exercise in knowing which way a text ran, looked at the letters in the box, which faced the wrong way, and reasoned that to make a line come out forwards one must put it in backwards; and since the letters were backwards already, one must evidently begin at the far end. It was impeccable logic. He set his name from the last letter to the first, with the care of a watchmaker and an eye for the gaps, and Winifred pulled a proof on the little press in the corner and held it to the lamp, and one corner of her mouth went in.

"'Eremnats Nailuj,'" she read. "It sounds like a minor Egyptian god. Everyone does it, my lord. I set the Lord's Prayer from the end at eleven years old and was sent to bed. You were being intelligent. It is the commonest mistake of the intelligent."

"Is it?"

"It is the only one they make. The stupid make others."

From the corner came a cough like a drawer being opened. Mr Orme had risen with his stick in his hand, and he crossed the floor at his own speed, which was a line a minute, and took the damp proof from her without a word and held it at arm's length. He read it from the bottom right-hand corner to the top left.

"Julian Stanmere," said Mr Orme. "Backwards, miss, it is perfectly plain. Even. Not a river in it. Who's this?"

"Mr Orme, he is—"

"A new hand," said Orme, with satisfaction, answering a question nobody had asked. "I thought so. You space like a man who was taught by somebody that hated rivers. That's rare, young man. They're all rivers nowadays. Where were you trained? Clerkenwell?"

"Harrow," said the Marquess.

"Never heard of the house," said Mr Orme. "But they've done you well." He gave back the proof and went to his corner and sat.

Winifred opened her mouth to say what the Marquess was, in a voice that would have carried to Pomfret Yard, and shut it again. It was Orme's first compliment to a living creature in three years, and she found, to her slight surprise, that she had no wish to take it back. The Marquess of Stanmere had been praised in his time for a great many things, none of them his own: for a griffin, for eleven farms, for a nose broken in a cause thought to do him credit. He folded the proof in four and put it in his waistcoat pocket, behind his watch, where he kept the things he did not intend to lose.

By noon he had set four lines of Lady Constance's circular the right way round, been told three times to hold his thumb differently, and been made by Toby the Keeper of the Pan. "Your Marquessship, the quarter. I'm only a devil, and you're in the apron." He took the copper pan from the imposing stone in both hands, level, a little forward of the body, as Gantry carried a salver, and emptied it into the pail by the door. The Drip, he found, had a note and an interval and kept better time than the parish. At one, when the first sheet of the circular came off the Albion, Fairweather allowed him to lay a hand on the bar, an honour commonly reserved for visiting bishops and the owners of dogs, and said "Aye," which meant that he might pull, and then "Aye," which meant that he had pulled and might stop. SOCIETY FOR THE RELIEF OF DISTRESSED GENTLEWOMEN, said the sheet, and *distressed* was spelt correctly, and he was foolishly moved. He had steadied the paper with his right cuff. It came away grey-black from the wrist to the button, and in the doorway Minton closed his eyes.

* * *

They dined at half past one in the basement kitchen, on the ham, which Mrs Hobday carved with the air of a priestess in a good cause. Afterwards Winifred went up to her father, whose foot wanted its poultice at that hour as other men's want a pipe, and Toby, through a mouthful, told the Marquess that if he wished to be of use he might fetch a bodkin from the long drawer under Miss Win's frame.

The drawer held a whetstone, two pairs of tweezers, a paper of pins, the bodkin, and a round bonnet-box of faded green pasteboard, tied with tape, which had no business there and filled it from side to side. On the lid, in a small upright hand he would have known in the dark, someone had written *K.F. 9*.

The reader will have guessed what was in the hatbox. The Marquess had guessed it too, and opened it, which is the difference between a reader and a man.

Nine bundles, tied in red tape, one for each of the letters, and each made up of the proofs from the first pull to the last, folded in four and gone soft as linen with handling. He lifted the top sheet. He had written its first sentence in the library at Stanmere Court at four in the morning of last September, with a sleeping dog on his foot, and had never seen it in this condition, grey with other people's thumbs. Down the margin ran her queries. *You say "we" in this paragraph and "I" in the next. Which? W.P.* He had said *Which?* to every vague question since he was nine years old. He found it in a stranger's hand, and had to put the sheet down.

Pinned to the proof was a slip in Minton's copperplate. *The author is obliged. He means I.* And at the foot of the sheet, after all her marks, the same pale hand: *Thank you.* Beneath that, in pencil, very small, in her own, was a thing nobody had been meant to see, because it had never been sent. *Not at all.*

Through the other bundles her pencil went like a hand through a hedge. Beside a passage on the poor-rate: *True.* Beside the labourer's loaf, where the Farmer had set down what a family of six could buy on seven shillings: *I have seen the loaf.* He had the green pocket-book in his coat, with ninety of her questions pasted into it, and now he held the half of the correspondence he had not known existed, and it was kinder than his.

"You have found the bodkin."

"I have found a hatbox."

She did not hurry. She took it from him and put the lid straight, which is not the act of a person who is angry, and set it on the case between them like a thing that had been introduced. "They are the Farmer's. You may look. Nobody ever has, and it is a dreadful waste of a hatbox. Nine letters. Ninety-one queries; I count them. Every one amended, and not once a word of dispute, and at the foot, *Thank you.* Do you know how many authors have thanked me in ten years? Two. One was a curate and the other was dead."

"Dead?"

"His executors. He had left it in his will." She lifted a proof out of the box without looking at it, as one reaches for a cup in one's own kitchen. "Mr Ingram will say only that he is a gentleman in the Weald with a head for figures and no patience with ornament. I have made him a large man with a red face and a dog, who has been right about every hedge in Kent. I am quite prepared to find he is a clergyman with the gout."

"And if he were something else?"

"I should be sorry. I have taken some trouble over the dog." She did not smile, quite. "I read him, my lord. I do not read what I set; it would be like a butcher making friends with the animals. But the Farmer I read, right way up, with my hands in the stick, and some nights I cannot set the next line." She held the sheet to the lamp. "'The price of corn is spoken of in this country as we speak of the weather, as a thing that happens to us, when it is a thing that is done by us.' I wanted a semicolon after 'weather'. He said no. He was right. It is the only time I have been glad to lose."

It had cost him two hours, that comma. He had written it as a semicolon at four in the morning and taken it out at six and put it back at seven, and had walked about Stanmere Court ever since in the knowledge that nobody would ever notice. Somebody had noticed. She stood in the green light of the skylight with her blue thumb on his sentence, and she was speaking of him with the open, unguarded face of a person who talks of somebody she has never met and expects to like.

"Miss Pargeter," he said. "The Farmer is—"

"Yes?"

The sentence in his mouth was five words long. He saw, with great clearness, all that would follow if he said it: she would be astonished, and then delighted, and then she would like the Farmer in him, with a loyalty already nine letters deep, and the Marquess would be introduced to her afterwards as the Farmer's other self, and would stand at the back. He had been liked in his time for a griffin, for eleven farms, and for some months now by one woman in a hatbox for being right about the poor-rate. It had come to him that morning, with Mr Orme's proof in his waistcoat, that nobody had yet liked him for a thing he had done badly and in good faith. It was an extremely small thing to want. He found that he wanted it very much, and that this was exactly the reason a man gives himself when he is afraid.

"—a very fortunate man," he said. "To be so read."

"He is not fortunate," said Winifred. "He is read by a compositor. It is a different thing." She put the lid on the box and the box in the drawer, and gave him the bodkin, and took up her stick.

* * *

Barnaby came down at three on Toby's arm, backwards, a step at a time, with his foot in its flannel and the air of a man who has been told that a peer is using his Albion and means to see it done with respect. He was established at the end of the stone on one stool, with the foot on another. Winifred had set the Marquess to read the bishop's proof, since he could not be trusted with the type. He found three errors, and she found two of them.

"My dear fellow, you have ink on your cuff."

"I know."

"It will not come out."

"I did not suppose it would."

"Good. That is how you tell a convert from a visitor. A visitor will wipe it, now, but I was going to say, about the toggle, that if it were set a trifle lower— no. Never mind." He regarded the Marquess over the spectacles on his forehead, that is, at the ceiling. "You are looking at the stick."

"I was looking at the thumb."

"Everyone looks at the thumb. It is the stick's, you know, not hers: the hollow. Two thumbs in the one place. Naomi's first. Naomi Lamb; a compositor's daughter. She set the Psalms at nineteen, in a back room in Warwick Lane over a chandler's, in Long Primer, for a Bible-warehouse that never knew whom to thank. The whole Book of Psalms, by a candle in a saucer, in four months, and I went round to read the proofs. I read them for a fortnight. They were perfect on the first day. I am told it is called courtship." The Albion sighed through the wall. "She died in the spring of 1807, of a consumption, and Win took up the stick the week after the funeral and has not put it down. She was seventeen. You will have noticed that she carries things."

"I had noticed."

"I let her," said Barnaby, "which you will also have noticed. I am the one sitting down." He lifted the flannel an inch, and let it fall. "It is a very fine excuse." Then, quickly, as a man gets a tooth over: "I am bound to ask, my dear fellow. It is the form. A father who doesn't ask is a father who can't be blamed, and I would rather be blamed. What are your intentions?"

The Marquess had been asked in his time by eleven mothers and one bishop what his intentions were, and had answered them all with a number. He found that he had no number.

"Honourable," he said.

"Quite right," said Barnaby. "Nobody else's are ever any use." He looked immensely relieved, and as though he could not have said why, and took the spectacles from his forehead and put them on his nose and, as a result, saw nothing.

* * *

It was a quarter to four when the sound of the Albion stopped, which in that house was like the stopping of a clock, and everyone looked up.

A small gentleman stood in the door of the composing room with his hat in one hand and a furled umbrella in the other. He was perhaps forty-eight, and neat as a pin that has been polished. He wore a coat the colour of snuff, with no snuff on it, and a pair of spectacles pushed up on his forehead and a second pair above them, and a magnifying glass hung on a ribbon from his waistcoat; and his hands, folded on the handle of the umbrella, had been scrubbed to the pink of a new mouse.

The Chapel closed its ranks without anyone appearing to move. Orme's stick stopped. Fairweather, in the press-room door, folded his arms. Sparrow, who had been whistling, broke off in the middle of a bar and found a great deal to do at the far end of the room, and in the passage below Mrs Hobday's hymn gave out at "the Lord is."

"Barnaby," said the small gentleman, in a voice as soft as a clean cuff. "I would not have intruded on a day like this."

"Hector!" said Barnaby. It rang a good deal too heartily. "My dear fellow, my dear Hector, come in, you are wet through—"

"Madam." A bow to Winifred. "My lord." A smaller bow, the exact bow due to a marquess and not an eighth of an inch beyond. "Hector Lovejoy, of the Phoenix Press, in Ave Maria Lane. Your lordship's servant. I had the honour to be Mr Pargeter's apprentice for seven years, and have been his neighbour for twenty-six. I have called to pay my compliments, as one does. I wish you joy, my lord. It is a very remarkable thing."

"Mr Lovejoy," said Julian. "You are very good." It was not a lie, and he was aware of saying it carefully.

Mr Lovejoy looked up. The Drip let go of its slate and struck the pan. "You have a leak, Barnaby."

"A trifle."

"One in four seconds. It will have the beam in three winters." He said it as another man would remark on a view. He was shown the Albion, which he asked to see as a man might ask to see a friend's grave, and ran one pink finger down the toggle without touching the ink. "Ebenezer's hand. Beautiful. I have always said, Barnaby, it was the handsomest folly in London." Barnaby, who had heard it called the folly a hundred times in the Row and the handsomest never, laughed far too loud. In the corner Mr Lovejoy gave the Old Lady a small nod, as one nods to a lady met earlier in other circumstances, and she, being oak, did not return it.

Toby had stationed himself by the street door with a stack of folded Gazettes under his arm, relics of the Thursday of the sixth that he had found at the bottom of the loft stair and had been selling in the court since Monday to the curious, at a shilling a head. Mr Lovejoy stopped in front of him.

"You have a number of the sixth of June, I think, my boy."

"It's a shilling, sir. They're relics."

"The price is printed above the title," said Mr Lovejoy. "Sevenpence, the stamp included. I should not like you to take a shilling off a gentleman when the Government has taken fourpence already." He counted the money into the child's palm. "One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven." Each coin was set down separately, in a voice of grave kindness, as pennies are laid on the eyes of the dead. He took the paper and folded it in three, with the care of a man who folds nothing carelessly, so that the end of the magnifying glass showed at the fold. Then he turned at the door to Winifred, who had not moved.

"A very fine W," said Mr Lovejoy.

He bowed to the room, and went out under the arch, and the rain closed behind him.

Barnaby had gone the colour of tallow. Winifred stood quite still, with the stick in her hand and her thumb in the hollow, and her face showed nothing, which in a face so much used to showing the weather was a thing to be afraid of. Julian, who understood none of it, asked.

"What is a very fine W?"

"It is a remark on a letter."

"I had gathered it was a letter. I am a reasonable man."

"Quite," said Winifred, which was his word, and she gave it to him in his own voice, and he heard it.

It was Barnaby who spoke at last, in the light high voice of a man making a joke so that the room will not have to. "He was the finest hand I ever trained, my dear fellow. Seven years. I never told him so. One doesn't. They get above themselves." He looked at his thumbs. "Hector holds my paper, you know. For the Albion and the damp. Seven hundred and forty pounds of it, at six per cent, falling due at Michaelmas, and a very civil gentleman about the interest."

*Michaelmas.* The word went into the Marquess like a pin. He had asked her a week ago in this room why Michaelmas, and she had said that it was a Quarter Day, and it had been, as she said, perfectly true. In the time it took Barnaby to finish the sentence he had arranged four things in his head: a banker, a lawyer, a plumber, and a word in Mr Underhill's ear. Three were money and the fourth was Mr Underhill. His hand had gone to his breast, where the Marquess of Stanmere kept, by long habit, the instrument for getting out of any difficulty in the world, which was a cheque-book on Child's, and it had stopped there, half drawn.

"Article the Third," said Winifred, without looking up.

The hand went back.

* * *

The Albion began again, and the room resumed itself piece by piece, as a room does after a bell. The lamps were lit at five, in June, in the rain-dusk of the Row. Minton went out to find a hackney, and the Marquess of Stanmere went out into the court by himself, in his coat and one ink-grey cuff, and stood by the pump.

He went through the verbs of the Third Article as a man tries the keys on a ring: *lend, give, advance, promise.* They were all the wrong key. He had signed it himself, with a tolerable laugh, in front of a witness who wept. It was an extremely well-made Article, and he had admired the placing of its second comma.

The rain ran into his collar. Behind him, through the window of the composing room, he could see the lamp over the first frame and under it a small square figure with a brass rule in her hair, who had picked up her mother's stick again and was setting a line, quickly, without looking at the case. Nobody had asked him to stay, and nobody had asked him to go. There was a sum, and a woman, and a man with a magnifying glass, and the thing in his pocket that would have made it all go away had been forbidden him by a contract of his own devising. He stood in a printer's court with his hands in his pockets and the ink of the Society for Distressed Gentlewomen drying on his cuff, obeying an agreement he had written himself, and found that it was the least comfortable thing he had ever done for anyone.
