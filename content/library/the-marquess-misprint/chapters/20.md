# Miss Fell Takes Off Her Hat

At half past five on the morning of the third of September Miss Agnes Fell sat on the edge of her bed in the attic of Hertford Street with a pen in one hand and a sealed letter in the other, and looked at the window, in which, because the candle stood behind her, a thin woman with a white streak in her hair looked back.

The streak began at the left temple and ran back into the grey like a path through a field. She had spent twelve years persuading caps to cover it. It had not occurred to her until lately that it was the one memorable thing about her, and that she had been hiding it as other people hide a scar, when it was the only distinction she possessed.

The letter was directed *To Lady Constance Dyer, on leaving*. She had written it across three nights, in the voice she kept for her best work, and it was the best thing she had ever done, a fact she would have paid a good deal not to know. It said everything. It said it twice, in different tenses. On the washstand beside the ewer lay a second paper, folded in three, which had come up on Sunday night with the chocolate and which said, in a hand like a fence: *Agnes. You will come to the morning-room when you have read this. C.D.* It had lain there two days. She had read it, without once lifting it from the marble, some four hundred times.

It is time the reader was told how Peregrine Pry was born, since the reader has known for some weeks who he was.

In the autumn of 1814 Lady Constance, in a season of economy, had arranged to pay her companion forty pounds a year, a quarter in arrear, on principle. In the same month Agnes's brother Matthias, a lieutenant on half-pay, who had by some process she had never been able to follow run up three hundred pounds in hosiery, wrote from the Borough to say that the hosier was growing weary. Agnes had by then sat a pace behind her employer's shawl at a great many routs. People speak before a woman holding a shawl as they speak before a clock. One night, for no reason she could afterwards give, she wrote an account of the patronesses of Almack's in the voice of a person whom everybody looks at, and sent it unsigned to the Phoenix Press by a law-stationer's boy; and on the Friday there came back three guineas and a note of one word, which was *More*.

She had found out that she was good as one finds a shilling in an old coat: first with suspicion, then with joy, and then with the reflection that one has been wearing the coat all along. She was proud of it. That was what she could not get into the letter on the washstand, though she had tried it in three tenses. She was proud of the turtle. She was proud of a sentence about the Dowager Lady C——, whose ear-trumpet was the only instrument on which she permitted herself to be played; and she was proud, Heaven forgive her, of the little quizzing-glass that Mr Lovejoy set at the head of the column in a Long Primer italic of great beauty, and that she had looked at every Friday night for two years as one looks at one's own name on a door.

At eight she went down, with the letter, and without her courage, which she had left on the washstand with the note.

The morning-room was cold, and Lady Constance was not. She sat bolt upright at the escritoire in the window, in black bombazine and the purple ribbon, with no trumpet in her hand and no Blücher at her feet; the pug, who knew a ceremony when he saw one, had retired under the table. She did not turn round. She said, to the glass:

"You have read my note."

"I beg your pardon," said Agnes. "I have read it a great many times, and—"

"Do not beg my pardon. It is a habit, and it spoils the sentence. Say whatever it is flat."

So Agnes said it flat. She said it in four sentences, which she had made on the stairs, and which came out in the order of a column, with the best at the end. "I am Peregrine Pry. I copied Miss Pargeter's Articles in the estate office at Stanmere Court on the night of the third of August, and I sent the copy to Mr Lovejoy on the fifteenth. He holds my brother's debt, which is three hundred and twelve pounds, and I give that as a fact and not as an excuse. I beg your pardon."

The last had slipped in after all, against orders.

Lady Constance turned from the window. Her face did not alter, which was how one knew. "You have been under my roof."

"Yes."

"For twelve years."

"Yes, my lady."

"I have given you a room with a window, and the use of a dog." Her voice was cold, exact and perfectly level, and each word was laid down like a card. "In that room you sharpened a pen to say that I am played upon by my own trumpet."

"Yes."

"I thought it the best thing you ever did." Lady Constance regarded the ceiling. "It has lain on my conscience since June that I admired it. Sit down, Agnes. Not there. There is a plover."

Agnes sat. She had expected a good many things in the night, and had prepared against all of them but this.

"I have burned twenty-one letters," said Lady Constance. "In twenty-one years. They were not mine, and I told my nephew so on Sunday. You posted one paper, which was not yours. We shall see which of us is the more ashamed. I have twenty years' start of you, and I mean to keep it."

"Letters?" said Agnes. "Whose?"

"You shall have the rest another day. I find that the habit of telling grows on one, and that it is very tiring."

"Am I to go?"

"You are to stay. I have not decided what for, but the dog is accustomed to your biscuits and I am accustomed to the dog." She looked at the letter in Agnes's hand. "What is that?"

"My notice."

Lady Constance took it, turned it over, read the superscription, and opened the third drawer of the escritoire, in which there was a space the exact size of a folded sheet. She laid the letter in it, unopened, and turned the key. "I shall keep it," she said. "I am told it is better than burning. Where are you going?"

"To Hand and Pen Court. I should rather say it to her face than write it. I have done enough of the other."

"Then go." And as Agnes reached the door: "Agnes. You will say it flat."

* * *

She walked, because a hackney is a closed box with a man in it who looks at one in the glass, and she had been looked at in glass enough that morning. Two miles and a half in the rain, under the black straw bonnet that she had worn for twelve years pulled down to the eyebrows, with a brim like a pent-house; and she saw nobody's face, which had been the intention.

Toby opened the door of Pargeter's, with half a pie in his mouth. He looked at her. "It's the lady with the mittens!" he said, and ran off down the passage shouting for Miss Win in the voice of a boy who has seen a ghost, or a bailiff, or in either case something that might want pies.

On the step Agnes did what she had not done in a public place since 1804. She took off her bonnet. It was a small thing. It was like undressing.

The composing room was quiet. The Gazette's inner forme had gone back to Pomfret Yard's own press-room, where, Mr Orme said, it was being done with the enthusiasm of a man pulling his own tooth, and Fairweather stood idle by the Albion with a cloth in his hand, like a verger at an empty church. Orme had taken up his stick again on Monday, on the ground that a man must do something with his hands. The Drip fell. Winifred stood at her frame with her back to the door, setting a card for the Dowager Duchess of Ormsgill, who desired the pleasure of two hundred persons' company on the nineteenth of October, and who had shown no sign of having read the *Crier* except by doubling the order.

"I am setting," said Winifred, not turning. "If it is Lady Constance's business, you may say it to my back."

"It is not Lady Constance's. It is my own."

"Then you may say it to my stick." Winifred held out a composing stick, over her shoulder, without looking; with her other hand she slid a pair of cases of Great Primer across the frame. "Set what you have come to say, Miss Fell. I shall read it when I reach the end of this line, and I shall not read it before."

Agnes took the stick. It was an iron one, Toby's, with a screw, and it was heavier than a pen by a good deal. She knew the lay of the case. She had counted it for a fortnight in the old brewhouse at Stanmere Court, as she counted everything, and had never meant to use it. She found the first letter with her thumb. The type was upside down and backwards, and the nick on its shank had to be felt for, like the edge of a stair in the dark; she set the line from left to right, with every letter facing the wrong way, and it took her forty minutes to say what she had said in Hertford Street in a quarter of one.

She set it in capitals, because it seemed to her that what she had to say was not a matter for the lower case. *I AM PEREGRINE PRY.* Nine lines, the width of a psalm. When she had set the last she stood holding the stick, in which the whole of it was lying the wrong way round, and found that her hands had stopped shaking some lines ago and she had not noticed.

Winifred reached the end of hers, stuck it, and turned. She read the stick the right way up, which is to say the wrong way round, as she had read everything since she was seventeen. Then she read it from the other end. She found two errors in the time it takes to say so: DBET for DEBT, and, in BROTHER'S, an E of the wrong fount, a size too small, which stood in the line like a child in a row of grown persons. She mentioned neither.

"Thank you, Miss Fell," she said. She had gone, as she did when angry, extremely polite. "That is very exact. Lady Constance has been told?"

"This morning."

"And she said?"

"That she had burned twenty-one letters, and I had posted one paper, and we should see which was the more ashamed."

Something went across Winifred's face and was put away. She had read a Florence postmark across a room in June; she did not say so. She was thinking, with the unfair clearness of the very angry, of her father, who had sat in this room on Saturday with the *Crier* over his face and said on Sunday at breakfast that he thought he would go to bed for a week; and of the bond on the house; and of the stick, which was Toby's, and would make a very fair missile. She laid it down on the ledge.

"You did not walk two miles in the rain to set one stick," said Winifred.

"No." Agnes's voice came out thin and she let it. "He writes to me on Fridays. A line only. On Friday he wrote: *The 28th, madam. Six o'clock. Mr Sowerby will swear before an alderman on the 25th. I shall be obliged by a column on the compositor's conscience. H.L.*"

"The twenty-eighth is a Saturday."

"The last before Michaelmas. There is to be a second number of the *Crier*, with the carter's affidavit, that you saw the error at a quarter to five and let the cart go. It will be at the corners at six in the morning. I did not send the column." She swallowed. "I do not think he knows yet why."

"He has an eye for a title," said Winifred. "On the compositor's conscience."

"He tells me what is true and useful, and nothing else. It is the most frightening habit in the world."

"Yes." Winifred looked at the Drip. "Yes, I have noticed."

She stood so for the time it takes a pan to fall three times. Behind her, in the corner, Orme's stick went on ticking at the case, deaf and perfect, setting someone's sermon. Then she said, in a different voice, the voice of the shop, which asks what the job is:

"It is mine."

"I beg your pardon?"

"I set it. I saw it at a quarter to five and I let it go, and Mr Sowerby saw me stand there. It is mine to tell, and he shall not tell it first. I shall tell it myself, in my own name, in a place where it will do the most harm, which is to me." She almost smiled; it was not a kind smile, but it was the first. "And I shall set the correction."

"The *Gazette* will not print a correction. Mr Underhill—"

"Mr Underhill edits the *Gazette*. Mrs Rudge owns it."

"Mrs Rudge is at Bath."

"Then I shall write to Bath." Winifred took up a bodkin, looked at it, and put it down. "And there is the money. I cannot pay eleven hundred pounds in September, and I shall not be bought out of it, by anybody."

"Miss Quarles keeps her father's books," said Agnes. "I may be—" She stopped. It was the sentence that had hanged her. She had said it for twelve years without noticing and for three days could not say it at all. She said it now, with deliberate dread, like a woman putting her hand back on a stove. "I may be quite wrong, but I do not think she has been asked."

"No," said Winifred. "She has not."

"And there is the matter of the *Gazette* on the streets before six. The run takes the Albion and the Old Lady near three hours at the best, and it cannot be done short-handed. I counted, in the press-room, on my way through. The Albion wants two men at the bar, and you have one." Agnes looked at her hands. "I may be quite wrong, but I think you ought to ask the Marquess to pull it."

Winifred did not answer. She picked up the Duchess's card, found a place for her thumb on the edge of it, and read it as though it had been in a foreign tongue.

"I should like," said Agnes, "to read your proofs. I am very good at finding what is wrong with a thing in print. It was my profession."

"Mr Ingram reads for the *Gazette*."

"Mr Ingram has a quinsy."

"He always has a quinsy." But Winifred held out the card, across the frame, without looking up. "The third line," she said. "Tell me what is wrong with it."

Agnes took it. She read it once, with her bonnet in her left hand, and said, "There is a comma after *honour* that does not belong." Winifred looked at it for a very long time, and took it out.

* * *

She wrote the letter that evening, at the kitchen table, when Mrs Hobday had gone up and Barnaby's snores were coming down the speaking-tube in a rhythm like a slow press. It ran to eleven pages. It began *Madam, On the sixth of June last the Gazette printed a paragraph that was false, and I set it. I have the honour to ask your leave to tell you how*; it began upright, in the small square hand of the proof-margins, and grew smaller as it went, like a person going down a stair. It told the whole. It did not leave out the Drip, nor the cat, nor the cart, nor the arch. When she had signed it she did not read it over. She had a feeling that if she did, she should find something she wished to correct, and it would be the truth.

"I will take it," said Agnes. "The Bath mail goes at eight from the coach office in Piccadilly. I beg your pardon, but I think you would rather it went by a person who knows what is in it."

"Yes," said Winifred, and gave it to her.

At half past six Miss Fell put the packet in her cloak, took up her pen-case, and went out through the arch of Hand and Pen Court into Paternoster Row. It was raining. She had her bonnet in her hand. She had meant to put it on at the corner, and at the corner she found that she had not.

She walked down the middle of the Row, between the dark shops with their lamps already lit, past the booksellers' apprentices and the umbrella of a curate and a woman who sold chestnuts; and she looked, for the first time in two years, at every face she met. None of them knew her. The rain came down on the white streak at her temple and stood there in beads. She did not put up a hand to it.
