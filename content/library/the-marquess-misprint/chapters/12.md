# Let It Stand

The Stanmere carriage stood in Piccadilly with its lamps lit and its wheels hub-deep in an arm of the sea. It had come down from Hertford Street in good order as far as the dip below Devonshire House, where the road falls towards the Green Park and the drain beneath it, which had been asked since six o'clock to carry away the whole west end's opinion of the weather, had given up the attempt. There it had stopped, with its four lamps shining on a sheet of black water that went off down the hill into the dark, and the rain coming down upon the roof with the exact sound of a great many people drumming their fingers.

Inside it were two persons, each in a corner, and neither saying anything.

After some minutes the coachman climbed down from the box. Pegg was fifty, with a face like a walnut and a gift, much prized by his employers, for hearing nothing. He took a horse by the head in either hand and went forward into the flood in his drab coat, up to the thigh, at the pace of a funeral, and he did not look back. This left the carriage, to any eye that cared to look, a lit yellow box with its blinds drawn, from which nothing could be seen and in which nothing could be heard but the rain; and it should be set down here that the arrangement was entirely Pegg's. The Marquess had given no order. Pegg had driven the sixth Marquess through the whole of a quarrel and the seventh through the whole of a Season, and he would be remembered at Michaelmas for his tact; there are men who can find the lamps of the next carriage at the bottom of a hill without once turning the head.

"My lord," said Winifred.

"Miss Pargeter."

"I have something to tell you which is not covered by the Articles."

"Is it a thing I should sit down for?"

"You are sitting down."

"Then I am prepared," said Julian; and was not, as it turned out, in the least.

She said it plainly, in short sentences, looking at the blind. She had practised it for twenty-eight days, in the dark, in the room on the second floor with the cat on the foot of the bed, and it came out in the order in which she had practised it, as a line comes out of a stick. "At twenty minutes to five on the morning of the sixth of June, Fairweather handed me one of the first sheets, which had been hanging to dry since two, and a heel of bread. I read the Fashionable Intelligence. I found my name in it. I did not need to be told what had happened, because I had set the page; I knew where each line had come from. I knew at once."

He said nothing.

"I did the arithmetic. If I stopped the cart, I must reset the forme and print eleven hundred again, and the cart would go at nine, not a quarter past five, and that was Clause Six, and Clause Six was a second breach, and the contract was void. If I let it go, there was a chance that the paragraph would be corrected, and that was Clause Nine, and the contract was void in another way. But a chance is not a certainty. Stopping the cart was a loss I could count. Letting it go was a loss that might not come." She drew a breath. "It is the sort of arithmetic a person does who has seven hundred and forty pounds to find. I took the uncertain one."

"And the carter?"

"Sowerby. He asked if there was anything the matter with the load. I said nothing. I stood in the arch with the sheet in my hand until the cart had turned into the Row, and he looked back once, at the corner. My father's bell rang. I went up and gave him his tea." She turned her head at last. In the light that came through the blind she was very small and very upright, with the rule in her hair. "I have told my father that I made the error. That is true. I have not told him that I saw it. I have not told anyone. You and I are the only two people living who know it for certain, and Mr Lovejoy, I think, knows enough. I thought you should hear it from me."

He was silent for so long that she began to count. She had reached forty-one.

"Say something," said Winifred. "Please. Anything. You may say that it was wicked. It was."

"I am considering the arithmetic," said Julian.

It was, in point of fact, what he was doing; but it was not what he was feeling, and he was a little surprised to find the two so far apart. He had thought since the sixth of June a number of generous things about the error. He had pictured the rain through the slates, and a tired woman, and a mischance; he had been prepared to forgive it, handsomely, as one forgives a rose for its thorns. He had not pictured this. He found, examining himself in the lit dark of his own carriage, that he preferred it. A mischance is a thing that happens to a person. A choice is a thing a person does; and he had sat for twenty-eight days beside a woman who had done one, in the same hat, and been exactly as civil as before.

"Then we are the same sort of coward," he said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"When I read the paragraph at breakfast, I laid a pencil on it. An H pencil, sharpened at five. I laid it flat across the words *entirely well founded*, and I did not make a mark. Minton stood at my elbow with the draft of a correction in his hand, and said *My lord?*, and I said *In a moment.* I said it twice. It is the second moment I should like to take back." He turned the ring. "I had a pencil, Miss Pargeter. I was in the act of correcting the *Gazette*. I am the only man in England who reads it with a pencil. And I put it down."

"You said on the seventh that you would let it stand."

"I had decided on the sixth. I only did not know that I had." He looked down at his hands. "It is the whole of my character. It would be a great relief to me to suppose there was another."

She did not say that it was not true. It was the first time that he had known her not to say a kind thing that was untrue, and it moved him more than the kind thing would have done.

* * *

"I should like to show you something," said Julian.

"Is it a thing I should sit down for?"

"You are sitting down."

"Then I am prepared," said Winifred; and there was a little breath of a laugh in it, the first since Hertford Street.

He took it out of his breast pocket. It was a small flat book of green morocco, much handled, with a ribbon marker, and he laid it in her gloved hand and said nothing at all. Inside the lid, on the first leaf, in a small even hand she did not know, was a date in November: and below it, pasted square on the leaf with a thin tide of paste at the edge, a slip of proof the size of a thumb-nail, cut with scissors from a margin. The slip said *A semicolon is not a courtesy.*

It was in her own hand.

She turned the leaf. There was another. *Delete 'deplorable'. You have used it three times in a column.* And another, and another, a pasted slip on every leaf and under each a line in the same even writing, in pencil, dated: *She is right.* *She is right.* *She is wrong, and I shall not say how.* *Quartern. She is right.* There were pages of them. She went through them with her thumb, the blue one, past a hundred, a hundred and three, with the lamp of the carriage swinging a little on its bracket as the horses moved and the rain going on overhead; and she saw her own commas, and his figures, and the whole of a year laid out in two hands, one over the other, as a proof lies over a forme.

"You are the Farmer," said Winifred.

"I am the Farmer."

"*The author is obliged. He means I.*" She heard her own voice go up. "That was Minton's slip. In the hatbox."

"Minton wrote it. At my instruction. I thought it was amusing."

"You thought it was—" She shut the book. She held it in both hands in her lap and looked at it with an expression that Julian could not read in the dark, and then could, quite suddenly, when the lamp swung: she was white round the mouth, and her eyes were very bright, and it was not at all certain which of two things was about to happen. "Nine months," said Winifred. "I wrote to you for nine months in the margins of a sheet, and you were in a house in Curzon Street, with a pencil. I told Mr Ingram I should like to make you say you were wrong. I said it in front of you. You stood at my stone and let me say that a man who has never been wrong is a wall."

"You did put it very well."

"I said it to your *face*. On the twenty-second of June. And you told me that the Farmer was a bore in person."

"I said I was told."

"You have the conscience of the Articles," said Winifred. "I will give you that. You did not lie aloud." She struck the book with her hand, once, on the cover. "And at Lady Constance's table, before sixteen persons, I informed Sir Marmaduke Crake that I was acquainted with the Kentish farmer, and you choked on a bone."

"It was a pheasant."

"It served you right."

"It did," said Julian. "It was extremely painful. I assure you, it was not a thing done for effect."

"You came to the Row on the seventh of June," said Winifred, and there was an odd shake in the voice, "to find out who had been correcting your commas."

"And to ask who had set the type. The one reason was a pretext for the other." He had the grace to look at the blind. "I have not yet decided which."

She laughed. It broke out of her in a helpless rush, and she put the green book against her mouth, with her shoulders shaking, and he sat across from her and watched it happen with an expression of the most extraordinary gravity, as a man watches a thing he has been told he will not be shown. It was a long time before she could speak. "*The quartern, not the peck,*" said Winifred, into the morocco.

"I was waiting to be asked."

"I should have asked."

"I should have said." He took the book from her gently. He did not take the hand. "You wished me wrong, once. I am prepared to oblige."

"Then oblige."

"I was wrong. I was wrong on the sixth of June, and every day after it; and wrong at the stone on the twenty-second, and in my own library on Sunday. I have been wrong in the *Gazette* a hundred and three times, and you have the receipts." The carriage rocked. Somewhere ahead, in the rain, Pegg was speaking to a horse. "It is a very strange thing. I find that I don't mind it at all."

* * *

They came to the arch at a little after one in the morning, with the flood behind them and the rain easing. Pegg drew up in the Row, at the mouth of the Court, which was too narrow for the carriage; and Julian handed her down, and paid no attention whatever to the coachman's absence of expression, and walked with her under the brick arch, eight feet wide, into the dark.

The Court was empty and shining. The pump stood in the middle with its handle laid like a drowned arm, and above them the sooty brick of Pargeter's went up three storeys into the black, with one light in it, high up, where Brevier sat in a window. Under the keystone of the arch, a smaller cousin of the Drip was falling into a puddle with a note she could have sung. She stopped under it.

"Here," said Winifred. "I stood here."

"I know."

"I had good boots. I did not use them." She laughed, shakily, at nothing. "It is a stupid thing to say. It is the thing I have said to myself, in the dark, for twenty-eight nights."

He did not say that it was not stupid. He did not say anything. He stood in front of her, a tall man a little stooped, with the lamp of the court's one lantern in his eyes, and the white notch in the left eyebrow lifted, and he put out his hand and took the rule from her hair, which came down. He put the brass strip in her palm and shut her fingers over it. And then he kissed her, under the arch where the cart had gone out, with the rain falling beside them and a small clean drop between: once, with great care, as he would have set a word, and then rather less carefully, and for some time.

* * *

It was Winifred who stopped. She stopped with her hand against his waistcoat, flat, as one lays a hand on a forme to feel whether it is level, and she took a step back, and looked at him in the lantern light with her hair on her shoulders and her mouth not at all steady.

"We have broken an Article," she said.

"Which?"

"Six."

The corner of his mouth went. "I believe it said *an Attachment of the Affections.* I have been seeking a loophole for some time. I find that it cannot be done by attaching one oneself."

"Do not be clever. You are not clever. You are exact, which is a different matter." She put her hand to her hair, and discovered the rule in her fist, and looked at it. "I will say it once, and I shall say it properly. I will not say yes tonight."

"I haven't asked."

"You are about to. I can see the clause forming." She drew a breath. "While my father's notes are Lovejoy's, I will not be a woman who was bought by a dinner. Do you see? I should never know. I should be in your house, and I should think, *he arranged it;* and it would be a thing between us for forty years. I have seven hundred and forty pounds to find and eighty-seven days. Ask me at Michaelmas. When I do not need you."

He was a long time silent. It came to her that she had never before seen the Marquess of Stanmere with nothing in his face at all. He had a trick of answering a vague question with *Which?*; he had been put, as it were, by a question into the state of a man in a chair with nobody on the other side of the table, and she watched him find it was possible to sit there.

"Stet," said Julian.

"Yes?"

"Let it stand. The Articles, to Michaelmas. And I shall ask you then, and you will tell me." He stopped. "I have, as you may have observed, a very bad habit of arranging things. I propose to be broken of it by an expert."

"And the Farmer?"

"I would ask you not to tell anyone the name. Not yet. Not your father. It is not the shame of it. I find that I should like, once, to be answered by the man and not the peer. I should like to be argued with." The signet turned. "I have not had that very often."

"I shall not tell," said Winifred. "It is not in the *Gazette*."

It came back to her then. She had not been looking for it; it came as it always does when it is not looked for, as a letter drops out of a case of its own accord. It was the Duchess, in the dim drawing-room in Hertford Street, at the pitch at which she said everything: *Which of them knows?* And Constance, with the trumpet laid by: *Neither.* She stood under the arch, with a drop of cold water falling upon the toe of her shoe, and understood precisely what had been meant, and who it was that had not known, and how long; and that it had been settled at a dinner for sixteen, by two old women with a pug, a good while before she was told.

"Heavens," said Winifred.

"What is it?"

"Nothing. A proof coming back." She turned to the door.

It was on the latch, as it always was. She pushed it, and the passage was dark and smelt of ink and lye, and a draught went up the stairs; and out of the wall by the composing room door, from the brass mouth of the speaking-tube Barnaby had rigged to his bed, came a long sleepy rumble that carried a good deal further than he supposed, out through the open door and across the Court to the tall man standing in the lantern-light with his hat in his hand.

"Win? Is that you? Is that rain, or the Marquess?"

Winifred put her mouth to the tube.

"It is rain, Father," she said.

The rain, which had been easing, came on again at that moment with a fine unanimity, in a long grey wash across the Court, upon the pump, upon the sooty bricks, upon the Marquess of Stanmere standing in his evening clothes with his face turned up to it; and it would be an exaggeration to say that Winifred Pargeter had told her father a lie. It was rain. The Marquess was merely also there.

* * *

At two in the morning, a hundred yards away, in Ave Maria Lane, there was one light burning in the long front of the Phoenix Press, in a first-floor window, behind a bow of a hundred and twelve small panes. It had been burning since eleven. It was a candle in a tin sconce, and under it, at a high desk, with a pair of spectacles pushed up on his forehead and another pair on his nose, a small neat man in a snuff-coloured coat was bent over a ledger. He was counting something. His lips moved. From time to time he made a mark with a very sharp pencil in the margin, and drew a line, and counted again; and he did not look up when the rain came on harder, since it was not his roof.
