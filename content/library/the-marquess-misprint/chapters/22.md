# The Quaker and the Clause

A post-chaise from Bath came into Pomfret Yard at ten minutes to ten on the morning of the twenty-fourth of September, splashing a drayman, and out of it was lifted, by a postilion and a footman acting in some sort of consultation, a very small old woman in Quaker grey, like a parcel that has been marked This Side Up and is being treated accordingly.

"Thee may put me down," said Mrs Rudge. "I am not a parcel, though I have travelled as one."

She stood on the cobbles in the rain, seventy-two years old and four feet eleven, in a grey bonnet like a coal-scuttle with a white cap beneath it, her better ear turned toward the noise of the yard and a pencil standing out of her reticule like the hilt of a sword. She looked at the offices of the *Morning Gazette*, a long dark house with a bow window and a brass plate, as a woman looks at a child that has been left in the charge of servants. Then she went in by a back stair, and was not seen again until she was in her chair.

Winifred's letter had reached her on the fifth. The answer, of one line, had reached Hand and Pen Court on the eighth: *I shall come when my chest will let me. Do nothing until I do. K.R.* Winifred had done nothing for sixteen days, with great skill, as other people do something. She had printed the Duchess's cards and the first of the Black Bull labels. She had said the opening of her speech to the Drip some four hundred times.

They had come in a hackney, she and Barnaby, with his foot on the opposite seat. "Win," he had said, at the corner of Fleet Street. "Whatever you say in there, say it in the order it happened."

"It is the only order I know."

"Your mother used to say that a thing was true when it came out in the order it happened. She said that lies come in order of convenience." He blew his nose, a trumpet-call, and pushed his spectacles up. "I shall stop in the cab. I have been told to say nothing to anybody. I mean to say it to the horse."

Mrs Rudge had sent for them both, and for a third. Winifred had sent word by Minton that she would tell it alone, and the third party had not argued. He had merely arrived at a quarter to ten, and sat down upon a bale of newsprint at the foot of the steps, under the lean-to, with his hat on his knee, since nobody had forbidden bales.

* * *

The counting-room of the *Gazette* had a stove, a ledger-desk, a hard chair, and a clock that was nine minutes fast on principle. In the hard chair, with her good ear to the room and her pencil in her lap, the proprietor was already seated. Mr Underhill stood before her, in a coat buttoned correctly for once, pink to the roots of his hair. Mr Ingram stood at the window in his muffler. On a stool at the far side, with a leather folder on one knee and the glass on its ribbon at his waistcoat, sat Mr Lovejoy, who had been invited by Mr Underhill, and who had come, as he went everywhere, early.

"Winifred Pargeter," said Mrs Rudge. "Come in out of the wet. Ezekiel Underhill, thee is pink. Thee was pink in the fourth month of 'ninety-seven, and I said then that it was a poor colour for a man who prints the truth."

"Madam—"

"Presently. I would sooner hear thee last." She turned the good ear a fraction. "Thee may begin."

He began. His voice, which had been a great organ for nineteen years, came out in the upper register, like an organ with a mouse in it. "The position of the *Gazette*, madam, is a simple one, and I have set it out in a memorandum. By the ninth clause of the contract of 1812, any error of the press requiring a correction shall render the contract void and the bond of five hundred pounds forfeit. An error has been proved. It has been proved in a public print, and by the confession of the party. The contract is void, the bond is forfeit, and the house in Hand and Pen Court answers for it. Mr Lovejoy, with great public spirit, has been so good as to offer to take up the work at four-fifths of the late price."

"I have drawn the heads, madam," said Mr Lovejoy, rising an inch, "if you wish to see them."

"Thee will keep them in thy folder, Hector Lovejoy. I have not come to be sold anything." Mrs Rudge took up the pencil, which was the signal, as every clerk in the building knew, for the end of other people's remarks. "Winifred Pargeter. Thee wrote me eleven pages. I have read them four times, with this, and made no mark on them, which is not my custom. I should like to hear thee say it. All of it. Ezekiel Underhill will be quiet, which will be good for him."

So Winifred told it.

She told it in the order in which it had happened, which is the only order a compositor trusts. She told the Drip, and the copy that came in damp at five from a clerk in a fair hand, and the second Drip at eight, which blotted the name-line. She told the cat. She said that a page had fallen at eleven into pie, from a cord that she had told a boy of thirteen to reknot and had not looked to herself; that the boy had had a pie in one hand and the cat underfoot; that she had never asked him and he had never said; and that she would be obliged if the room would take the cord for hers. She told the heap on the stone, and the two lines of one measure that lay together in it, the Marquess's name-line and her own advertisement for fair copying, with the W in it that had a nick in the left foot. She said that a compositor does not read; she sees letters, as a baker sees flour. She said that she had set what she saw.

Mr Underhill made a small noise, as of a man swallowing the word *libel*. Mrs Rudge's pencil moved a quarter of an inch, and he stopped.

She told the sheet at twenty minutes to five, hung to dry since two; Fairweather's heel of bread; her own name. She told the arithmetic. She had done it standing, in the time it takes to say it: a reset, a reprint of eleven hundred, a cart at nine; Clause 6 on one side of the scale and Clause 9 on the other. She gave the figures aloud and softened none of them, because in her experience a figure softened is a figure lied about. Mrs Rudge listened with her head down, and when Winifred reached the arch she looked up.

"How long was the cart in the arch?"

"Four minutes."

"And what did thee think of, in the four minutes?"

"The bond. The house. My father." Winifred stopped. She had not put this in the letter. "I did not think of the Marquess at all. He was a name on a copy-slip. It is the thing I am most ashamed of, madam: I thought of him as a measure."

"And then?"

"I let it go."

It was very quiet in the counting-room. The stove ticked. In the yard a cart went by, and a carter swore at a horse in the tone of a man who has seen a great many weeks go past in the rain.

"The compositor's honesty is touching, madam," said Mr Lovejoy, quite gently, "and arrives four months late."

"Yes," said Winifred.

She did not defend herself. She had considered it, and found that there was nothing to defend that she would care to have defended.

Mrs Rudge laid down her pencil. "Why now?"

"Because the *Gazette* is the only paper in London whose owner reads it with a pencil."

The old woman looked at her for some seconds with clear, unhurried, unamused eyes, the eyes of a person to whom nothing has been said for forty years that she did not hear. Then she turned the good ear to Mr Underhill.

"Ezekiel Underhill, I have heard thee call the ninth clause the glory of this house. I have thought about it at Bath, which is a place made for the purpose. A newspaper that cannot say it was wrong cannot be trusted when it says it is right. I said that to thee in the sixth month, in this room, and thee took it for a remark upon the weather. The ninth clause is a rule that teaches printers to hide. It has taught one. It is struck."

"Madam, the contract—"

"The contract is restored as of the sixth of the sixth month, without the clause, and the bond is returned. Write this in its place. *An error of the press shall be corrected in the number next following, in type as large as the error was set in, and the printer who first reports it shall not be penalised.* Write it, Ezekiel Underhill. I have a good ear for dictation and a poor one for argument."

He wrote it. The pen shook slightly, and the room, with the tact of long habit, looked at the stove.

"And on Saturday," said Mrs Rudge, "the *Gazette* will print an erratum. It will be the first in nineteen years. The compositor who made the error will set it, and sign it, in Great Primer. I do not wish it softened. I wish it to be where it will be seen."

"The *Gazette*, madam, does not retract." It came out with something of the old organ, but thin, like a note held after the bellows have been let go. "It clarifies."

"Then thee will have the honour of doing it for the first time." She took a folded sheet from her reticule. "And beside it will run a tenth letter from the Kentish Farmer, which came to me at Bath on the third. I have read it three times with this pencil, and found nothing in it that wants correcting, which has never happened to a Farmer before. It begins, *For A Kentish Farmer read Julian, Marquess of Stanmere.*"

There was a pause, in which the clock, nine minutes fast, struck the hour.

"The Farmer?" said Mr Underhill. "My Farmer is—"

"Thee never asked who he was, Ezekiel Underhill. Thee asked only whether he was sound." Mrs Rudge folded the paper. "He is sitting on my bale in the wet, and I think it good for him. It runs, if the writer will let his name stand under it, and I have no doubt that he will."

Mr Ingram looked at the ceiling with the face of a man who has kept a secret for eighteen months and is not certain whether to be relieved. Mr Lovejoy, on his stool, remarked "I did observe the commas," to no one in particular; and then, because it was true and might be useful, he stood.

"It is only fair, madam, to tell the *Gazette* that the *Crier* will print Sowerby's account on Saturday at six. The carter has sworn to it. I should not like you to hear it first from a hawker."

"Print it, Hector Lovejoy." Mrs Rudge did not raise her voice. "Thee has not told a lie in thirty years. I have watched." She turned to the stove, the clock, and the editor, in that order. "Then the *Gazette* will be carted at a quarter past four, an hour before its time, and be on the streets before the *Crier* is cried. It has never been done."

"Madam, it cannot—"

"It will be done by the Pargeters, Ezekiel Underhill, who have done a harder thing this morning. Thee may inform thy carters."

* * *

Winifred came down the steps at eleven with her gloves in her hand. It was still raining, in the thin, discouraged way in which it had rained since May; and at the foot of the steps, on a bale of newsprint, with his hat on his knee and his hair stuck to his forehead, the Marquess of Stanmere got up. He had been there an hour and a quarter and looked it.

"Well?"

"I have been given until Saturday," said Winifred, "to say that I was wrong, in type the size of a door."

"Great Primer is eighteen points. It is rather smaller than a door."

"You have not seen the paragraph."

He opened his mouth and shut it. He had learned, in the last three weeks and at some expense, the exact moment at which a sentence of his began to be an arrangement, and he stood at the edge of it like a man at the edge of a pond. "May I—"

"Dick Sparrow has gone to Mr Lovejoy," said Winifred. "We are short a pressman. The Albion wants two at the bar, and Fairweather will not do it with the boy standing on a box. You may pull it."

"I should be honoured."

"You will be paid nothing."

"I had not hoped otherwise."

"You signed it," she said. It was the other matter, and it came out sideways. "The tenth. With your name."

"You told me to, in July, in the brewhouse." He turned the signet on his finger. "You were right. I am a little late in being told so."

"I have not forgiven you."

"I know."

"I have forgiven the bar-pulling."

She went past him across the yard, toward the pump, where a hackney stood with a gouty foot raised at its window and a horse being addressed in a low voice on the subject of Mrs Rudge; and she did not look back, having learned from her trade that the thing one looks back at is the thing one sets again.

He stayed where he was. On the bale beside him, where he had been sitting, lay a folded newspaper, someone's Tuesday *Gazette*, left by a porter gone to his breakfast, and the rain, which had found it, was beginning to turn the inner sheet to pulp. It was an extremely small matter. It was a stranger's property and worth sevenpence. He looked at it, and then he took off his coat, which was a good one, and laid it over the bale and the paper together, to keep the rain off.
