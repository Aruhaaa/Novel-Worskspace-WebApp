# The Road to Yalding

The Season, which had been wet from its first night, guttered out through July like a candle in a draught, and nobody was sorry but the milliners. Parliament had risen. The Duchess had gone to Brighton to disapprove of the sea. The *Gazette*, having nothing to say in August, said it at length in the second column, to the satisfaction of Mr Underhill, who held that a quiet month was a form of praise; and in Hand and Pen Court the Drip fell upon the stone with the regularity of a clear conscience, while two persons who had once broken an Article under an arch kept all the others with a rigour that would have shamed the Chapel. The Marquess of Stanmere called on Tuesdays and Fridays at hours convenient to the press. He stood at the far end of the composing room and discussed the price of paper. Once, handing her a proof, he touched her sleeve instead of her hand, and they both regarded the sleeve for some time as though it had said something improper.

That was how matters stood on the morning of Tuesday the thirtieth of July, when Lady Constance, on the principle that an engagement left in London through August turns like milk, removed the whole of it to Kent. The party, assembled in the Row at five o'clock in a rain that had not the grace to wait for daylight, looked less like a visit than the migration of a small and eccentric nation. There was the Marquess's travelling-coach, Pegg on the box, which held Lady Constance, the pug, Miss Fell with her writing-case upon her knee, and the Marquess. There was a second coach, yellow, enormous and hired, which smelt faintly of the dogs of a former century, and into which Barnaby Pargeter had been handed with his gouty foot in a bucket of warm bran.

Mrs Hobday, who stayed to keep the house and Mr Orme, stood under the arch with her arms folded and commended the bucket to the Lord. "*Thy foot shall not be dashed against a stone,*" she said, "provided the ostlers do their part."

And at the tail of everything, behind two horses the colour of old boots, came a waggon belonging to a carrier of Bermondsey named Heaver, who spoke seldom and then chiefly in oaths. It bore, under a tarpaulin and a great deal of rope, the Old Lady, whom eight men had needed the whole of the previous afternoon to get on to it; two cases of Pica and Great Primer; eleven reams of paper in oilcloth; and, on a hamper from which a single indignant ear protruded, Toby Mudge, who had been given three pies to see him to Kent and had eaten them by Lewisham. The hamper spoke at intervals, in a voice not unlike Mr Underhill's.

The Quarleses were to follow at their own pace, and Lord Rupert upon a borrowed hack; and in a gig of her own went Miss Verity Quarles, who had declared that she would sooner drive thirty-eight miles in the rain than sit eleven hours beside her mother's smelling-salts, and who set off first, at a speed that suggested she meant to arrive before the weather.

The reader will forgive the narrator for not taking him over every mile of the Maidstone road, which was that summer a river persuaded to carry vehicles. The hay stood rotting in its cocks the whole length of the Kent Road, and the wheat beyond Farningham, which ought by the thirtieth of July to have been gold, stood the colour of a wet mouse.

Winifred had counted the days to Michaelmas that morning, from habit, as other women count stitches. There were sixty-one. She did not mention it, and the coach, which was large, held her father, Mr Minton and a quantity of silence of the kind that has been politely agreed upon. Mr Minton sat opposite with his watch in his palm, consulting it every four minutes with the expression of a man waiting for it to confess. Barnaby, who could bear a silence about as long as the Albion could bear a loose quoin, said somewhere past Foots Cray:

"My dear Win, did I ever tell you of the Bible of 'thirty-one?"

"Yes, Father. Four times."

"The King's printers, Barker and Lucas, who left the *not* out of the seventh commandment, so that the whole kingdom was instructed in the plainest English to commit adultery. Three hundred pounds fine, and every copy burned that could be found. I have never been able to read the Decalogue since without feeling for the compositor." He gazed with great benignity at the rain. "A man may be hanged for a thing he did on purpose, my dear, and it is no great matter, he knows where he is. It is very much harder to be fined for a thing he didn't."

Mr Minton looked at his watch. Winifred looked out at a hop garden gone black on its poles. Barnaby, who had not the least notion what he had done, took a pinch of snuff with the air of a man who has contributed to the conversation.

* * *

At the Bull in Wrotham, a little after two, they took up Dr Orlando Pym. He was waiting under the archway with a hat that held a puddle and a portmanteau that clanked: a long mild gentleman of fifty-odd, in a clergyman's green-black coat, who had a living on the Medway and had gone up to London the previous Friday to petition the Dean and Chapter of St Paul's to take the lightning-rods off their dome. He had been received by a verger. Lady Constance, appealed to from the step of the first coach, said that her carriage was full and held up her trumpet to prove it; and so it was Barnaby who put out a hand and said, "My dear fellow, you will be wet," and brought the Doctor in.

"It is the rods, madam," said Dr Pym to Winifred, arranging the portmanteau upon his knee, whence it continued to clank. "They went up in 'sixty-nine, and since then the electrical fluid that ought to discharge itself in an honest clap has been drawn quietly out of the heavens, and what is left has nowhere to go but down. I have figures."

"For what period, sir?"

"For Kent, madam, since the first of May."

"That is ninety-one days."

"It has rained on eighty-six of them," said Dr Pym, with the deep content of a man whose theory has been borne out by the weather.

The hill at Wrotham comes down off the Downs into the Weald in one long slope of chalk and clay, which in a dry year is a pleasure to the eye and in the wet of 1816 had the surface and the temper of a cheese. The postilion put the skid under the near wheel at the top, as he ought, and the skid did what a skid will do on grease, which is to become a sledge; and half-way down, in the middle of a sentence of Dr Pym's concerning the discharge of the clouds, the whole ponderous yellow coach turned slowly to the left, as a stout lady turns to look at a hat, and lay down in the ditch.

Nobody was hurt. The ditch was deep rather than wide, and held to the depth of a man's thigh a liquid the colour of tea. The coach lay at a cant of thirty degrees with its off-side wheels turning in the air, and the bucket of bran had travelled the length of the interior and, by way of Dr Pym's hat, arrived at Dr Pym. Barnaby, wedged in a corner with his foot above his head, observed peaceably that he had been wishing to put it up since Lewisham. Winifred got the door open over her head and climbed out upon the side of the coach, which was now the roof, and from there to the bank. Mr Minton, who had gone out first through the window in the interest of lightening the load, had arrived in the ditch up to his chest.

She took him by the tails of his coat, in both hands, and hauled. He came up out of the clay like a cork out of a bottle and stood on the verge streaming, and looked at his watch, which was full of ditch.

"Five minutes to three," said Mr Minton, with the calm of the thoroughly ruined. "It has stopped. Miss Pargeter, I think I ought to tell you something before we arrive. It may come up."

"Come up, Mr Minton?"

"Peregrine Pry." He wiped his face with a wet glove and made it worse. "After the *Crier* of the sixth of July, with the flood in Lady Constance's kitchen, which only the household could have known, his lordship suspected me. It was perfectly reasonable. I have ink upon my finger; I am anxious; and I had, as it happened, a motive of shame. He put the question on the Tuesday, in the library, quite baldly: *Minton, are you the author of the Quizzing-Glass?* It had been three days. It had felt a week."

"And were you?"

"No!" said Mr Minton. "I had one proof of it in the world, Miss Pargeter, and it was in my pocket, and it was worse." He lowered his voice, although the nearest person was a postilion. "A sketchbook. Forty drawings. Of his lordship. His lordship as a heron. His lordship as Atlas, with a ham. His lordship correcting the Archbishop's signboard with a pencil. They are not unkind. I think they are, rather, the contrary. But one does not wish to be found out."

Meanwhile, at the ditch, the rescue had begun. The first coach had gone on a quarter of a mile before it was found that the second was not behind it, and the first to return was Lord Rupert, on his hack, who looked at the yellow coach in its ditch and said "Good God" with real feeling and "Is anyone hurt?" with rather less. The second was the Marquess, bareheaded, on foot, at a run, whom Winifred observed coming down the hill with an interest she attributed entirely to the coach. Rupert, in a waistcoat that had cost more than the coach, remarked that Stanmere had asked him to a great many things in twenty years but never before to a ditch; and Julian said "Lift," with his shoulder under the axle and his hat in the mud; and they lifted, with the postilions at the horses and Sir Ephraim, who had come up behind in his own carriage, calling "Plain terms, gentlemen, together," and the coach came up out of the clay with a sound like a cork leaving a bottle, and stood on the road, steaming.

"He turned over all forty," said Mr Minton, at her elbow, in a low and unsteady voice. "It took ten minutes. He said, *You have my nose right.* And then he said, and I have it by heart, *I accused you on no evidence but your fingers, which are inky because you work for me. I am sorry for it, and I should like the one with the ham, if you can spare it.*" He swallowed. "Two sentences. I have them written in my pocket-book."

"And Mrs Bunting?"

"Her ladyship decided that the leak must come from the kitchen, and dismissed her. It lasted a day. She was reinstated when her ladyship recollected that Mrs Bunting could not spell *turtle*." Julian, passing with the mud to his knees, put a hand for a moment on Mr Minton's wet shoulder and took it away again, and Mr Minton went the colour of a ham. It occurred to Winifred that if Mr Minton was not Pry then somebody else was; and she put the thought away, as she put away a good many that summer, in the drawer where she kept the proofs she had not yet found time to correct.

* * *

The waggon arrived at the foot of the hill at four, at a walk, with the tarpaulin streaming and Toby asleep upon the Old Lady with his mouth open. It crossed the brook that had left its bed at the bottom of the road; and the near hind wheel dropped into a hole the brook had made, and the axle-tree parted with a noise like a pistol.

Heaver stood in the road and said what a carrier says, at length and in a regular metre. Winifred waited for it to end, as she had waited for many things to end, with her hands folded before her.

"How far to a wheelwright, Mr Heaver?"

"Wrotham, miss. Four mile up the hill."

"And a sound axle?"

"Maidstone."

She looked at the waggon. It stood on three wheels and the stump of a fourth with its tail in the brook, and on it, lashed under the tarpaulin, eight hundredweight of English oak, the only press in Kent. "Hop-poles," said Winifred.

There was a stack of them in a gateway, ranked like a palisade: a thousand ash poles, fourteen feet long, which had stood there since March waiting to have bines put on them. She had two taken down. From her bag she took a coil of page-cord, the string with which a compositor ties up a page of type; and out of her hair, after a hesitation in which Rupert, who was watching, said nothing at all, her mother's brass rule, which she laid flat along the break like a splint along a bone. The poles went alongside the axle, one on either hand. The cord went round them, wet from the brook, to shrink as it dried; and into the angle of the cord she drove four wedges, in the order in which one drives the quoins of a forme, from the middle outward, a little at a time. The difficulty was to lift eight hundredweight in order to do it, and for that she needed six men, who were found in the next field, idle for want of work, and persuaded by a stone jar of Black Bull Entire, a full gallon, which Sir Ephraim produced from the boot of his carriage without being asked. "I never travel without the product," said Sir Ephraim, "any more than a physician travels without his lancet." It was a principle of her father's that no machine was ever lifted by an argument.

They let it down. The splint creaked. It held.

"My dear girl," said Rupert, "I have seen a duke take less trouble over a divorce."

"Mr Heaver will drive at a walk," said Winifred, putting the rule back into her hair with a care she did not give to her own head, "and if he hears it speak, he will stop."

* * *

They came to Yalding at the last of the light, which was not much. There was the long old bridge, and the church of St Peter and St Paul at its foot, and the Medway running full and brown beneath the arches, a good deal fuller than a river should be that is not yet in flood. The road ran out of the village between hedges that dripped, and turned at a lodge into an avenue of limes; and at the end of the avenue the Court stood on its slight rise, Elizabethan red brick with a Georgian front put on like a clean collar, with the meadows going down below it to the river, silver and drowned, and the oast-houses of the home farm with their white cowls all turned to the wind like a congregation. No smoke rose from any chimney.

At the lodge gate a cluster of women had gathered under sacks, to look at the carriages. They had stood an hour in the wet to do it, with a number of children distributed among them like the effects of a sale; and when the first coach had gone through, the women closed in upon the second, which was slower, and a voice at the window said, "Be you the lady in the paper?"

Winifred let down the glass. The voice belonged to an old woman as small and brown as a nut, in a man's coat and a sack for a hood, leaning on a stick; she was, Winifred afterwards learned, eighty, and she looked in at the coach with the unsparing interest of a person who has not many entertainments left.

"I am Miss Pargeter."

"That's what it said. Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row. Jem's mother had it read out to us at the Plough, off a bit of a newspaper somebody's cousin left. Is it right?"

"It is in the *Gazette*," said Winifred.

It came out as it always did, evenly, with the small pause before the second word that Lady Constance had taught her in Hertford Street. It had served her at Ormsgill House and at the Duchess's table and before an Archdeacon. It had never before been said to anyone with a stick.

Mrs Fryer considered it. "Then it's a lie, my dear," she said, not unkindly, "for I can't read it."

Somebody behind her laughed, and was hushed. Winifred sat with her hand on the window-strap. For six weeks the formula had been a shield and a joke and, by degrees, something very like a principle; it was true, every word of it; and it had not till this moment struck her that it was only true for those who could read it. It was a thing one said to people who believed in print rather than in faces. Mrs Fryer believed in faces. She had stood in the rain for an hour to look at one.

He had got down from the first coach and come back along the line on foot. He stopped at the window, and took off his hat to a woman of eighty in a sack.

"Mrs Fryer. How is the leg?"

"Wet, my lord."

"And Jem? Is the fever down?"

"Down, thank the Lord and the Doctor's physic."

"And Mrs Pankhurst's youngest? The croup."

"Mended. Though Pankhurst's cow's gone dry."

"Duchess?"

"Duchess," agreed Mrs Fryer, "for want of grass, my lord, there being none."

He listened. That was what Winifred observed, from the window, with the rain running down the glass between them. In London he had a manner for every room, and all of them were dry; in Kent his sentences grew short and his silences long, and the silences were not empty, but were used, as a compositor uses a quad, to give the words about them room. He knew the wives by their Christian names and the children by their ailments, and, with a gravity that made Winifred look hard at the window-strap, the cows by their grievances. A thin boy hung at his mother's skirt with a sleeve over his mouth, and Julian's eye went to him and rested a moment and went away, as the eye of a man goes to an account he has not been able to balance.

"Is it true, my lord?" said Mrs Fryer. "Her in the coach."

Winifred waited for it. He had said it for six weeks to dukes and archdeacons with a face of stone, and it would have served him now.

"I'll tell you what is true, since you cannot read it," said the Marquess of Stanmere. "Miss Pargeter is a very good printer, and I am more fortunate than I deserve to be seen in her company."

It was an answer Article 4 would have passed without a mark. It had the advantage of being perfectly accurate, and of containing no engagement whatever. "Ah," said Mrs Fryer. She considered him a while longer, and then Winifred, with her sack-hooded head on one side. "Well. That's better than a newspaper."

* * *

Mr Wragg was on the step with a lantern: a grey man of fifty-eight, with a face as unrevised as a milestone, who regarded London as an infection that sometimes arrived by carriage. "This is the printer woman, my lord?" he said. "This is Miss Pargeter, Wragg." "Ah," said Mr Wragg. "The printer woman." Beside him stood Mrs Pickett, who had kept the keys of Stanmere Court for forty years and carried them like the Last Trump, and who announced to the assembled company that there was no fire lit in the house but the kitchen, the wood being wet and the coal not come, and delivered it as one announcing a death in the family.

The waggon came up the avenue at ten o'clock, at a walk, with Heaver at the horses' heads listening to the axle as one listens to an invalid; Toby asleep upon the Old Lady; and Brevier, released at last from the hamper, sitting on the tarpaulin with the air of a cat who has been to Kent before and thought little of it. The steward's office was the old brewhouse of the Court, from the days when the Court had brewed: a long stone room with a copper the size of a bath in one corner, a high desk, eleven bundles of leases in red tape, one for each of the farms, and a smell of cold hops. Mr Wragg unlocked it with the air of a man opening the family vault to a stranger who has expressed an interest in the bones.

"The press, miss. Nobody told me."

"I am telling you now, Mr Wragg."

"Ah," said Mr Wragg.

It took her, Toby and two footmen, both of them called John, until a quarter to midnight to put the Old Lady where she wished her, on the flags under the great beam, from which Wragg's man hung the block and fall by which she had been swung down from the cart; and when it was done, and the footmen gone, and Toby rolled up in a sack on the settle with the cat on his feet, Winifred lit a single lantern, set it on the stone, and stood a long while with her hand on the cold bar, doing nothing whatever. She could not sleep in a house that had no press in it. It was a failing of the same family as the Drip.

It was after midnight when a second light came into the yard. She saw it through the window, smeared with rain: a lantern, carried from the direction of the house by a tall man who wore no hat. It crossed the cobbles to within ten yards of the brewhouse door and stopped. He did not knock. She did not go to the door. He stood in the wet with his light on the stones about his boots, and she stood in the dry with her hand on the bar, sixty days now from Michaelmas, it being past midnight, and rather more than that number of feet from him; and the rain came down between them in the two cones of lantern-light with a good deal of patience. Two lanterns burned in Kent that night, one in a brewhouse window and one in the yard outside it, and for a good part of an hour neither of them moved.
