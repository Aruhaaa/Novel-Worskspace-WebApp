# A Page in Pie

Every four seconds, or thereabouts, a drop of water let go of a broken slate in the roof of Pargeter's printing house, fell eleven feet through the brown dusk of the composing room, and struck the bottom of a copper preserving-pan with a small flat note, like a coin put down by a very patient creditor. The pan stood on the imposing stone for the purpose. Toby Mudge, who was thirteen and the printer's devil, was made to empty it into the pail by the door every quarter of an hour, and had been so made since the first of May, which came to a great many quarters and had given him views on arithmetic. The household called the leak the Drip, and Mrs Hobday said grace over it, since it was present at every meal.

It was Wednesday the fifth of June, 1816, and the grates of London were lit. Nobody in Hand and Pen Court could have told you why the summer had failed to arrive, and the newspapers, which could generally tell you why anything had failed, were reduced to calling the weather a scandal. The wheat stood thin in the fields, the hay was rotting about Islington, a quartern loaf was a shilling and expected to be more, and the town, having nothing better to do indoors, was still chewing over Lord Byron, who had gone abroad in April and left a great deal to chew. The rain ran down the pump, through the brick arch and out into Paternoster Row, where it met the rain from every other court and made, at the corner of Ave Maria Lane, a river with opinions.

The reader who has never stood in a printing house on a wet evening is asked to furnish one as follows. Two rows of wooden frames, each holding its sloping cases of type like a lectern; a stone slab in the middle, under a skylight the colour of dishwater; a proofing press; a speaking-tube of japanned tin that climbed the wall and vanished through the ceiling towards a gouty man in bed. Make it colder than a church. Add the smell of lye, lamp-oil and wet wool, and under these the faint sweetness of lead, which compositors say they cannot smell. At the first frame, under the best of the lamps, stood Winifred Pargeter, who was twenty-six and five feet four, and square across the shoulders, and wore her dark hair pinned up with a brass composing rule thrust through the knot, so that from behind she looked as though she had been shot at with a very small arrow and had declined to notice. She smelt faintly of lye. Her left thumb was blue-black to the ball. It would not wash, and she had stopped asking it to.

At five by St Paul's, which struck through the rain like a man with a cold, a boy came in under the arch with his hat held before him in both hands, as though it contained something hot. He was perhaps eleven, in a coat cut for a brother of fifteen, and he had been sent from Curzon Street by Mr Minton with orders to put his paper into the hands of a compositor and not a devil. It was a great deal for a boy to carry, and he delivered it all at once.

"Mr Minton's compliments, and it's the last of the Fashionable Intelligence, and it's to go in tonight exactly as written, and I'm to give it to the lady and not to him." He looked at Toby, who was eating a pie.

"I'm a devil," said Toby, with dignity and some gravy. "It's different."

Winifred took the slip out of the hat. It was a half-sheet in a clerk's fair hand, damp, as everything in London was damp, through no fault of its own. She did not as a rule read what she set, the sense being apt to get in the way of the work; but this once the copy pleased her, and she read it standing, aloud, because Mr Orme in his corner could not hear a word unless she did.

"'We are authorised to state that the report of an engagement between the Marquess of Stanmere and Miss Quarles, daughter of Sir Ephraim Quarles, Bart., is entirely without foundation, and that his Lordship is at present engaged to no lady whatever.' Heavens. A gentleman has issued a bulletin on his own bachelorhood."

"Eh?" said Orme.

"Lady Quarles," she said, louder, "has told the town for a fortnight that her Verity is to be a marchioness, and the Marquess has written to the Gazette to say he never heard of it. Poor Miss Quarles. To be refused by a man who never asked, and at sevenpence."

"Is it true," said Toby, looking up from the gravy, "that gentlemen don't eat pies? Only I've been told it and I can't make it hold together."

"It is true of this one. He lives, I am told, on alternate Thursdays."

Orme had held out his hand for the slip. He was seventy-one, the Father of the Chapel, and had set type since before the American war, a line a minute, like a clock with a conscience. He took the half-sheet to arm's length and read it from the bottom right-hand corner to the top left, as he read everything, and gave judgement. "Stop after Bart., miss."

The whistle in the speaking-tube shrieked. Winifred pulled the cork, and a voice came down it like coals down a chute.

"Win! Is that the copy? Tell Toby the pan wants emptying, my dear fellow, I can hear it filling from here, it has a note like a bishop's, and now what would happen if the toggle were set a trifle lower, I wonder, it would — no. Never mind. What is the copy?"

"The Fashionable Intelligence, Father. The Marquess of Stanmere is not to marry Miss Quarles."

"A wiser man than most. Set it as written, Win. Underhill would find a comma in a coffin, and the Gazette has not printed a correction in nineteen years, and he is rather proud of it, so we shall not be the ones to spoil it. Toby! Are you eating? I can hear you not saying anything."

"No, sir," said Toby, swallowing.

She began to set. The stick in her left hand was her late mother's, of brass, worn bright at the thumb-rest, and into it, upside down and in a looking-glass, her right hand dropped the letters one after another, nick uppermost, with a click like small rain on a tin roof. The click and the Drip kept time together, as a married couple do when they are not speaking. By six she had the Births and Marriages and the court news in the galley, and by seven the Marquess's paragraph was half in the stick, and she talked to it the whole way, as she always did when the copy pleased her.

"'At present engaged to no lady whatever.' That is a long line, it wants breaking. Mr Orme, if I were a marquess I should have it engraved on a plate."

"Eh?"

"Nothing, Mr Orme."

At the foot of the third column, where the eye of the Gazette's reader never fell, stood three lines of her own, kept standing since Lady Day at a price she preferred not to dwell on: FAIR COPYING in a plain hand, for authors, lawyers and composers of music, and below it, centred, *Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row*. The capital W had a nicked left foot, an old wound in the Pargeter fount, and it printed with a hairline gap, a white thread across the leg, as though the letter wore one boot thinner than the other. She had meant to replace it since March. A new sort was fourpence; but fourpence is only fourpence when there is also an afternoon.

At eight the roof, which seemed to have no other occupation, began a second Drip. It found a new road through the slates above the first frame and fell, with the unerring malice of water, upon the slip where it hung from the copy-holder at the height of Winifred's eyes, and struck it on the name-line. When she lifted the slip from the hook, the lower half had gone to a grey map of the Low Countries, with three words left standing on it like churches. She had set a good half of the paragraph and carried the rest in her head, having read it aloud, and she saw no reason for alarm. She laid the ruin on top of the case to dry.

"Two now," said Toby, interested. "I shall have to be twice as quick."

"You will have to be neither. Put the pail under the frame."

In a better-regulated week the damage would have been mended at eleven, when the proof of the page went across to Pomfret Yard by a boy and came back marked by Mr Ingram, the Gazette's reader, who had not in nine years let a comma pass without a hearing. But Mr Ingram was in bed with a quinsy and a mustard plaster. Mr Underhill, the editor, had sent word that proofs this week would be read by the compositors themselves, which Barnaby said was like asking the cat to be her own mouse; and by a rule of Mr Underhill's older than the quinsy, no proof went to Pomfret Yard after midnight, an editor kept from his bed by a proof being, in his own opinion, an editor who will find an error in it out of spite.

At ten Mrs Hobday came up the cellar stair with a can of tea, a quarter of cold mutton and the stump of a candle, humming *Rock of Ages* in the key of a woman carrying a tray. She was sixty and wide, with forearms like hams.

"Cleft for me," she sang, and cleft the mutton accordingly. "Man shall not live by bread alone, miss, and I notice he does not live by type either. Is it nearly done?"

"I have two columns to go."

"The Lord made the world in six days," said Mrs Hobday, "and did not have Mr Underhill." She went down singing.

At eleven, with the Drip at its slowest and Winifred gone through to the press room to see about a roller that stuck, the first thing went wrong in Pargeter's that night; and it went wrong, as most things do, with nobody looking.

Page two stood ready on the galley frame, tied in its cord: four columns of Long Primer, Fashionable Intelligence above the rule, advertisements below it, some thirty pounds of lead. Toby had been told to carry it to the stone, as he had been told on every Wednesday since Michaelmas, with both hands. He carried it with one, because the other held the last of the pie, and the cord, which Winifred had told him on Monday to knot afresh, was not knotted afresh. Brevier, the shop cat, a grey animal with a torn ear who had been named for a very small size of type when she was very small and had since outgrown it, came across the floor after a moth. It was never settled whether Toby tripped over the cat or the cat over Toby.

The page fell. A page of type falls all at once, with a noise like a dresser of cutlery pitched downstairs, and afterwards it lies on the floor in a state called pie: not a page, nor a word, nor properly a letter, but a drift of small grey sorts, some of the lines still holding hands in twos and threes where they went down, the rest scattered like a congregation at a fire. The pie lay on top. Both pies, in a sense; Mr Mutlow's beefsteak, face down on the Fashionable Intelligence, which seemed a fair judgement.

Toby stood in the middle of it for as long as it takes to count three. Then he picked up Brevier, who was unhurt and affronted, and went out by the back door to the coal-hole, where he told her everything. She had been a party to the event and did not contradict him.

Orme slept on, with his chin on his chest and one hand curled round a stick that was not there.

Winifred came in at the noise and found the wreck, the sleeper, and nobody to blame, and supposed, as anyone would, that the cord had parted. It had been a loose cord. She had said so on Monday. "Heavens," said Winifred, extremely politely, and Orme opened one eye.

In the doorway behind her Fairweather, enormous, looked at the floor and said "Aye." Dick Sparrow, over his shoulder, whistled through the gap in his front teeth. Sparrow had lately taken to wearing a clean neckcloth, which the Chapel had remarked upon, as the Chapel remarks upon everything, and had not yet explained.

"Two hours," said Sparrow, "for a page that wants three."

"Then it wants two hours of me. Go away, Mr Sparrow, and whistle somewhere with a roof. The forme must be locked by one or the cart goes empty. Mr Orme, distribute the loose sorts, please. Everything that is not in a line."

"Eh?"

"Everything that is not in a line!"

"Backwards, miss," said Orme, rising, "it is perfectly plain."

She did not ask what was.

The reader may suppose that she read as she worked. She did not. A compositor does not read; she sees letters, as a baker sees flour, and makes a line as a baker makes a loaf, by weight and look and ten thousand repetitions, with the sense going by overhead like weather. Sense is a luxury of those who read type the right way up. Winifred read it upside down and in a looking-glass, so fast that she had lost the knack of reading it any other way, and could have set a sermon in Hebrew without knowing whether it was a sermon.

By midnight she had the advertisements back, and the Births, whose lines had held together in the fall, and three lines of the Marquess's paragraph, which had held as well. A fourth was in pieces. In the gap she wanted a line of the right measure, and the heap, which was much of a muchness, offered two, lying together on the stone like sisters in one bed: both began with Miss, both ended on a comma, both were spaced out to the same length. One was the end of the Marquess's paragraph. One was her own advertisement. The first lay under the crust of the pie; the second lay on top, clean; and the slip, which might have told her, was a map. She had seen *Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row* every Gazette day for twelve weeks. It had long ceased to be a name and had become a piece of furniture, and a person no more reads her own name than she reads the shape of her own doorstep.

The rain had taken what it liked from the slip, and it had liked the negatives. The rest she supplied from her hands. In four years of Fashionable Intelligence the phrase that most often followed an engagement had been *entirely well founded*, and the one that most often followed *engaged to* was *the lady*; and the fingers know what the eye has not been asked.

At twenty to one Fairweather came and stood at the end of the stone. "Aye," he said, which in the dialect of Pargeter's meant that the Albion was inked, the paper was wet, and the Lord did not wait.

"A quarter of an hour."

She planed the page down with the mallet, drew the quoins tight with the shooting-stick, and lifted one edge of the chase to see that nothing rose. It held, a slab of iron and lead as handsome as a ham. There ought to have been a proof. There was a proofing press at the end of the room and a boy to work it; but the boy was in the coal-hole, and the clock said a quarter to one, and the rule said midnight, and Mr Ingram was in bed with a mustard plaster. She looked at the forme once, as one looks at a child in a Sunday coat, and found it uncommonly clean.

The whistle shrilled in the wall.

"Win! Is it locked? Has Ingram's boy been? My dear girl, there was a man called Ruddock in my day who proofed with his eyes shut and found more errors than any reader in London, I always said it was the dark —"

"Mr Ingram is ill, Father. The forme is locked."

"Is it clean?"

"It is as clean a page as I have ever set."

It was perfectly true, and she was to remember saying it.

The presses began at half past one. In the back room the Albion, which Barnaby's cousin Ebenezer had forged for him in Clerkenwell in 1813 and which rival printers called Barnaby's folly, drew down its platen with a long iron sigh under Fairweather's hand, while Sparrow rolled the ink; and the Old Lady, who was oak and screw and built like a gallows, answered from the other wall with a groan, Orme laying on, Toby come back from the coal-hole in a straight paper hat to beat the ink-balls, and Winifred on the bar. The sheets came off one after another, glistening, and were hung on lines across the room like the washing of a family that wrote to the papers.

"You are very quiet, Toby."

"Yes, miss."

"It was the cord. I ought to have knotted it myself, and not told you."

"Miss —" said Toby, and beat the ink-balls till they sang. "It's only the ink, miss. It's got in my eye."

The last sheet came off at ten past four. Winifred sat down on a bale of paper and did not read a word of them.

At twenty to five Fairweather, who said "Aye" about eleven times in a working day and meant something different each time, planted himself in front of her with a heel of bread in one hand and in the other a sheet that had hung on the line since two.

"Aye," he said. It meant *Eat*.

Daylight had come into the press room at some time after four, as it does in June even when nobody has asked for it, a grey wet light without a source. She took the bread in her right hand and the sheet in her left, and because her hands were full and her head was empty she did what she had not done since six o'clock the evening before. She read.

> MARRIAGE IN HIGH LIFE.—We are authorised to state that the engagement between the Marquess of Stanmere and Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row, is entirely well founded, and that his Lordship is at present engaged to the lady.

She read it three times. The first gave her the Marquess. The second gave her the Row. The third gave her the W, which had printed as it always did, with its hairline gap in the left foot, white on black, like a crack in a plate; and after that there was no use in reading further, because there is only one Miss W. Pargeter of Paternoster Row, and she was standing in her own press room with a heel of bread in her mouth.

"Is the cart loaded?"

"Aye."

"Is Sowerby on the box?"

"Aye," said Fairweather, in a tone that added, *And what is the matter with your face?*

Winifred counted, which is what she did. Eleven hundred sheets, and the cart in the court to take them. She had only to say the word to Sowerby and he would stop, being a man who stopped. Then the forme must be unlocked and mended: half an hour, if the quoins did not stick. Fresh paper must be wetted and left to take the water: an hour, whatever one did. The two presses must run again at four hundred an hour, which was nearly three; and the cart that should leave at a quarter past five would leave at nine, and come into Pomfret Yard when the hawkers were already in the streets with nothing to cry. By Clause 6 a cart that came after six was a breach, and a second breach in any quarter voided the contract at the Proprietor's option, which in practice meant Mr Underhill's; the first had been on the eleventh of May, in a rain that floated the Row, and the quarter had not yet ended. Mr Underhill, who had been dined three times since Lady Day by Mr Hector Lovejoy of the Phoenix Press, had been heard to remark on the eleventh of May at some length.

And if the cart went, there was Clause 9. Any error of the press requiring a correction in a later number made the contract void and forfeited the bond of five hundred pounds. So she might escape the one clause only by breaking the other, and the contract that kept the roof over the Drip, and the Albion, and her father, was in either case a thing she could lose. The difference was this. If she stopped the cart, the loss was certain and it was hers, a late cart with her name upon it. If she let it go, the loss was only possible: it wanted a gentleman to be angry, and an editor to be brave, and a correction to be printed by a paper that had never printed one. She was twenty-six. She had known editors.

What she thought in the next quarter of an hour she was afterwards to tell once, plainly, to the one person with a right to it. The narrator will wait.

She went out into the court with the sheet in her hand and her mother's stick knocking at her hip through the apron. The cart stood by the pump with its tilt up and the oilcloth roped down over the bundles, and the rain bounced on it. Sowerby sat on the box in his oilskin, a bent man with a face like an old saddle who had carried the Gazette for nineteen years behind a horse called Jehu, which had not driven furiously since the Peace of Amiens. Winifred walked across the cobbles and stood in the arch.

St Paul's struck the quarter. Two floors above her, in a room she could not see, her father's hand-bell began to ring, as it rang every morning at that hour, cheerfully and out of time, meaning tea, and what news of the Gazette. Sowerby clicked his tongue. Jehu leaned into the collar, and the cart came across the court and drew rein an arm's length from her, and the horse blew.

"Morning, miss," said Sowerby. "Wet. Is there anything the matter with the load?"

Winifred stood very still in the arch with the sheet in her hand, which is a thing compositors do well, standing being most of the trade, and she said nothing at all. The rain ran off the brim of Sowerby's hat. The bell rang on, upstairs, in the dark.

"Miss?"

She stepped back against the brick. The wheel went by her skirt with the breadth of a hand to spare, and the cart went out into Paternoster Row, and Sowerby looked back once from the corner of Ave Maria Lane, and then the tilt was gone into the rain.

She had good boots and did not use them.
