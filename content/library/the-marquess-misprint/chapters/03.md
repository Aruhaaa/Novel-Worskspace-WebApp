# Articles of Agreement

Mrs Hobday served breakfast as she served everything else, to music. She stood at the head of the long table in the basement kitchen at ten o'clock on Friday morning, which was the late breakfast of a house that would be at its frames again from six that evening until five the next day, with the bacon-fork in one hand and the hymn-book, which she did not need, open in the other, and she went round the company in the order of the verses: a rasher to Mr Orme at "Come, let us join our friends above," a rasher to Mr Fairweather at "And can it be," and to Toby, who was the youngest and visibly had the worst conscience, a rasher with an egg upon it and the whole of the fifth verse.

"Let me to thy bosom fly," she sang, and let Mr Pargeter's rasher fly to his plate. "While the nearer waters roll. And the waters are rolling, Mr Pargeter, in the front passage, from the same source as ever. The Lord is my shepherd. He is not, however, my plumber."

Barnaby Pargeter sat at the foot of the table with his gouty foot in its flannel upon a stool and his spectacles on his forehead, where they had been for two days, and said, "Quite so, Mrs Hobday," in the voice of a man who has not heard a word. He had been told on Thursday evening, in the parlour, with the door shut. Winifred said afterwards that he had taken it in three stages, as a man takes a tooth: a joke, then a silence, then a second helping. The joke had been, "My dear girl, I always said you would marry above the stone." The silence had lasted from nine o'clock until St Paul's struck ten, and in it he had looked at the ceiling, where the Drip came through. The second helping had been cold mutton, and he had eaten it all, and then he had taken her hand in both of his, which were ink to the wrists, and said, "Win. Whatever you did. I should have mended the roof," which was not an answer to anything, and was the kindest thing she had ever heard him say.

At ten past ten a boy from Pomfret Yard came down the area steps with a letter in a hand that sloped like a man leaning into a gale.

> My dear Pargeter,—Never in nineteen years has the *Gazette* been so asked for. We were sold out in the Strand by nine and I have been obliged to order six hundred more from your forme, which I trust is still standing; I have been stopped in Fleet Street by two bishops. As to Clause 9, which concerns errors of the press, I need hardly remind you that there has been none. The Marquess's household authorised the paragraph; the *Gazette* printed what it was authorised to print; and the *Gazette*, as you know, does not retract. It has only to be right. I shall wait upon you and Miss Pargeter, and meanwhile am, with the liveliest sense of the honour, E. Underhill.

"Six hundred," said Barnaby, after a pause. "My dear fellow, that is a second edition on a forme that has stood since Thursday. I should not like to say what the type thinks of it." He read the letter again and held it up to the light. "He has put 'the Marquess's household authorised the paragraph' and 'there has been none' in the same sentence and walked away from the collision. A very fine, safe man. He would find a comma in a coffin, and what a pity he has not the other thing, the thing for the sense."

"It means," said Winifred, "that so long as the engagement is not shown to be false, there is no error. And if it is shown to be false, there is."

"Well, there it is."

"There it is." She had a letter of her own in her apron pocket, folded in four and sealed with a wafer, which she had written at five in the morning and meant to send by Toby to Curzon Street at noon. It said what she had to say in nine lines. She was fond of it.

Toby came down the stair at a run, with a pie in his mouth and his paper hat over one ear. "Miss! It's bailiffs!"

"It is not."

"It is, miss, there's two, and they've brought their own umbrellas, and one's got a broken nose, and they're standing under the Drip!"

Winifred set down her cup. Bailiffs, in her experience, did not bring umbrellas; it was a point of their trade to be wetter than their prey. She went up the cellar stair with her sleeves still pushed above the elbow and her hand going, out of habit, to the rule in her hair.

In the composing room a tall man in a dark wet coat was standing with his head tilted back, looking at the roof. He had put his hat under his arm. Behind him, in the door, a second man, immaculate, held a second umbrella furled like a pike and consulted a watch. The tall one was stooped, as people are who read a great deal and have never been told to stop, and his nose had been broken at some time and set by someone in a hurry. He was following the line of the Drip upward, from the copper pan on the stone to the broken slate, with his eyes, and as she entered he said, without looking round, "Third slate from the ridge. Four inches by three."

"I am aware," said Winifred.

"You will want a plumber, and a man to lay on a ladder in the dry. There will not be any dry before October." He lowered his eyes, and they were pale grey and tired and, she thought, slightly appalled at themselves. "Miss Pargeter. I am Stanmere. I ought to have written. I came instead, which I see is worse. This is Mr Minton, who is mine."

"My lord. I wrote to you." She took the letter out of her pocket. "I had better say it instead. I made it. My father did not. He was in bed with his foot, and I was at the frame, and the error is mine to the last letter."

"Did you? Yes. I assumed so. I assumed it from the W." He took the letter she held out and, because it was there and she was watching, broke the wafer, and read it.

She had seen men read proofs for ten years. She knew the look of a man whose hand has begun to move before his mind has been consulted, and she saw the pencil come out of his coat as if on its own account, and a small mark appear in the margin of her best letter, against the first line.

"That comma," said the Marquess.

"I beg your pardon?"

"After 'Lordship.' 'I beg leave to inform your Lordship, that the paragraph.' It divides the verb from what is informed."

"It is a comma of respect."

"A comma is not a bow."

"My lord, a comma is a courtesy. It goes where a gentleman would bow. I was taught so at my mother's elbow, and she was the daughter of a compositor, and if she were alive she would bring it up with you personally."

"A semicolon is a courtesy," said the Marquess, strangely, and stopped. A look went over his face which she did not understand at all and which she was afterwards to understand perfectly. "I beg your pardon. It is a habit. I came to say I was sorry for your inconvenience, and I have begun by mending your punctuation."

"It is generally held in the Row to be the shortest way into anyone's good opinion."

"It is the only way into mine," said the Marquess; he said it as one mentions one's height. Behind him Mr Minton looked at his watch, and Toby, in the door to the stair, removed the pie from his mouth in order to hear better.

"Miss Pargeter," said Julian. "Who will be inconvenienced by a correction?"

"I?"

"You. Say how much. As a reader asks it. What does the *Gazette* do?"

She told him. She did not mean to tell him so much, but it was the first time since Wednesday that anyone had asked her a question that had an answer, and it went out of her like water from a cracked pan. Clause 9: any error of the press that required a correction in a later number voided the contract, and the bond of five hundred pounds was forfeit. The *Gazette* had never printed a correction in nineteen years. Mr Underhill kept the fact in front of him like a flag. The contract was three parts in five of the house's bread; it paid the journeymen, and the paper, and the lamp-oil, and the slate which was not mended.

"So that if I were to write tomorrow to say that I did not mean it—"

"You would be believed. And it would be a correction. And Mr Underhill would be obliged by his own pride to put Pargeter's out of Pomfret Yard before the ink was dry, in order that no one should say the *Gazette* had made a mistake." She stopped. "That is the sum. I do not mean it to move you. It is only the figures."

"And the cost of the error, to anyone? To me, for instance."

"A fortnight of embarrassment, I should think."

"A fortnight of peace," said the Marquess. He walked three steps down the room and three back, which in that room was a considerable promenade, and stopped at the imposing stone, where the Drip fell into its copper pan with a small flat note. "I have been hunted since I was twenty-four. This morning, I am told, the salver fell from forty-one to nine in a week, and that was on a rumour. A fact will clear it. I should have the summer to myself, for the first time since I came into the title, and I have something I wish very much to do with a summer. Where the cost of mending a mistake is greater than the cost of the mistake, a careful reader does not mend it. He writes in the margin *stet.* Let it stand."

"That is lying."

"That is letting stand. It is the difference between a falsehood and a silence."

"There is no difference in the Row."

"There is a very great difference in the *Gazette*, which is the only place that it is heard." He turned round. "I do not propose that you say anything. The *Gazette* has said it. I propose that neither of us contradicts it; until the end of the Season; and that the paper does the rest."

She was outraged. It lasted, by the clock of St Paul's, about nine seconds, and passed through three colours. Then she did what she always did, which was to count. She counted the bond, and the roof, and the six hundred copies standing in the type, and her father's foot. She counted the bailiff she had not yet had at the door. She counted herself, one square-shouldered woman in an apron, with her thumb stained, and a Marquess in her composing room offering her the use of him as one offers the use of an umbrella. "I do not tell lies aloud," she said at last. "It is a rule. I do not say it is a virtue. I say it is a rule, like not smoking over the type."

"Then you need not."

"Not the Season. Michaelmas. The twenty-ninth of September."

"Why Michaelmas?"

"It is a Quarter Day. Everything in the trade falls due at Michaelmas, my lord." Which was perfectly true, and not at all what she meant. "And at Michaelmas, I will have the sole right to end it. Not you. Me. You will be jilted."

"I," said the Marquess, with an expression of great interest, "shall be jilted."

"By a printer's daughter. I understand it is considered forgivable."

"I should like it in writing."

"My lord," said Winifred, "I am a printer. I do not make a bargain that I cannot set in type."

---

She set it standing at the case, without copy, as a man might talk to himself, and Julian stood at her elbow with his hands behind him and watched her hands.

He had not meant to watch them. He had meant to watch the type, in which, on a good day, a Marquess might have been expected to find something to correct. But the hands were doing a thing he had not seen done, and it held him as a juggler would have. They moved on the upper case and the lower case without her eyes going to either, and each letter went into the stick as though dropped by somebody who knew exactly where it would fall. They were a small woman's hands, square, quick, light as a bird's, with a blue-black stain on the ball and nail of the left thumb which was clearly permanent. The whole left hand held the brass stick against her apron. The stick was worn bright at the thumb-rest, and her thumb lay in the hollow of it as a head lies on a pillow.

These were the hands that had written, on nine sets of proofs, *A semicolon is not a courtesy.* He knew it as one knows the answer to a riddle, without a sense of having solved anything. It was not the smallest part of his wish to be gone that he could not for the life of him have said whether he wished it for her sake or his own.

"Article the first," said Winifred, to the stick, and set it. "'The announcement in the *Gazette* of the sixth of June to stand as printed, and neither party to contradict it until Michaelmas Day.' You are looking at my thumb."

"I am looking at your spacing."

"It is a very good thumb for spacing. Article two. You will call twice a week."

"Twice a week."

"At hours convenient to the press. And I shall appear with you on no fewer than six occasions. And at your table once." She did not look up. "I do not say it for pleasure. A thing nobody sees does not stand."

He dictated, after a while, where she let him. She would not allow *affection* and had no reason to give; she took out *lady* from his draft and put in *Miss Pargeter*, in a type that was larger. He proposed a clause by which a fund should be set up at Child's for the benefit of the house, and she took it out of the stick with a flick of one finger, and set the third article in its place in a dozen letters. "'The Marquess shall not lend, give, advance or promise any money to Miss Pargeter or to Mr Pargeter, directly or by any third person.' I will be paid for work. I will be paid for work at ordinary rates, as any customer pays. I will not be paid for being looked at."

"I see."

"You are going to tell me it is a point of pride."

"I was about to say that I admire the placing of the second comma." He had, in fact, been about to say a good deal else, and was astonished to find that it had left him, and that the article, as set, left no space in it for any other. It was a very good article. He signed away the right to be useful, in his head, and was aware of it as one is aware of a loose tooth: a small foreign matter of loss.

The fourth article was hers, and settled her conscience. "'Neither party shall be required to utter in their own voice any statement they know to be false; the *Gazette* shall be relied upon to speak for them.'" The fifth gave her the right to end it. The seventh limited the knowing of it to her father, to Mr Minton, who was standing by the door with his watch and the look of a man being measured for a rope, and to the Chapel, which was the whole of Pargeter's, by custom and oath: and she read it aloud to Mr Orme, who had come from his corner to see what the noise was.

"The Chapel keeps it, Mr Orme."

"Eh?"

"The Chapel keeps it!"

"The Chapel keeps everything, miss," said Orme, with dignity. "It is what it is for." Mr Fairweather, who had come in behind him with his arms folded across his chest like two bolsters, said "Aye."

It was the sixth that she set last, without a word, and with a sort of gravity.

"What is that for?" said Julian.

"It is a point of order. I have set eleven novels in three volumes for the circulating libraries, my lord. I know what becomes of people who make a bargain."

"What becomes of them?"

"The usual thing."

"Ah," said the Marquess. "That."

---

By noon the forme was locked and the Albion drew down its platen on a single sheet of foolscap, with a long sigh like a church organ with a cold; and the shop stood about it as a household stands about a christening. Winifred pulled the proof herself. She looked at it, up and down, the first thing Pargeter's had printed since Wednesday that any person had troubled to read, and she read it twice, once forwards and once backwards, with a pencil in her teeth, while the Marquess read it also, over her shoulder, with his own. They found nothing. It was an uncomfortable moment for them both, and in the silence the Drip said what it thought.

> ARTICLES OF AGREEMENT, made at Hand and Pen Court, Paternoster Row, on the seventh day of June, 1816, between the Most Noble Julian, Marquess of Stanmere, and Miss Winifred Pargeter, printer.

> I. The announcement in the *Gazette* of the 6th of June to stand as printed, and neither party to contradict it until Michaelmas Day, the 29th of September, 1816.

> II. Miss Pargeter to appear in company with the Marquess on not fewer than six occasions, and at his table once; the Marquess to call at Hand and Pen Court twice in every week, at hours convenient to the press.

> III. The Marquess shall not lend, give, advance or promise any money to Miss Pargeter or to Mr Pargeter, directly or by any third person.

> IV. Neither party shall be required to utter in their own voice any statement they know to be false; the *Gazette* shall be relied upon to speak for them.

> V. At Michaelmas Miss Pargeter shall have the sole right to end the engagement, and the Marquess shall bear all the blame.

> VI. Neither party shall form an Attachment of the Affections.

> VII. The truth shall be known to no persons beyond Mr Pargeter, Mr Minton, and the Chapel of Pargeter's.

> Printed by B. Pargeter, Hand and Pen Court, Paternoster Row.

At the foot she had set the imprint, as the law requires, and left the white space for two names. Julian saw that she had put her father's, *B. Pargeter*, and that she had not hesitated over the initial.

Barnaby was fetched. He came down the stair backwards, one step at a time, with his foot in its flannel and Toby under his arm for a crutch, and stood in the composing room blinking at the Marquess of Stanmere through spectacles that were still on his forehead. "My dear fellow," he said, "you will have to forgive the roof," and shook his hand with a hand that left a perfect print on the glove. He was shown the sheet. He read it with one finger. "'The Marquess shall not lend, give—' very proper, Win, very proper. 'Neither party shall be required to utter—' ha. 'Neither party shall form an Attachment of the—'" He read the sixth article aloud, in a thin voice like a clerk reading a will, and when he had reached the end of it he put the sheet down upon the stone, and sat down on the nearest stool, and wept. He wept with his whole body, from the spectacles down, and the Drip kept time. "Oh, Win," he said. "Oh, my dear. A *Marquess*."

"Father, you are to witness."

"I shall, I shall." He wiped his eyes with the flannel from his foot. "Where does he sign?"

The Marquess had taken up the pen. He held it, Barnaby remarked, as a bishop holds a hot potato; the thing wanted a firmer grip and, in his judgement, a little prayer. Julian looked at the sixth article. It lay on the damp paper in a plain Pica, among the sevens and the fives, among the rule of a gentleman's money and the rule of a lady's tongue, like a small stone in a good bread.

"I never fall in love," he said, "it is a family trait," and with a tolerable laugh he steadied the sheet with his left thumb, which had touched the forme an hour before, and signed. There were two counterparts, because a bargain wants a second copy as a man wants a second shoe; Fairweather pulled the second, in a single pull; and the second was signed also, and Winifred's name followed his, upright and small, with a flourish nowhere, like a notice to quit.

At the street door Julian turned. He had his hat and his umbrella, and Minton had the other, and Toby, who had been allowed to hold the door, said all at once, "Are you really going to marry our Win?" and the whole room stopped.

"I am assured," said the Marquess, "that it is in the *Gazette.*"

Winifred, at the stone, heard it. She looked at him, and she looked at the sheet, and something happened at the corner of her mouth. She was twenty-six years old, and had been engaged for twenty-nine hours, and she had just been handed the best sentence she had ever heard, and she took it, as a compositor takes a good sort that has fallen out of somebody else's case, and put it in her own.

When the door had shut and the umbrellas had gone out under the arch like two large black flowers, she went and lifted the second counterpart from the line where it had been hung to dry. It was her own, to keep. She meant to put it flat in the lid of her mother's brass stick-case, where it would not curl. On the margin, beside the sixth article, the Marquess of Stanmere's left thumb had left a perfect inky print, whorls and all, as a witness might leave one who had not been asked. A clean rag lay within reach. She did not take it.
