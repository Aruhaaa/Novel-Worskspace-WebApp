# The Crooked W

At seven in the morning on Friday the fourteenth of June, in a counting-room above the Phoenix Press in Ave Maria Lane, a very small man washed his hands. He washed them in yellow soap, in a basin that a boy had filled for the purpose and would empty and fill again, and it was the third time he had done so since rising; and when they were the pink of a boiled prawn and dry as paper he took up a magnifying glass on a ribbon, and went to the north window, and laid out the *Gazette*.

Mr Hector Lovejoy was forty-eight, and the neatest man in the Row, which he did not live in, being too exact to inhabit anything so crooked. He stood five feet two in his boots. He wore a coat the colour of snuff, with no snuff on it, and carried two pairs of spectacles on his forehead, one above the other, for no purpose anybody had discovered, since he read type with the glass. He believed nothing that he could not see, and nothing that he could see until it was enlarged.

There were three numbers of the *Morning Gazette* on the slope of the desk, set edge to edge: Tuesday the fourth, Thursday the sixth, Tuesday the eleventh. He had known since the sixth, at his breakfast, in the way a man knows who sees a face in a crowd and cannot yet say where; by Monday he had the where. He had gone to Hand and Pen Court yesterday to look at the faces. This morning he took the last step, which was to prove it, because Mr Lovejoy did not allow himself to know a thing that he could not lay out on a desk.

On the fourth, at the foot of the third column of the second page, stood three lines in a Long Primer that he had known for thirty years. FAIR COPYING in a plain hand, for authors, lawyers and composers of music; and beneath it, centred, *Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row.* On the eleventh, the same. On the sixth, between them, the advertisement stood with its name cut off, a gap of white at its foot exactly the depth of one line, the quads that had filled it shining faintly under the glass like the place where a tooth has been. And on the same page, at the head of the second column, under a lost spaniel, in a paragraph about a marquess, stood a line that had been somewhere else.

He put the glass to the W.

The Pargeter fount was a Long Primer that Barnaby had bought new in 1782, the year before a boy of fifteen named Hector Lovejoy had been bound to him. The capital W had been nicked in the left foot ever since, and it printed, as it had printed on every Pargeter handbill in the City, with a white hairline across the leg, as though the letter wore one boot thinner than the other. He saw it on the fourth. He saw it on the eleventh. And he saw it, black on grey, in the Marquess's paragraph, in *Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row*, with the same hairline, and the line itself spaced out as a centred line is spaced, with quads at either end, to fill a measure it had never been cut for. A secretary writing for a marquess does not give the bride's address as a trade imprint. A secretary does not give it at all. It was a printer's line, from a printer's own advertisement, and it lay in the wrong paragraph because it had been in a heap on the floor.

He did not need the Marquess's words. He had set a hundred denials, and a denial has the shape of a door: one knows where the hinges go. *Well founded* wanted to be *without foundation*; *engaged to the lady* wanted to be *engaged to no lady whatever*. The *Gazette* had printed the joints of a denial, out of order, with a name in the middle that had no business to be there.

Standing at the window with the glass in his hand, Mr Lovejoy saw the whole of it as clearly as if he had been in the room. He saw the rain come in at the broken slate that every printer in the Row knew about, and the slip blotted. He saw the *Gazette*'s reader abed with a quinsy (the apothecary lived in Ave Maria Lane, and had told his barber). He saw the rule against proofs after midnight. He saw a page on the floor, because a page does not wander; a page is dropped, by a boy; and two lines of one measure lying side by side in the pie, and a woman of twenty-six at midnight with her sleeves above the elbow and the forme to be locked by one, setting what her hands knew. It was a beautiful error. It had the grace of a thing that nobody could have done on purpose. He would have given a year's profit to have seen the page go down, and for the space of perhaps a minute Mr Lovejoy was almost sorry.

He had been Barnaby Pargeter's apprentice for seven years, from 1783 to 1790, and in all that time Barnaby had never once told him to wash his hands, and never once noticed that he did. It is possible to build a good deal on seven years of that. Mr Lovejoy rang for his clerk.

Mr Fogg was long, damp and agreeable, like a candle left out overnight. He had been eleven years in the counting-room, in which time he had learned that his master wished to be told only what was so, by Mr Fogg's own observation, and in the fewest words.

"We shall walk to Pomfret Yard, Mr Fogg. I have an advertisement to place."

"Yes, sir. Is it a new advertisement?"

"It is a new fount of Pica, Mr Fogg. It is an advertisement in every sense that I can honestly give the word."

* * *

Pomfret Yard lay six-tenths of a mile from the Row, down Ave Maria Lane and Ludgate Hill and into Fleet Street, and in the rain it cost Mr Lovejoy, Mr Fogg and one umbrella twenty minutes. In the course of them Mr Lovejoy said nothing whatever, and Mr Fogg, who understood that this was a good sign, said less.

The counting-room of the *Gazette* was long and low, with a stove at one end and high desks along both walls, and a smell of ink, tobacco and loyal sentiment; at the desks a dozen clerks sat entering sevenpences. Mr Lovejoy laid down four guineas for seven lines, to run a month, and counted them aloud into the clerk's palm, "One. Two. Three. Four," and the clerk, who had been robbed in his day by cleverer men, counted them again and found him exact. And from the inner room, with the sound of a vessel coming into dock, came Mr Ezekiel Underhill.

He was large and pink and fifty-five, with a speaking voice like the bass pipes of an organ, which he used on a clerk, a visitor and a coal-scuttle alike. He was blowing like a whale with pride. "Lovejoy! My dear sir! You find us in the full flood of success. You will have seen our number."

"I have it by me, Mr Underhill."

"Eleven hundred, sir, and six hundred more by Friday, and the Strand cleared by nine. I have been stopped in Fleet Street by two bishops. A marquess, a printer's daughter, and the *Gazette* the first to announce it! I give you my word, sir, there is not a woman between here and Tunbridge who has not asked me whether it is true. I mean to raise the run again at Michaelmas. I mean to double it."

"And is it true, Mr Underhill?"

"Is it—" Mr Underhill's face, which was pink, became pinker. "The Marquess's household authorised it, sir. The *Gazette* printed what it was authorised to print. I have always held that a newspaper cannot be answerable for the facts, only for the printing of them."

"I have heard it said," observed Mr Lovejoy gently, "that in nineteen years the *Gazette* has never found occasion to correct itself."

"Nineteen years and a month, sir." The pink deepened with pleasure. "The *Gazette* does not retract. It clarifies. Between ourselves, Lovejoy, Pargeter has two presses and a roof, and I have sometimes thought that a journal of our circulation might be better served by a house with, ah, a broader foundation. But he has this daughter, and now this marquess, and one cannot have everything. A very fine house, if the roof were mended."

"A very fine house," said Mr Lovejoy.

In the corner of the counting-room, on a hard chair beside the stove where no one sat and no one looked, an old woman in grey was reading the *Gazette* with a pencil. She wore a white cap. She had turned her better ear to the room, and she read as a farmer walks a field, line by line, from one hedge to the other, and now and then put a small mark in the margin and went on. Mr Lovejoy had known the proprietor of the *Morning Gazette* by sight for six years, and had never been introduced, because she was a person nobody introduced. He had once taken her for the stove.

"Does thee know the engagement is well founded, Ezekiel," said Mrs Rudge, not looking up, "or only that it has been printed?"

Mr Underhill bristled. It was a remarkable thing to see, in so pink a man, like a ham in a rage. "The Marquess authorised it, madam."

"A newspaper that cannot say it was wrong," said Mrs Rudge, making a small mark in the margin of the Births, "cannot be trusted when it says it is right."

"The *Gazette* has not been wrong, madam."

"I did not say it had. I said it could not say."

Mr Underhill discovered a pressing need to look at a ledger. The pencil moved down the column. And Mr Lovejoy, who had been watching the pencil with more attention than he had given the editor, understood in the space of one sentence what Mr Underhill had not understood in six years: that the sharpest instrument in that room was the one with the pencil. He had come that morning with the whole matter in his head, and a half-formed notion of laying it in Mr Underhill's hands as a dog lays down a bird. He saw now what would happen. Underhill, in terror of a correction, would bury it. And the old woman, who read with a pencil, would not let him; she would order the *Gazette* to say it was wrong, and Pargeter's would lose a paragraph's pride and keep a contract, and Mr Lovejoy would have bought a bird for nothing.

He decided to wait. A fruit is picked when it falls into the hand; and it falls the harder for being left to ripen on a high branch.

In the yard a bent man in an oilskin was washing the wheels of a cart with a besom, in company with a horse of great age and no illusions. Mr Lovejoy stopped under the arch, with the rain running off the umbrella on to Mr Fogg.

"Who is that, Mr Fogg?"

"Sowerby, sir. The *Gazette*'s carter. Nineteen years on the box."

"And he was in Hand and Pen Court at five o'clock on the morning of the sixth, with the cart, for the sheets?"

"He is there every *Gazette* morning, sir."

Mr Lovejoy regarded the back of the oilskin for the space of a breath. It had a face on the other side, he supposed, like an old saddle, and it had been standing, he supposed, within a few yards of a woman with a sheet in her hand. "I should like to know what he saw. You will find him in the Goose and Gridiron, in St Paul's Churchyard, when his round is done; he is a man of regular habits. You will buy him a quart. You will say that you are Mr Fogg, clerk to Hector Lovejoy, and that you have a thirst and a fellow-feeling for carters. If he asks who sent you, you will tell him. It is astonishing how rarely a man asks."

"And what am I to ask him, sir?"

"Nothing. You will talk about horses." He walked on, into Fleet Street. "A quart freely bought is the smallest debt in the world, Mr Fogg, and a man pays it in his own time. A fruit is better for being asked after before it falls."

* * *

The Phoenix Press was the long building in Ave Maria Lane with the handsome front: a bow window of a hundred and twelve small panes, each of which its master had seen cleaned, and a brass plate on the door polished every morning at six by a boy who had been told what would happen if he did not. Behind the counting-room were six presses, forty hands and a silence of the kind only achieved by a man who has never raised his voice and never needed to. Mr Lovejoy did not print a lie. He held it to be the whole of his respectability. He printed true things in the places where they did the most harm, and since the *Town Crier* had been three times threatened with the law and never once cast, it may be said that the system answered.

At four in the afternoon a boy of ten, a very ordinary boy who looked as if he had been told nothing, put into the porter's hand a packet sealed with a plain wafer, as he did every Friday, and went away. Mr Lovejoy took it upstairs himself.

It was the column called *The Quizzing-Glass*, by Peregrine Pry, and it came, as it always came, in a law-stationer's round hand, as flat and characterless as drawn lead, so that nothing of the writer survived the copying but the opinions. The boy fetched it from a hole in a wall in Cursitor Street and could have told you nothing but the wall. Mr Lovejoy had never asked who Pry was. He held that a man should find out a thing in the order in which it was useful.

He read it standing, with the glass. *Mr Pry presents his compliments to Mr Treacher, of St James's Street, whose Misprint has sold four hundred and eleven impressions at a shilling plain and two coloured, which is three hundred and sixty-two more than the Marquess of Stanmere has sold of himself. Mr Pry has nothing to add to the title.* It went on for a column. It was wicked and exact and unsigned, and it was extremely good. He found one fault, a comma after *impressions* that parted a verb from its object, and mended it with the pencil from behind his ear; it was his only liberty with Mr Pry, and he took it every week. Then in the margin, beside the last line, he wrote one word, and underlined it once.

*More.*

* * *

At four o'clock, in the basement kitchen of Hand and Pen Court, with an hour to spare before the copy, Winifred Pargeter sat at the end of the long table with the books of the house open before her. Her father sat opposite, with his foot on a stool and his spectacles on his forehead, and the Drip, which had followed them down in the form of a bucket, struck the bottom of it at her elbow with a small flat note.

There were the day-book, the ledger, the cash-box (eleven pounds four shillings and sevenpence) and three slips of stamped paper lying on top of the heap like a row of teeth. Each was in her father's loose hand and another's, neat as copperplate, in which the word *Lovejoy* had been written with a care that made it look like a monument.

"Three hundred pounds, the twenty-ninth of September, 1814," read Winifred. "Two hundred and forty, Lady Day, 1815. Two hundred, Michaelmas, 1815. Seven hundred and forty pounds at six per cent, falling due this Michaelmas, renewed to it in March as it was renewed the September before. And the clause." She read it with her finger under the line, as she would have read the proof of a man she distrusted. "'And in the event that the said Barnaby Pargeter shall cease, by whatever cause, to be the printer of the *Morning Gazette*, the whole sum shall become payable upon demand.' By whatever cause. He wrote that, Father, in his own hand."

"Yes, my dear. It is very clear. Hector was always clear."

"And the bond to the *Gazette* is five hundred pounds, forfeit upon a correction. And the cash-box holds eleven pounds four and sevenpence."

"Quite. Well, now, Win, about the bond—"

"The Lord is my shepherd," sang Mrs Hobday, descending the cellar stair with a tureen held before her like the Ark of the Covenant, "I shall not want. Pease. It is a Friday, and the Lord provides on a Friday in pulses." She ladled. "Cleft for me," she told the bowl, and went up again.

"Go on, Father."

"What? Oh. Yes. The bond." Barnaby took a spoonful of pease, and put it down. "It is not the money, Win, is it? I see that. It is that W."

"He said it to the room. He said it as another man would say good day."

"Hector never in his life said a thing that was not so. It is a great fault in a creditor." Barnaby shook his head. "He was the finest hand I ever trained. I never told him so. One doesn't."

"Father, in seven years, did you tell him anything?"

"I told him to mind the roof," said Barnaby, and looked up at the ceiling, where the Drip came through, and they both began, helplessly, to laugh.

It stopped, as it does. She looked down at the three slips, and pulled the ledger towards her. "The Society's circular is six pounds ten, when it is paid. There is the bishop. There is the Gazette. Father—"

"The thing is, Win, about the bond, that I have been meaning to—"

"Give us this day," said Mrs Hobday, reappearing at the foot of the stair with a loaf under her arm, "our daily bread." She set it down upon the ledger. "And forgive us our trespasses. It's the Wednesday's, but it has the virtue of age." She went up singing.

Winifred moved the bread. "You were saying."

"I was saying that— the bond, my dear, the bond is—" and the cellar door opened for the third time, and Mrs Hobday appeared with a second tureen, steaming, and wearing the expression of a woman who has been given a text.

"And the Lord said unto Peter, Rise, kill, and eat," said Mrs Hobday, in a spirit of exegesis. "And Peter said, Not so, Lord. And the Lord said, What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common. Acts, ten. It is the bone of the Marquess's ham, miss, with a turnip. I never was one to see a gift go to waste because of the donor's rank."

Winifred looked at the soup. She had ruled, on Thursday morning, that the Third Article said money; she could not in conscience now find that it spoke of soup. She took the spoon.

Barnaby ate. He was a man who had been given by Providence, three times in a quarter of an hour, a reason not to speak, and he took the hint. What he had been about to say, and had been about to say since the sixth of June, and indeed since the spring of 1812, was that the bond of five hundred pounds which Mr Underhill had required of him when the contract was signed had not been secured upon his word. It had been secured upon the house: upon the brick and the slates and the Drip, upon the table at which his daughter sat counting. He had signed it on a wet afternoon with a good pen and a light heart, because it had seemed to him a thing that would never be called. A correction had been unthinkable. The *Gazette* had not printed one in nineteen years.

At twenty minutes to five a boy came down the area steps in a coat with a phoenix on the button, and left a note, and would not wait. It was addressed in a hand so even it might have been engraved; Lovejoy wrote as he printed, and his letters had never in their lives been at odds. Barnaby took it. He read it twice, with the spectacles brought down to the place where they were meant to be, and passed it across the soup.

> Mr Pargeter,—My compliments on your daughter's engagement. I find I shall require the full sum at Michaelmas. H.L.

"Full," said Barnaby. "He has renewed twice. I was going to ask him at the quarter-day for— " He laid the paper on the table and set his hands flat on either side of it. "I did not know he was a man who could be cruel in so few words."

"He is not cruel," said Winifred. "He is exact. It is much worse."

She read it a third time. *My compliments on your daughter's engagement.* She had known since the sixth that someone would see it. It had never occurred to her that the someone would be so civil. A hundred and seven days: she had the figure before she had asked for it, as she always did. Ninety-one, with the Sundays out. Seven hundred and forty pounds was eight pounds two shillings of every working day of them, in a house that took, on a good day, three.

"Father," she said. "Do you know, I have been looking at this the wrong way."

"My dear?"

"I have been reading it as a thing done to us. It is not. It is a thing done in reply." She laid her thumb, her blue one, upon the word *engagement.* "Mr Lovejoy does not send a compliment that he cannot sell. He has told us in sixteen words that he knows what we are worth and what we are not. Very well. I am engaged to a marquess. It is in the *Gazette*. Every duchess in Mayfair has a dinner-list that wants setting, and a visiting-card, and a funeral ticket, and a notice of an at-home, and the Society for the Relief of Distressed Gentlewomen is only the first of them. I am to be seen on six occasions. I shall be seen on sixty. I shall leave a card." The colour had come back to her face, and with it something Barnaby had not seen in it since the sixth, and which he took, with misgiving, for appetite. "They want to look at a printer. Let them look at a printer. They shall pay for the privilege at ordinary rates."

"My dear girl, you cannot print a Season."

"Mr Orme has printed worse."

St Paul's struck five, through the rain, like a man with a cold. Upstairs the street door opened on the boy from Pomfret Yard with the copy, damp, in a clerk's fair hand, and she wiped her hands on her apron and went up to be a printer. Barnaby remained below with the pease, the bread, the Marquess's ham and the bond, and ate on; and it was to be a long time before he told her.

* * *

That night, a hundred yards apart, the two houses were engaged upon the same work, at the same hour, in the same fount, as like as two hands. In Hand and Pen Court Winifred Pargeter set the Fashionable Intelligence with the Drip at her elbow and no one to proof it. In Ave Maria Lane Mr Lovejoy set the *Crier* himself, as he did every Friday, because he trusted nobody else with the comma.

At one in the morning, with the forme locked and the presses begun and the Phoenix Press given over to the thump of the ink-rollers and forty men who did not speak, he went into the counting-room, and lit a candle, and drew up a stool to his own case. He took a W from the upper case of the Long Primer, from the same foundry as Barnaby's, sound as a bell. He laid the glass to it. With the point of a penknife, by the candle, on a scrap of waste, he nicked the left foot of it a hair's breadth, and pulled a trial, and nicked it again, three times, until it printed as the other did. It took eleven minutes, which was ten more than the matter was worth, and it cost him a W that had never done anyone any harm. In thirty years he had never put a flaw into a sort except by accident, and then he had thrown the sort away. He did this one perfectly.

He set a line. MISS W. PARGETER, in capitals, in the stick, with the spaces exact. He locked it into a little chase no bigger than a hymn-book, and inked it, and pulled a proof on the small press in the corner of the room, and held it to the candle. The glass found it at once: a thread of white across the foot of the W, the width of a hair, precisely where Barnaby's had it.

Mr Lovejoy looked at it for some time. Then he folded the proof small, once, and again, to the size of a wafer, and opened the drawer in which he kept the three notes of hand and the other things he intended to be paid for, and laid it on top. He locked the drawer, and washed his hands, and put out the candle. In the dark, under the paper, the little square kept its secret: a W with one boot thinner than the other.
