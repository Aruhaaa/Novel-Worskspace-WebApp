# Queries in the Margin

By the Saturday the letters addressed to *The Hon. Miss Pargeter* had filled the corner table in the composing room to a height at which they threatened to become a feature of the Court. There was no such title. Nobody had conferred it, and Winifred had not been consulted; but the Post Office, which sorts by what people hope rather than by what is the case, had decided overnight that the intended of a Marquess must be the daughter of at least a baron, and had written it on the covers in a hundred different hands.

Toby read them aloud. He sat on the stool beside the proofing press with a bun in one fist and the heap on his knee, in the voice he used for the Psalms on Sundays, which was the voice of a boy who has been told that this is how it is done.

"'Madam,'" read Toby. "'I take the liberty. The Reverend Josiah Pettifer, curate of nowhere in particular in the diocese of Lincoln, begs leave to offer his hand, his heart, and a living of ninety pounds in Rutland, the roof of which is entire.'"

"*Dnaltur*," said Orme, from the far corner, without turning round. He was seventy-one and slightly deaf, and set the line in his stick like a metronome with a grievance. "A dreadful word backwards. Like a sneeze."

"Here's another curate. That's three. This one's got a cottage, a cow and a Hebrew grammar." Toby turned it over. "He says the cow is amiable. Is that a good thing in a cow?"

"Go on," said Winifred, not looking up from the stone.

"'The Emperor of Tartary, Hoxton, has the honour to inform Miss Pargeter that he will receive her at any hour between nine and noon, and has instructed his Chancellor, who is his sister, to prepare a throne in the back parlour.' It's got a seal. It's a button." He put it down reverently. "There's a lady at Cheltenham, a Mrs Hepplethwaite, who wants to buy your wedding-gown in advance, miss, for eleven guineas, to wear to the Pump Room and give back clean. She asks what colour. There's a widow with six children and a cough, and a poet who wants a subscriber for *The Press, a Poem in Nine Books*, and a gentleman of Clerkenwell with a pamphlet on the Second Coming, who thinks the Marquess would like to be told the date."

"Put them in the box."

The box lay under the imposing stone and was labelled in Toby's hand *Hon.* Winifred answered none of them. She had worked out on the Wednesday that a person who replies to one begging letter will be obliged to reply to four hundred, and that a person who replies to none has only to be called hard-hearted by a small section of the Strand. There was, however, one letter that Toby read in his Psalm voice and did not finish, because Winifred crossed the room and took it out of his hand. It was from a milliner in Walworth, in a hand that fought every word, and it asked whether a girl of fourteen who could spell might be taken as a compositor's apprentice, since the writer had been told that a lady of the Row would understand. Winifred read it twice and put it in her apron pocket, where it stayed.

"There's the *Crier*, too," said Toby, with his mouth full. "I bought it off the hawker at the arch, miss. Twopence. I put it down as research."

"It is not research. It is a *Crier*."

"It's got a bit about the rout."

She did not say *give it to me*. She said, "Read it, then," in the voice of a person who would sooner have a tooth drawn, and went back to the stone; and the Chapel, which consisted that morning of Orme, who could not hear it, and Fairweather, who would not comment on it, and Brevier, who was asleep upon the cases, was spared nothing.

"'The Quizzing-Glass,'" read Toby. "'By Peregrine Pry. Mr Pry presents his compliments to the Waterloo Rout, which was held at Ormsgill House on Tuesday last in a manner worthy of the victory, that is to say, in a great noise and in the wet.'" He took a bite of bun. "'I beg your pardon, but is it quite usual for a lady about to become a Marchioness to wear in her hair a brass instrument for the measuring of lines? A gentleman of the press assures Mr Pry that it is called a rule. Mr Pry would like to know who is to be ruled.'"

"Very droll," said Winifred.

"It goes on. 'Her Grace of Ormsgill, who has been dispensing opinion from the same sofa since the Peace of Amiens, was observed to write upon a card and to show it to the young person in blue, whose accomplishments run to the reading of a thing the wrong way up. The card, Mr Pry is happy to report, concerned a Mrs C——, who was at that moment passing. "She has been a bore since Trafalgar," observed the Duchess, "and tonight she is a bore with a medal."'"

The stick in Winifred's hand did not move. She had been in the act of setting a letter, and she finished the act, and laid the sort in its place, and stood.

"A bore with a medal!" said Toby, who liked it very much. "That's good, that is. Who's Mrs C——? Is it the lady in the puce?"

"Toby."

"Only I should like to know, miss, because Mrs Hobday says—"

"Toby. Go and empty the pan."

He went, with the bun. Winifred stood at the case with her hands on the edge of it and did what she always did when a figure did not come out right: she went back and counted. The card had been written on the Duchess's knee, in a gold pencil, and shown to nobody. It had been read aloud once, in a murmur that went no further than the arm of the sofa; and the Duchess's remark had followed it at the Duchess's usual pitch, to which nobody in that saloon but the sofa had been attending, for the excellent reason that the sofa was where one attended. She ran down those who had been at that end. Lady Constance, with her trumpet. Miss Quarles, at the arm. Lord Rupert, at the other.

Three. She was seldom wrong in a count, and she was not wrong now, in her way: a person who holds a shawl is not, in anyone's arithmetic, sitting down.

She did not like where her mind rested. It rested, with a lightness that seemed to her very unfair, on a peach-coloured waistcoat and a long amused mouth. She told herself that the whole saloon had had a hundred opportunities of seeing the Duchess's pencil, that Lady Constance would sooner have swallowed her trumpet, that Miss Quarles kept books. The rest of the column was about Lady Quarles's faint, and was, she was obliged to admit, extremely funny; which made it worse.

* * *

Mr Ingram of the *Gazette* came at noon, wrapped in a muffler in the second half of June. He was a long, yellow, kindly man with the damaged voice of one lately recovered from a quinsy, and the habit of correcting any signboard he passed, which he did now, upon entering the Court, with a small dry cough. He carried the *Gazette*'s bound file under one arm and a note from Mr Underhill under the other.

"The gentleman in the Weald," he wheezed, "has asked that the nine letters be reprinted as a pamphlet. Three hundred copies, for private circulation, at his own charge, in Pica, upon a better paper than the *Gazette* ever sees. And he requests, as usual, Miss Pargeter's queries in the margin." He coughed. "He says he finds he has come to depend upon them."

"Does he?" said Winifred. "He has never said so before."

"He says a great many things to Mr Minton," said Mr Ingram, and looked, with rather more attention than was needed, at the ceiling.

It was at this moment that the Marquess of Stanmere came in at the door, in a dark coat, with his umbrella under his arm like a rifle and an air of having been sent for. "Article the Second," he said, to the room at large. "A Committee on the Poor Rate kept me on Thursday, and I am a call in arrears. I trust it is convenient." Mr Ingram's cough, which had been merely a cough, became in an instant an affair of the lungs, and lasted, remarkably, until Julian had crossed the room and taken up his station at the farther side of the imposing stone.

There he remained. It must be said for Winifred that she did not set him to any task; she supposed that a man who had worn an apron for the whole of an afternoon on the thirteenth had earned a rest. She opened the file at the first of the nine letters, which had been cut from the *Gazette* and pasted to leaves, and sat down at the stone with a pencil, and began to read as a reader does, with the end of the pencil lightly against her lip. Julian watched. He had a view of the whole sheet from the other side, upside down, and of the pencil as it moved, and of nothing else. He was a proofreader of the first water, but nobody had ever taught him to read a thing the wrong way up, and she, who had been taught it at six, wrote with her wrist curled round the page like a boy at an examination.

It is no light matter for an author to watch his prose being read by someone who has no idea whose it is. It went on for an hour and ten minutes. At each little scratch of the pencil he felt, with more precision than he would have thought possible, the exact place where a tooth had been put into a sentence he had been fond of; and at each long pause, when she drew the pencil from her lip and held it above the paper and put it back, he held his breath, and was ashamed of himself, and could not stop. At the seventh letter her pencil stopped altogether. She looked at the page. She wrote: *You say that a labourer's household eats a peck a week; your sum is for the quartern loaf. Which? Also, the commas.* She did not read it aloud. She tore the slip from the margin, folded it into one of the plain covers that Mr Ingram had brought, and sealed it down with a wafer; and Mr Ingram, who watched this with an expression of studied blankness, laid the file under his arm and went away to have his lungs seen to.

"I should like to meet him," said Winifred.

"Who?"

"The Farmer. Mr Ingram will say nothing. Nine months, my lord, and all he will tell me is *a gentleman in the Weald with a head for figures*, as if that narrowed it. I should like to meet him."

"I am told he is a bore in person." Julian turned the signet on his finger. "They generally are, the good ones. They spend it all on the paper."

"I do not wish to be entertained. I wish to make him say that he was wrong. Once." She put the cover on the stone, wafer uppermost. "I have queried him ninety-one times and he has conceded three. All of them commas. A man who has never been wrong in print is not a writer, my lord. He is a wall."

"A wall is much admired," said Julian, "by persons who want to lean on something."

"I wish to put a window in it."

"I think," said Julian with care, "that a man who has never been wrong may write better than one who has. He has no excuses ready."

"And no mercy."

"Mercy is the other thing a writer wants less than people suppose."

"It is the only thing I ever wanted from one." She regarded him over the stone. "You argue very well for a person who has never met him."

"I have a great respect for a wall," said Julian, "having been educated beside several," and the corner of Winifred's mouth did something it had not been told to, and he looked down at the stone and found himself deeply interested in a quoin.

* * *

Rupert came at half past four. Toby announced him from the door in a whisper that carried to the end of the Court: "A lord with flowers, miss, and he says he isn't a bailiff." He wore a coat of bottle green, a hat with a rakish dent, and a waistcoat that on any other man would have been an offence against the Peace; and he carried in his arms a damp packet of Covent Garden pinks wrapped in a page of the *Gazette*, which he laid on the stone with a bow.

"A bad bargain at fourpence the bunch," said Lord Rupert, "but I am told it is the thing. Miss Pargeter. Stanmere, how d'ye do. I have a pair of greys at the corner and a curricle, both belonging to a man who owes me two guineas, which makes them the only things in London that do. It is the fashionable hour and the Park is at your disposal. It will rain on us, of course. I thought you might care for a turn."

Winifred looked at the pinks. She looked at the Marquess, who was standing at the end of the stone with his hands behind his back, and whose face had assumed the expression of a man reading a lease. It occurred to her that the Articles wanted her seen, and that Hyde Park at five o'clock, even in a drizzle, was where one was seen; and that she had not been seen at all since the Tuesday.

"I should like it very much," she said.

"Quite," said Julian.

She knew the word. She had heard it in the cold of a yard on the thirteenth, and used it, once, in his own voice, to his face. "Is that yes?" said Winifred.

"It is the other thing."

"It is Article the Second, my lord. In company. I am to appear on not fewer than six occasions."

"I had not thought of Lord Rupert as company."

"He is a great many people," said Winifred, getting her bonnet from the nail. "I shall be quite safe in the crowd."

* * *

The curricle was an elegant, shabby affair with a hood that could be put up against the weather, and a tiger of nine years old in a striped waistcoat who clung behind with the stoicism of a limpet. The greys went at a decorous trot. Rotten Row in a drizzle at five o'clock that summer was a place of sparse and sodden grandeur, a dozen landaus and phaetons going round and round under dripping elms like exhibits in a small and expensive menagerie, and every head in every one of them turned.

"Now then," said Lord Rupert, leaning back with the reins slack in one hand. "I know, and you will admit nothing, and I find it the most convincing admission I have ever heard."

"I admit that it is raining."

"Excellent. We shall build on that." He tossed a guinea, caught it, and put it away. "My dear girl, I shall not trouble you with it again. I have said my say, and Stanmere is the best of men, and an ass, and I should not wish to be the person who explained to him that he is both. But I should like you to know what I am, because you will hear it elsewhere, and I had rather be the first with my own bad news. I am a debtor."

"I had understood that."

"I owe a great deal of money to a number of persons, and eight hundred pounds of it to a single man, who sends me a receipt for my own interest, spells *Tremayne* correctly, and has never once mentioned the matter. It is the only debt I have that frightens me, because he does not mind. He is a man who only ever does anything on purpose. I mean, of course, Lovejoy."

The wheel of the curricle passed through a puddle; the tiger said *Whoa* to nothing; Winifred did not move her face. "Mr Lovejoy of the Phoenix Press."

"The same. He prints the *Crier*, you know. I read it. I find it extremely well written. It gives me a pain." He laughed, lightly, as another man might have remarked upon the weather. "I tell you only because I seem to be the sort of person you tell things to. It is a quality I have never been paid for."

She looked at the side of his face, which was handsome and open and had a smear of rain along the cheekbone, and wondered whether she was being given a confession or a handkerchief to be dropped. He had been at the sofa. He owed the man who printed the paper. He had admitted both without being asked, and she had known him four days. She decided that she would not ask him anything. It is a great art, not asking, and she had been practising it all week upon the Marquess.

"Look," said Lord Rupert. "The Duchess's carriage. She will not bow, she never does, but she will see you have been seen. Wave your glove."

She waved the small glove, with the thumb in it, and the Duchess's yellow chariot rolled by without so much as a flicker of a purple turban, which was, in the language of the Park, a compliment of the first magnitude.

* * *

It was past seven when she returned to the Court, with the pinks damp in her arm. The pressroom door stood open on a smell of ink and warm iron and a thump she knew in her bones. The Society for the Relief of Distressed Gentlewomen having discovered that it had another fifteen hundred gentlewomen to distress, Lady Constance had ordered a second run, and in the back room, in a great grey light from the skylight, the Albion was at work. Fairweather stood at the bar. At the inking-table stood the Marquess of Stanmere, in his shirtsleeves, with his coat folded on a bench and ink to the elbow, wearing a leather apron that had been Barnaby's and an expression of terrible concentration, applying a roller to a forme as though it might bite.

"Aye," said Fairweather, which was the whole of his report.

Julian looked round. A smear of black lay across his forehead, and one of the white notches in his eyebrow showed through it like a star. "I came to see whether Fairweather needed a hand," he said, "and found that I did."

"Lord Rupert has been a very agreeable companion."

"He is a rake."

"He is a debtor." Winifred put down the pinks. "He tells me it is not the same thing, and that he would much prefer to be called a rake."

"I should think he would."

"He says he owes eight hundred pounds to Mr Lovejoy. He said it as another man would say it was damp."

"Did he?" Julian laid the roller down very carefully on the slab. For a moment he seemed to be considering the width of the forme, and the tympan, and the iron that held them both; and he said, "Quite," in a voice so level that it might have been rolled. Then he picked up his coat. "I must go. Minton will have a dinner on the table and a grievance with it. I shall call on Tuesday."

"It is Tuesday and Thursday, my lord."

"So it is."

He took the apron off, and hung it up, and nodded to Fairweather, who said *Aye* in a tone of the deepest approval; and at the door of the pressroom, as he went out, he glanced back at the imposing stone, where the plain cover she had sealed that morning lay waiting for Mr Ingram's boy, with its wafer uppermost, and said, absently, as one speaks to a man who is not in the room, "The quartern, not the peck."

He went out. The door swung. The Albion thumped.

Winifred stood where she was, with the damp pinks in her hand. She had not read the slip aloud to anybody. It was sealed; Mr Ingram had not opened it; the one other person who had been present was on the opposite side of a stone, and did not read upside down. It was a very small thing. Compositors, however, are trained to notice very small things.
