# Rupert in the Hop Garden

"I wished to say it in the most improbable setting I could find," said Lord Rupert Tremayne, "so that if it is received badly I may put it down to the scenery."

A hop garden in August ought to be a green cathedral: twelve feet of bine climbing every pole, the cones hanging like small pale lanterns, and the whole acre smelling of beer before there is any beer to smell of. The garden at Hope Farm, on the morning of Monday the fifth, was a field of poles from which a black wet rag was falling. The bine had rotted at the root and slid to the ground; the cones were brown and soft as old gloves; and down the alleys between the poles, bent double, in a line, moved a dozen Sussex women picking the mould from what survived with their fingers, for sixpence a day, and singing nothing.

Winifred had come up from the brewhouse in pattens, holding her skirt in her hand, having left her father in charge of the Old Lady, which had pulled two hundred Subscriptions since seven and was owed an hour's rest. Rupert stood at the end of an alley with his hands in the pockets of a coat that had been made to be seen in at Newmarket, and surveyed the hop garden as one surveys an account. He took a guinea from his waistcoat, tossed it, caught it, and put it away again without looking at it; and she understood from this that he was in earnest, since it was the first time she had known him to put the guinea away.

"I owe two thousand three hundred pounds," said Rupert. "I have it by heart; I say it in my sleep, like a collect. Eight hundred of it is to Mr Lovejoy, whom I dislike a great deal more than my tailor, and I dislike my tailor with a warmth that does me credit. My father the Earl has said he will pay no more. He said it at Christmas, and at Lady Day, and I took no notice, but this time he wrote it, which is how I know he means it. And there is Miss Quarles, whom I like a great deal too well to marry for her brewery. She would not have me for it in any case. I have no brewery."

"Lord Rupert—"

"I come to the point, you see, by the longest road I know. Marry me." He said it quite gently. "It is a perfectly sensible match. Lady Rupert Tremayne, with her press behind an earl's second son: not much, but a name, and a name is a very useful thing to put across a door. Stanmere need bear no blame, because you could not jilt him if you were engaged to somebody else; you would merely have been flighty, which is thought of a Season. I should leave you to your type and you would leave me to my debts, and we should be extremely civil at breakfast. I say it with the most perfect seriousness. I have said nothing lightly since Thursday and I find it exhausting."

Winifred looked at him. He was a long amused young man with russet-gold curls beaded with rain, and his mouth, which was made for laughing, was for once only straight. "Do you wish to marry me?"

"Not in the least," said Rupert. "I wish to be of use to somebody once before I am thirty, and you are the only person of my acquaintance who would put me to a good one."

"No," said Winifred.

She said it as gently as he had asked. "I am grateful. I will tell you why, since you have told me so much. I have been a convenient thing once. I was a paragraph, and I am being a decent one until Michaelmas. I will not be one twice."

"Because of Stanmere."

"Because of me." She did not look away. "You are a good sort of rascal."

"I am told it is the best sort," said Rupert, and he laughed outright, and she saw that he was delighted. "My dear girl, I have never been refused by anyone I liked. I find it extremely interesting. It is a great deal more interesting than being accepted by anyone else." He pulled a hop-bine from a pole, looked at it, and let it fall. "Now I have a thing to say that is less amusing, and I should be obliged if you did not make the face you made at the Duchess."

"Which face is that?"

"The one that says you have counted the sofa." He turned the guinea out again, and did not toss it, and held it. "Lovejoy called on me. At my lodgings in Albany, a week ago Friday, in a snuff-coloured coat, with his hat in his hand. He was entirely civil. He said that he had held my paper for some time without once troubling me for it, and that he was sure I should wish to be of use. He then asked, in the same voice, whether I had ever happened to see a paper. I said, which? He said, a paper, signed by two parties, in a printer's type. I said I could not think what he meant. He said he was sure I could not."

Winifred's skin went cold along the arms. She said, in a perfectly level voice, "And what did you tell him?"

"That I would think of it. It seemed the safest answer to a man who counts aloud." He gave her the full benefit of his long mouth. "I have thought. The answer is no, and I am telling you instead, which I imagine is the opposite of what he hoped for. I find that I am not a man who can be bought; it is extremely inconvenient. I had always meant to be."

"Why tell me?" It was the question a compositor puts to a proof that is too clean.

"Because he asked me to find it, and it did not appear to me that a man could find a thing in a woman's keeping and be of use to her at the same time. One has to choose." He shrugged, and the Newmarket coat shrugged with him. "I had meant never to choose anything. It has been the whole plan of my life, and it has worked extremely well until this week."

She had counted the sofa. She had counted it on the twenty-second of June, in the composing room, with Toby reading aloud; Constance, Verity, Rupert; and she had put Rupert at the bottom of the list, and the bottom of the list had moved. She stood in the hop garden with the rain on her face and found that she had stopped counting.

"You should burn it," said Rupert. "Whatever it is. I do not ask."

"A printer does not destroy a record." It came out sounding like an article of faith, and she heard it. "It is very nearly the only one we have."

"Then lock it up." He put the guinea in his pocket. "Lock it very well."

"Rupert."

The voice came from the far end of the alley, and was not raised, and Winifred knew whose it was before she turned. The Marquess of Stanmere had come up from the barn with a bundle of Wragg's accounts under his arm, in boots to the knee in mud, and he had been standing at the end of the alley, it was clear, for a little longer than was strictly polite. There was no misunderstanding. A good many persons in this book will misapprehend each other before Michaelmas, but the Marquess had seen what he had seen, a man in a good coat saying something serious to a woman in the rain, and he did not pretend to have seen anything else.

"Rupert, are you asking Miss Pargeter to marry you?"

"I was."

"Did she accept?"

"She said I was a good sort of rascal."

"Quite," said Julian.

They had known each other for twenty years, since the day a boy with a broken nose had given a boy with russet curls the use of a Greek lexicon for a pillow, and their quarrel took the form of two men conducting a debate of great courtesy in a hop garden, up to the ankle, while twelve Sussex women pretended to be deaf. Julian said that he might at least have been told. Rupert said that to tell him would have been to have it arranged. Julian said that he would not have arranged it. Rupert said that he would have had a dinner for sixteen and a settlement drawn by Thursday, and Julian, who could not honestly deny it, said that was not the point.

"What is the point?"

"The point," said the Marquess, "is that I would be obliged if you would go and drown yourself in the river."

"It is already in the river," said Rupert, with a bow. "It is the whole county. I have no doubt that I shall find it presently." He lifted his hat to Winifred, with the rain running off the brim, and walked away down the alley with his long loose step, and did not, she noticed, take out his guinea.

They were left standing among the poles. For a time neither of them said anything at all. He shifted the accounts from one arm to the other.

"You were not jealous," said Winifred.

"I was extremely jealous. I find it very ordinary, like toothache." He looked at the mud on his boots. "I should have liked to hit him, and I should have missed. He has been proposing to people since Harrow and nobody has taken him seriously, which has been his misfortune and also his protection. I think he meant it. I think it was the most honest thing I ever saw him do."

"He said he wished to be of use."

"He wishes to be of use to everybody. It is the only occupation he has ever followed." He did not look up. "Did you want to say yes?"

"No."

"Good," said Julian. And then, in the tone in which he would have read out a column of figures: "Fifty-five days."

"Fifty-five?"

"To Michaelmas. I counted."

She had counted it herself that morning, between the Old Lady's first pull and her second, and she did not say so. She did not answer at all. She did not need to.

She might have told him then what Rupert had said about the paper. She had the sentence ready, in order, like a line set and justified; and she saw quite clearly what would follow, which was that he would have it arranged by dinner. He stood in the rain with Wragg's accounts under his arm so plainly arranging nothing that she let the sentence go back into the case, to be thought of afterwards at some length. He nodded, as Pankhurst had nodded in the barn, twice, as at a price that was fair if disagreeable, and went away up the alley with the accounts, and she found that she was holding a hop-bine she did not remember picking up. It was the black rotten kind. She put it down.

Far across the garden, past the last of the poles, two figures could be seen at the top of the slope: one in a brown dress, with a ledger under her arm and her spectacles flashing on their chain, and one in a coat made for Newmarket. The brown dress was telling the coat something, with her free hand, in a series of short flat movements like a person laying out a hand of cards. It appeared to be the price of something. The coat was bending to listen with its head on one side, and the distance did not conceal that it was delighted. Whatever they said, they were quarrelling, and happily; and Winifred, who had been refused no one and asked a good deal, stood among the dead hops and watched them with a feeling for which she had no name in any type.

* * *

That night, when the house was asleep, she sat on the edge of the bed in the room they had given her, with the green baize lid of her mother's stick-case open on her knee and a candle on the washstand.

Her mother's brass composing stick lay in its slot, worn bright at the thumb-rest. The lid had a lining of green baize, and the baize had a flap, and under the flap, flat against the wood, lay the second counterpart of the Articles of Agreement: foolscap, one fold, seven paragraphs in Pica, an imprint at the foot, and beside the sixth a smudge in the shape of a man's left thumb. She had taken it out, on Saturday, to look at that smudge. She had put it back on Sunday morning, from the table where she had found it, a little to one side of where she had left it, and had thought nothing about the side.

A printer does not destroy a record. She had said it to Rupert as a principle, and it had been one; but by bedtime it had turned into something else, which she would not have cared to look at in a good light. It was that she could not have burned this sheet if the house were afire. She was in the habit of the *dele*. She had cut her way out of every difficulty of her life with a stroke of the pen, and put the mark in the margin, and gone on, and been called quick. It had not before occurred to her that there might be a piece of paper she did not wish to be quick about.

She smoothed the foolscap flat. She laid the baize flap down over it, and pressed it, and turned the little brass key in the lock that her mother had had put on the case for the Psalms; and she hung the key on its ribbon about her neck, under her night-gown, where it lay cold for a moment against her breastbone, and then warm.

It was, as the narrator is bound to admit, two nights too late.
