# Cards on the Salver

There is a way of carrying a silver salver that a butler learns at about thirty and does not afterwards forget: level, a little forward of the body, at the height of the third waistcoat button, with the wrists loose, as a man carries a sleeping child or a bad piece of news. Gantry, who was sixty and had carried both, bore the salver across the breakfast-room at Stanmere House at eight o'clock on the morning of Thursday the sixth of June, and set it down at his master's left hand, and the cards slid on the silver in a long soft drift, like a trick performed by a conjuror who has been told that the audience is leaving.

"Yesterday's, my lord," said Gantry. "With the letters."

"How many?"

"Nine, my lord. Of which three are letters." He gave the figure in the voice of a man reporting the height of a river.

The Marquess of Stanmere did not look up. He was thirty-one, six feet one in his stockings and nearer six in the mornings, since he did most of his reading at a slant. He had sandy hair cut shorter than any hair in Mayfair, pale grey eyes, long hands spotted with ink, a nose that had been broken at Harrow in a dispute over the aorist of a Greek verb (he had been right, which was the whole trouble), and a small white notch in his left eyebrow where a cricket ball had once taken the other side. He had been up since five. Since five he had eaten a cold mutton chop, drunk two cups of tea he had not tasted, and worked through the Corn Returns for the Home Counties; and since seven, when the *Morning Gazette* came up from Pomfret Yard in the hand of a boy who had run the whole way and looked it, he had been reading that, with a pencil, because he could not be left alone with a printed page without improving it. He could not pass a tradesman's signboard in Bond Street without correcting it under his breath, and the tradesmen of Bond Street, who had heard him do it, regarded him as a kind of weather. By eight he had mended a comma in the Births, a *recieve* in an advertisement from Bath, and the *Gazette*'s opinion of the Bey of Tunis. He was now on the second page.

He found the paragraph at the head of the second column, under a lost spaniel, and the narrator is in a position to say that for the first ten seconds it was a joke.

It was precisely the joke a newspaper makes when it does not know it is making one. *His Lordship is at present engaged to the lady.* It had the cadence of wit, and he was halfway into that cold pleasure which an exact man takes in a beautiful mistake when his eye reached the name in the middle of it, and for the next ten seconds it was not a joke at all.

*Miss W. Pargeter, of Paternoster Row.* He had read the word Pargeter at the foot of every proof for nine months. *Printed by B. Pargeter, Hand and Pen Court*: it was the imprint of the house that set A Kentish Farmer.

The reader ought now to be let into a secret that the Marquess had kept, since the fourteenth of September last, from the whole of London but one anxious man. At intervals of about a month there had appeared in the *Gazette* nine long letters upon the Corn Law, the poor-rate and the price of the labourer's bread, and they were signed *A Kentish Farmer*, and nobody had found the Farmer out, though three noblemen had denounced him at dinner. His manuscripts went in a plain cover, carried by Mr Minton, to Mr Ingram the reader, who passed them to the Row; and the proofs came back by the same road, with the Farmer's sentences corrected in a hand that was small, upright and entirely without respect, and initialled in the margin with two letters, *W.P.* He had supposed W.P. to be a man of sixty with a cold in the head.

He put his hand into his coat and found the pocket-book without looking, as one finds a tooth. It was small and bound in green morocco, and it held, cut from the margins of nine sets of proofs with a pair of nail-scissors (Minton, holding the lamp, had winced at every snip), some ninety queries, each pasted on its own leaf and dated in the Marquess's hand. He opened it at random. *You use "poor-rate" and "poor rates" within a page. I do not say which is right. I say choose. W.P.* He turned a leaf. *Delete "deplorable". Your figures are deplorable enough without help. W.P.* He turned another. *A semicolon is not a courtesy. W.P.*

He sat for some time with the pocket-book in one hand and the *Gazette* in the other, a man holding two kinds of evidence, until Gantry, who had not left the room and who knew from the set of his master's shoulders that something was the matter, as a ship's cat knows weather, said, "Mr Minton, my lord," and Mr Minton came in.

Mr Ambrose Minton entered as a man enters a church in which he has dropped something. He was thirty-five and immaculate and grey about the mouth, with a pocket-watch already open in his left hand and, on the first finger of his right, a stain of ink that no pumice had ever shifted. He carried the *Gazette* at arm's length, folded to the page, like a dead bird he had found on the step and could not decide whether to bury.

"My lord. I came the moment I saw it. I wish to say at once that I wrote, and your Lordship will recall that I wrote, 'entirely without foundation.'"

"I recall it very well. It was a good sentence. I particularly liked 'no lady whatever.'"

"It has come out, my lord, as—"

"As the exact opposite. Yes. There is a certain symmetry in it." Julian turned his own copy upon the cloth and laid one long finger beside the offending line, as a man shows a bird in a hedge to someone with poor sight. "Mr Minton, I have been engaged for a little over an hour, and I find I am not good at it."

Minton looked at his watch. It was four minutes past eight. He looked at it every four minutes, as other men breathe, and had never been known to be late or at ease. "I sent it straight to the Row, my lord. I did not take it by Pomfret Yard. Your Lordship was altering the last sentence until a quarter to four, and the counting-room shuts at four, and it seemed a saving of time. If I had taken it to Mr Underhill's people, a clerk would have read it over, and I have been thinking since half past six that a clerk would have—"

"Copied it. Clerks copy. You were prompt, Minton. The division of labour in London is that the secretary is prompt and the printer is accurate, and it appears that on Wednesday night one of us kept to his side."

"I shall write to Mr Underhill this minute demanding a correction in Saturday's number. I have the first line. 'Sir, the Marquess of Stanmere, having observed.' I had got as far as 'observed.' I thought I should be guided by your Lordship as to whether it ought to run 'regrets' or 'requires.'"

"In a moment," said the Marquess.

Minton looked at his watch.

Lady Constance Dyer was announced in the sense that Gantry's mouth had begun to open when her voice came through it. "I know he is at breakfast. I can see he is at breakfast. Do not take the dog." She was sixty-four, tall and rigid in black bombazine with a purple ribbon at the throat, the widow of a general and the aunt of the house, and she carried a lorgnette on a chain, a tortoiseshell ear-trumpet in her reticule, and under her left arm a pug called Blücher, who had one eye, a flat opinion of everybody, and the look of a small old banker who has lent money to the wrong party. She did not need the trumpet. She had the hearing of a bat. She used it as other women use a fan, to put between herself and whatever she preferred not to be told, and she raised it now towards Minton, who had opened his mouth, and said "Eh?" in a tone that shut it.

"Who," said Lady Constance, "is Miss W. Pargeter?"

"Good morning, Aunt Constance."

"It is not a good morning. It is raining, and you are engaged. Who is she?"

"I am informed that she is of Paternoster Row."

"That is an address. I asked for a person." She let fall the lorgnette. "Count the salver, Gantry."

"My lady, I have—"

"Count it again."

Gantry counted it again, with his lips, and arrived at nine.

"Forty-one," said Lady Constance, "on the Monday before Augusta Quarles began to talk. Nine yesterday, of which three are letters, which is to say three women who wish it known they had meant to call. It is a question of arithmetic. That was a rumour, Julian. Consider what a fact will do."

"Which fact?"

"The one under your hand."

A footman, who had been waiting for his turn at the door with the face of a man with a hot plate, came forward with a note on a second salver. It had been brought from Bankside, Gantry explained, by a brewery boy on a dray-horse; a very small boy, he said, on a very large horse, which was standing at the area railings and eating the geraniums. The seal was a plain red wafer. The hand was square and upright and without a flourish, the hand of a young woman who kept books, and it said: *Thank you. V.Q.*

"For what?" said Julian.

"For the first morning in a twelvemonth," said Lady Constance, "on which her mother has not said your name over the toast. She is the only sensible Quarles. It is a pity about the brewery." She set the pug upon a chair, where it regarded the Marquess with its single eye as it might have regarded a debtor.

It was at this point that Lord Rupert Tremayne strolled in, laughing, which was how he did most things, in a waistcoat of lemon silk that was slightly too beautiful for the hour. He was twenty-nine and lanky and russet-gold, with a long amused mouth, the *Gazette* folded to the page in one hand and in the other a guinea, which he tossed and caught, tossed and caught, as he had done to the knowledge of Mayfair since Lady Day without once being seen to spend it.

"My dear Stanmere. I came the moment I read it, which was the moment my man brought my chocolate, which was a quarter of an hour ago, and I have run the whole way. I met my tailor in Bond Street, and he bowed. A tailor who bows is a tailor about to sue, so I came round by the mews. Is she handsome?"

"Which?"

"The lady. Miss—" He consulted the page. "Pargeter. Is she handsome?"

"I have no idea."

"Is she rich?"

"She is a printer, or her father is."

"Is it true?" Rupert tossed the guinea. "I only ask so that I may know which of the three to congratulate you on."

"I have not met her," said Julian, and heard how it sounded, and laid his pencil down upon the cloth very precisely, parallel to his knife. "I have been engaged since seven o'clock this morning to a lady I have not met, cannot describe and can tell you nothing of except her trade, and I had it from the *Gazette* at sevenpence, like everyone else."

"But, my dear fellow, it is the most romantic thing I ever heard. Lady Jersey will weep. Augusta Quarles will—" Rupert's face lit like a lamp. "Has anyone told Augusta Quarles?"

"Only the *Gazette*."

"Which is delivered in Brook Street at eight. She will have had it with her toast. Poor Verity. Poor Augusta. Well, you will write and put it right, of course. One does. 'Sir, I observe—'"

"I have the first line," said Minton, from the corner.

"Minton," said the Marquess.

"My lord?"

"In a moment."

Lady Constance had said nothing for some while. She now raised the lorgnette and looked at her nephew through it, a thing which at close range was like being measured for a coffin by a woman who had views on the timber, and she went on looking for a full minute, which is a long time to be looked at through a lorgnette. In that minute she worked out the whole matter. She had known him thirty-one years and been wrong about him twice, both times agreeably. She saw that he had not known the girl; that the *Gazette* had blundered; that he, who ought at this moment to be dictating, was sitting with his pencil parallel to his knife, and was turning his ring. It was a heavy gold signet with the Stanmere griffin on it, and he turned it round and round his finger when he was thinking, and never knew that he did.

"You will not correct it," said Lady Constance.

"I have not said I would."

"No. You have been not saying it for ten minutes. It is how your father began."

"Began what?" said Rupert.

"Eh?" said Lady Constance, and put the trumpet to him.

The cards lay on the salver. Minton's watch ticked, with a sound of small dry insects. Blücher, on his chair, sighed through his flat face like a bellows with a hole in it.

"In ten years," said Lady Constance, to nobody in particular and to the ceiling in general, "nothing has made Augusta Quarles leave a house with her hat crooked. I have sat in a room with that woman while the roof came in at Lady Jersey's, in the year seven, and she went out by the door with the hat as she had put it on. I should like to see what this does."

"You would like to see."

"I said I should like to see it. I did not say I should arrange it. Arrangement is for people who cannot wait." She gathered the dog. "I shall be at Hertford Street. I do not ask where you will be. I inform you that I am at home to nobody until I have thought, and that I think quickly, and that the name of the thing I am thinking about is Pargeter. Rupert, you will take me to my carriage and you will not tell me about your tailor. Come."

"My dear Constance, I never tell anybody about my tailor. He tells them about me." Rupert gave the Marquess a look of such pure delight that it was impossible to resent, and bowed himself out after the aunt with his guinea going up and down behind his back like a very small sun.

The door closed. The footman withdrew with the second salver. Gantry took up the first, and the cards slid on it with the sound of a pack being put away, and he went out also, with his face in perfect order.

"My lord," said Minton, after some time. "The first line."

Julian drew the *Gazette* to him. He found the paragraph, and under the words *entirely well founded* he laid his pencil flat across the page, like a bar across a gate. It was a good H pencil, sharpened with a penknife at five that morning. A single stroke in the margin, the proof-reader's *dele*, with its curl like a pig's tail, would have cancelled the whole line, and he had made that mark ten thousand times upon the work of other men without a qualm. The pencil lay where it was. Minton looked at his watch, and looked at it again, and found that four more minutes had gone by in which his employer had neither written nor spoken nor moved, except his thumb, which turned the ring.

It was not a decision. The Marquess of Stanmere had not decided to let the thing stand. He had only decided to look at it, which, as every printer knows, is how a great many things come to stand.

"Minton," he said, "I should like to know everything there is to know about the firm of Pargeter."

"The printers, my lord?"

"The printers."
