# Oxen Named for Officials

Forty wagons stood in the Salt Company yard in three ragged rows, and a very tall woman was reading their oxen aloud to them.

"Registrar. Deputy Registrar. Auditor, Assistant Auditor, and that one's Auditor's Cousin, he's only on loan. Guildmaster, who stands in the middle of the road and makes everyone go round him. Quartermaster, Treasurer, the Speaker." She had a voice that crossed a yard without being raised, and a long salt-hook slung across her back like a bishop's crook. She was six foot two, with a shaved head and a red scarf wound three times round her neck, and when she grinned a front tooth was chipped in a small neat triangle. "Rule Forty. He sleeps all day, he's against the wheel. And that one," she said, pointing the hook, "is Mihkel."

Reet looked. The ox was dun-coloured, deep in the body and heavy in the shoulder, with a pale belly like a mustard-yellow waistcoat.

She made a small noise, and put her hand over her mouth.

"You know him?" said the woman.

"I know someone very like him."

"Everybody does." She looked down at Reet with sharp, amused, assessing eyes, and then past her at Toomas, who had been dried by a barge-woman's stove and was still faintly green below the knee, and at Taavi, who clinked. "Aino Rebane. Guard. I've thirty-six oxen and forty wagons, and I'll tell you now the arithmetic is a matter of faith. Four of them tow, like ducklings. You'll want to be pilgrims."

"We are pilgrims," said Toomas.

"To?"

"The Bridge at Palu. The Name-Day feast. We are going to tie a ribbon."

"Nobody goes on pilgrimage to a bridge." Aino folded her arms. "It's six days of mud for a rope. The Company doesn't care what you are. I care whether your boots are clean, because a pilgrim has dirty knees from praying, and yours are spotless."

"We pray standing," said Toomas.

She looked at him for a good five seconds. "That's the worst lie I've heard this month," she said, "and I've been in the Salt trade since I was fifteen. Nine marks for the three of you, and your own bread, and you walk when I say walk. I don't want to know why."

"Six."

"Nine. The sick one's cheaper."

"I'm not sick," said Taavi. "Technically I'm only apprehensive."

"Eight, then," said Aino, "and I'll take the funny one at half." And she stuck out a hand like a spade, and Toomas shook it, and counted eight silver marks into it from a purse that was clearly not going to survive the journey. When he offered to bind the Company's ledgers for life as a supplement, she looked at him as if he had offered her a goat.

It was after the third afternoon bell when the yard began to move. The master of the train, Hendrik Loo, a man who wore his hat indoors and out and had not been seen without it since the spring, walked down the line shouting names at oxen. The oxen ignored him. Registrar leaned into his yoke with an air of pained dignity, and the wagon in front of Reet shuddered, creaked and began to roll. She walked beside it with her satchel, her chain over her shoulder and the tracing against her ribs, and did not look back at the Hollow, because she could not stand to see how little there was to see. A dark smudge of rim and a thread of smoke. Lamp-boys. Mihkel, on his stool, with a bun.

The wagons made a noise like a village moving house. Axles shrieked, harness chimed, and under it all went the slow wet tread of thirty-six pairs of hooves on the frozen ruts, steady as a clock. Everything smelled of salt: the sacks, the planks, the oxen's flanks, the very air, so that every breath was faintly a preservative. Salt was the one cargo that did not mind where it woke up. A bale of wool could be ruined by a night in the wrong field, a cask of ink by a tilt of the road, but a block of salt weighed what it weighed in any country, and the Company had built a hundred years of trade on that dull fact. Reet, walking in the ruts with her chain clinking, thought it the most reassuring thing she had heard all week.

Aino fell in beside her at the third mile, with the hook over one shoulder.

"Right, rider," she said. "You're a Guild girl. I can see the ink. What's at the Three Pines?"

"Three pines," said Reet.

"Ha. Very good. What *else*?"

"A mile and a half south of the edge. One of the three was burned by lightning in the autumn of GY 308 and is black on its west side. The ford beyond it was shin-deep at the last Sheet. There's a stone with a K cut into it at the north end that marks the Kesk boundary, and a dead heron in the second bend of the stream, or there was a month ago."

"Is the heron still there?"

"I don't know. It was a month ago."

"Hm." Aino walked in silence for a while. "Kesk's well."

"Eleven feet to the water. Stone-lined. A cracked lip on the east side."

"What colour's the handle?"

"There isn't a handle. It's a rope and a bucket. The bucket's tin with a blue rim."

"You're guessing."

"I don't guess."

"Then you're mad," said Aino, "or useful," and she looked, to Reet's surprise, quite put out. "I hate that. I was hoping you'd be useless, and I could be rude to you till Palu."

"Both are possible."

"Don't be clever. It makes it worse." But she was almost smiling, and the chipped tooth showed. Reet found, with an odd sideways drop of the stomach, that she wanted to go on being useful to her, and that this was a thing she had not wanted from anyone in four years.

They reached the Seam's edge at sunset.

It was not a line. People imagined it as a line, a hedge, a river, and it was nothing of the kind; it was a thickening. Three miles from the Wharf Gate, where the Seam bellied north in a long belly that was a mile wider every year, the road ran on across heath exactly as it had before, and the heath looked exactly as it had, and the sky went tight. Reet could not have said how. It was as if the whole dome had been drawn up over a mast, and the air strained against it like canvas in a rising wind, with every sound she made coming back to her half a breath late. The light turned long and brass-coloured. The shadows of the wagons ran sideways, off the road, into the heather. The oxen put their ears forward, all thirty-six, and went on walking.

"Hold your hat," said Aino, who wasn't wearing one.

Nothing happened. That was the thing about the edge. Nothing happened, and you were in it.

They made camp in the first dusk, in a ring: forty wagons, thirty-six oxen, and a stew. The oxen were staked on long tethers outside the circle, each to an iron stake driven in with a mallet, and Aino went round them with a lantern, patting each flank and giving it a title. "Evening, Treasurer. Don't look at me like that, you know what you did."

Across the dark, a hundred yards off, another lantern swung. A voice came with it.

"Hoy the Salt! Who's your north?"

"We are!" Aino bellowed back.

"No, who's your *northmost wagon*?"

"We are, you ass, we're all in a ring! You're our north!"

"We're not north, we're east!"

"You're a wool train. You don't *have* an east!"

"We've an east and a west and a lost cousin! Who's your Guildmaster?"

"He's the one in the middle of the road!"

"We've one of those!"

"That'll be ours! Give him back!"

This went on for some while, to the great pleasure of the camp. It was stake-and-ask, the custom of the road: a caravan staked its oxen at dusk, then asked its neighbour where it was, because after the Shift nobody would be sure. They were the Mägi wool train, said the voice at last, thirty wagons of fleece, and they were stationed, so far as anyone could tell, to the left of the Salt Company. "Which left?" shouted Aino. "The left that's *ours*," came the answer, with dignity; and Reet, sitting with her back against a wheel, found she was laughing, silently, for the first time in two days.

Aino told the story of the wagon that woke in a barn, over the stew.

"Real barn," she said. "Hay, a cow, a farmer. This was a wagon-master I knew, Joosep, a decent man, and he'd gone to sleep on the Flats in the middle of nothing, and when he woke there was a roof. The cow was extremely upset. So was the farmer, who wanted rent for the night." She dug a spoon into the pot. "Joosep paid. A bale of salt. He said it was the only time he'd ever got a better night's sleep than he'd expected."

"Where was the barn?" said Reet.

"Where it had been. They'd gone to it." Aino laughed through her nose. "That's the Seam. It doesn't pick you up. It sets you down." She stood, brushed off her knees, and picked up from beside the wheel a wicker basket with a cloth over it. She carried it the way another woman might carry an infant. "Hens," she said. "I keep six. Best layers in the Seam. They sulk if I'm late."

"They're very quiet," said Reet.

"They're shy." She went off past the last wagon, into the dark beyond the lantern light, where the ground was bare of heather and the sky was already pricked with stars.

Reet watched her go. She noticed the direction, because she noticed everything: away from the camp, north, toward the Hollow. She noticed that the basket was lifted, just once, high, as one lifts a bowl at a feast. She heard, or thought she heard, a small dry clatter, like a hand of cards being shaken. She filed the three of them with the other things she had not yet needed, and then Taavi, behind her, was sick over the wheel.

"Sorry," said Taavi, from a distance. "I'm so sorry. It's the, technically it's the ground."

"It isn't moving," said Reet.

"It *feels* as if it's thinking of moving." He sat down, very green himself. "Some people get it before. Like a man who feels the weather in his teeth."

Toomas had come out of the dark with a twist of paper in his hand. He knelt beside the boy, which cost him, she saw, and she heard the knee crack like a stick. "Mint," he said. "Chew, do not swallow. It is dried, from my own window, and it has never done anyone any harm except my guests." He folded Taavi's fingers around it. "Look at the horizon. At a fixed star, if you can find one. And breathe out longer than you breathe in."

"Is that a proverb?"

"It is a remedy. The proverbs I keep for people who are not sick."

He stayed with Taavi for an hour, talking about nothing, the best way to cut a mitre, the price of calf-skin, a quarrel between two binders in his youth over a monogram, and Reet sat with her back to the wheel and watched him. She watched him, once, look back over the dark, north, to where the sky above the Hollow glowed a faint dull orange on the underside of the cloud. He looked at it longer than at the south. He said nothing. His face was perfectly calm, in the lantern-light, and his hand on the boy's back went on moving in small slow circles, and Reet thought: he is saying nothing about something, and it is very large. And she did what she did not often do. She let it alone.

Midnight arrived without any warning. It was quite dark. The fires had burned down. In the wagon above her someone was snoring in two keys. Reet, who had been lying on her back with her eyes open counting the stars she knew, found that she had stopped being able to hold the count.

The sound came first. It was not loud. It was low, so low that she felt it through the ground and into the base of her spine before it was a sound at all, and it came from the south, a long, deep, rolling roar like surf heard through a wall. All forty wagons shifted on their axles. Thirty-six oxen lifted their heads and lowed, one after another, down the line. The lantern on the wagon-tail guttered and leaned, and she saw the flame lie over sideways, pointing north, as if the whole world had tipped a degree on its keel.

And then she felt it. She had never been in the Seam at midnight. She had ridden out at dawn for four years to what the night had done. Now she was in it, and it was not like an earthquake, not like anything she knew; it was like standing on the deck of a ship that has begun, very gently, to turn. The stars slid. A black hill she had marked at dusk went by on her left, in no hurry, and a smell of wet stone, and cold, and brine came out of the dark; and all of it, the roaring, the turning, the sliding of the world, lasted perhaps the length of a long breath and went out.

Silence. A single ox, complaining.

Nobody spoke. Then Taavi, somewhere in the dark, said in a very small voice, "Oh, I'm going to be sick again," and from the wagon above her somebody laughed, and the camp began to murmur, and lanterns came up one by one.

Reet lay still. She was shaking. She ran her mind back down the evening, the road, the Three Pines, the heron, and found them all, and the cairn and the hedge and the Guild stair, and the smooth hole at the stone. Nothing new was missing. She had not drawn anything. She held the thought carefully, as one holds a cup that is nearly full, and she lay on the ground that had turned, and did not know where she was.

Aino came by with a mallet and a stake. She did not say anything. She walked round the wagon-tail in the lantern-light, found a place by the near wheel, and drove the stake into the ground with three exact blows.

"What's that for?" said Reet. "If it moves."

Aino leaned on the mallet and looked down at her, tall and black against the stars, with the red scarf pale at her throat.

"It'll still move," she said. "The stake's just so we know where we woke up."
