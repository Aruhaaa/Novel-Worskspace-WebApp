# The Night Outside the Rope

He told it to the sheet on the washing-line, and Reet counted the pegs.

There were eleven. She counted them twice, because it was easier to count than to listen, and the second time she noticed that the third peg was a different wood from the rest, a paler wood, ash against the others' beech. She noticed a chip on the well-lid, and a drain with a green lip, and the black knot in the wash-house door that looked, if you were fond of such things, like the Gullet from the south. She went on noticing. It was what she did instead of weeping, and it had never before failed to work.

"It was the autumn of the year two ninety-nine," said Toomas. "It rained eleven days. You will not remember it; you were seven. The Haldja came down out of the gorge above Palu the colour of a cattle-trough and began to eat the east bank, a yard a night, and the surveyors at Palu said it would be in the streets before the month was out. Palu is built on forty bridges, Reet. It is a town that stands on rope. A river that changes its mind in that gorge takes the whole of it, and the houses, and the women in the houses. My sister lived there. Helju. She wrote to me every Midwinter and twice in a bad year, and that October she wrote three times."

"And the Assembly?"

"Voted against evacuation on the ground of cost. They said the bridges would hold. They said the Atlas merely records." He was silent for a breath. "I was Keeper. I held the Atlas in my two hands every day of my life, and I had known for nine years what it did."

She did not move. A gust took the grey sheet and filled it and let it fall.

"By accident," said Toomas. "I want you to know that, though it is no defence. It was a small thing. A drain at the Hollow's own foot, which I had redrawn on the leaf for the sake of tidiness, and found changed, nine nights later, to match. I thought it a coincidence. I made six more tests. They were all of them very small. And then I understood what I held, and I told nobody. I wrote it in a notebook. I thought I was a man who would never use it." The chain-staff turned a quarter-turn in his hands. "On the ninth day of the rain I went down into the vault with the last jar of Founding base in the Guild's keeping, and a vial of Palu's water from the assay shelf, and I took the leaf, and I drew the river away."

"Away where?"

"Down a side-valley to the south. It was a pasture, I thought. I had it from the contours. A long dry fold with a few barns in it, running out on the far side of Leaf Hundred-and-sixty-three and down the Haldja's old course to the Ford." The brown eye was on the sheet; the white one on nothing. "I drew one stroke. It took four minutes. I did not think about where the water would go. I looked at the contours of my sister's town and I drew the river out of them, and the edge of the leaf was there, and the channel ran off it. I did not turn the page."

She said nothing.

"On the ninth night the river left Palu. The bridges stood. The barns were not barns. The valley was Soo, and it was on the next leaf, and it had fourteen roofs, and one night's water in the dark." He said it quite flatly. "The Assembly dismissed me the following spring on the ground of negligence. I did not speak. The leaf was struck from the Atlas as lost. It was neater, and nobody asked what had been lost from it."

"What did it cost you?"

It was the surveyor in her that asked. It came up out of her like a reflex. He turned his head and looked at her for the first time since the doorway.

"Mm," said Toomas. "I drew six miles of river. I have not been able to see the gorge above Palu since that night. I know that it is there. I am told that it is. I could walk it with my eyes shut and I would not know a single bend." He lifted a hand a little, and put it down. "You have, I think, begun to learn what I mean."

She had. She could have told him the exact size of it, and the colour, and the hush of snow. She said nothing about the pond.

"Where is the notebook?"

"In the false drawer of the Keeper's desk. I did not go back for it." And then he said, in the same flat voice, "My assistant had the desk after me. She was a very exact young woman, who kept the vault inventory and held the lamp the night I drew the river. She was not told what I did. She was told that she would one day be Keeper, and she was."

"Kaie."

"Yes."

"The dividers." Her voice had gone quite thin. "The bent leg. The four millimetres. You looked at my tracing in your workshop, Toomas, and your eyes went to the pinhole and stayed there, and I watched them. You *knew*."

"I suspected."

"You told me a far hand is beaten by a near one. You told me someone had to stand in Palu. You sat in your kitchen and made me tea and let me say 'fourteen Shifts, nine nights apart' as if it were something I'd found out on my own, and the whole time you knew exactly whose hand—" She stopped. She was shaking. She could not afford to be shaking; the ink would not take it. "You told me a true thing. And you left out the one that mattered."

"Yes."

"Do you know what Rule Eleven says?"

"That a rider reports and the Keeper interprets."

"It says that. And Mihkel said it to me, and you said it was cruel. And then you did it too." The wind took the sheet again, and it bellied toward her, and fell back. "I don't know who you are. I've been walking beside you for four days."

"No," said Toomas. "You have not known me at all. That is rather what I had hoped for."

She looked at him then. He stood in the cold yard with his moustache stirring and his bad leg in a tilt, a stooped old man in a Guild coat that had gone green at the seams, and she felt at that moment no pity whatever and found she could not name what she did feel. She thought of the eggs. She thought of a spit drying in the shape of a coin.

"I'm going to see to the oxen," said Reet, and went round the wash-house, and did not look back.

* * *

They rode out of Soo-on-the-Slope at noon, behind two oxen and a borrowed pony, in a cart that smelled of eels. Nobody mentioned the yard. Taavi, who had been told nothing, understood everything, and spent the afternoon making himself small in the corner of the cart-bed with his bandolier pressed against his chest. Aino drove. Toomas sat beside her on the board with his staff across his knees, looking at the road.

The country turned empty and open. The slope fell away behind them into a long rolling grassland, mile after mile of it, yellow as a lion, with here and there the pale smear of a salt-lick, and no road at all except the track the oxen made. Reet sat in the tail of the cart with her back to the direction of travel and her feet dangling. She watched the Slope go down behind the hills until even the lime trees were gone. She counted the miles by the oxen's paces. The number was exact. It rested in the place she kept all numbers, and she was aware of the pond, somewhere near it, in the hush, and of how little the hush weighed.

They made camp at dusk in the middle of nothing.

Aino walked the ring. She drove four iron stakes at the corners of the wagon-ground, and ran a rope from one to another, waist-high, hand over hand, and knotted it, so that the cart and the oxen and the cookfire stood in a square of tight hemp with a lantern hung on every side. "That's the rope," she said. "In the dark you hold the rope, and you go along the rope, and it takes you back to your fire. I don't care who you are. Outside the rope after the sun goes, you're on your own."

"I know what a rope is," said Reet.

"I'm sure you do."

She ate. She ate everything on her plate and did not taste it. Toomas, across the fire, made a small attempt at a remark, a question about the quality of Aino's fried eel, and was answered by Taavi, because Reet did not look up. It was early dark. The stars were out, thick and cold. The ground steamed a little as it gave back the day's heat. Somewhere in the north-east a wolf, or a dog, was saying something to no one.

She stood. She took the chain from the cart, sixty-six feet of it, coiled and weighted, and the plumb-bob on its cord, and the field book. From the box under the seat she took a vial, an empty one, and tucked it into her glove.

"Reet," said Toomas, very quietly.

"I'm going to measure."

"Mm. Measure what?"

"You said that unheld ground moves at night, and that a drawing lands. I have taken both of those on your word for four days." She did not stammer. She was telling the truth. "I have seen where the ground *was*. I've never seen it go. I should like to see something I can write down before I believe any more of what you say."

"Aino has a rule about the rope."

"Aino's not my Master," said Reet. "And neither are you."

Aino, on the far side of the fire, said, "Oi." But Reet had stepped over the hemp and was walking out into the dark, with the chain over her shoulder and her own shadow going long behind her in the lantern-light, and nobody came after her.

* * *

She walked two hundred paces due east. She counted them. At the top of a low rise, where the grass was short and a few flat stones stuck out of it like teeth, she stopped, and put down the chain, and drove the iron pin.

Then she did what she had been taught to do. She took three bearings on the three fixed marks she could see by starlight, the notch in the western hills, a lone thorn, and the faint glimmer of the cart-lantern. She ran the chain out a hundred links to the east, and pinned it, and ran it back, and wrote the figures in her book by the light of a shuttered lamp, and when she had finished she found that she was no longer angry. The anger had gone somewhere. It had been replaced by a clean, bright, steady stillness, the feeling of work.

It was very cold. The stars wheeled slowly. She sat on the flat stone with her back to the pin, and the field book on her knee, and waited, and thought, with a great calm, that she would be quite able to tell him the truth in the morning, whichever it was.

She did not know how long she waited. Time did not hold, in the Seam, in the small hours; it came in jerks. A little before midnight the wind dropped. Every sound she made began to come back to her half a breath late, as if the dome of the sky had been drawn tight over a mast. And then, from the south, from a long way off, there came a very low sound.

It was not like the night at the camp, which had been a roll, a ship turning. She was not inside the ring. She was on a rise in the open, with nothing round her, and the sound came toward her at the pace of a man walking, a vast steady grinding, and the whole dark country to the south rose up against the stars.

She watched it come. She would remember that. Something in her, in the cold white clarity of the surveyor, went on standing apart from the rest and taking notes. The hill to the south, a long low whaleback she had marked at dusk, stirred and *lifted*; the grass on its flank rippled outward in a wave, like a sheet being shaken out of a window; and the whole hill came forward with the grace of a tablecloth drawn across a table, sliding down upon her with its turf folding over in a long slow curl. The ground under her feet tilted. It tilted by degrees, the way a floor does on a ship, and her pin went over on its side, and the chain slithered away down the slope with a clatter, and the lamp rolled out of her hand and went out.

And at her feet, where there had been dry grass, there was a lake.

It came up out of the earth as she watched. A lake of white. Salt, not water; a crust of it first, blue-white in the starlight, pushing up through the turf like frost on a pane, and then the brine welling under, black and bright, in a sheet, spilling over the stones that had stuck out like teeth. It ran over her boots. It was so cold that for a moment she did not feel it. It smelled of the Flats, of the sea, of a thousand dead summers.

She ran.

She did not decide to. The body decided. She ran north-west, toward where the camp had been, with the brine slopping at her heels and the ground rolling like the back of an animal, falling twice, and rising, and running again with her book crushed in her fist. Behind her the whaleback hill came over the rise with a long soft roar and the lake rose to meet it, and she could not see the lantern. She could not see the lantern. She had lost the rope, and the camp, and every bearing she had taken, and there was nothing in the whole dark world but the sound of the ground moving.

Something seized her by the scarf.

It was her own. She had worn it round her neck since Soo, a strip of grey wool. It was hauled backward so hard that she choked and her heels went out from under her, and a huge hand closed on the back of her skull and wound itself in her cropped hair and pulled. She was dragged. She was dragged through salt and grass for a space she could not measure, and flung down on her face, and a voice in her ear roared, "Hold it! Hold the damned thing!"

It was a stake. She had her arms round it. It was iron, as thick as her wrist, driven into the earth to the last hand's breadth, and Aino Rebane, all six feet two of her, lay across her back with one arm locked round the stake and the other round Reet, and the red scarf had come unwound and was flapping across both their faces.

"Don't let go!" bawled Aino. "I don't care what it does! It'll do things! Don't let go!"

"I'm holding!"

"Hold *harder*!"

The world roared. It tilted forward and back, and the stake sang in her arms like a plucked string, and something huge went past in the dark with a noise like a sea, and she shut her eyes, and held.

* * *

It took a long time to end. She never afterwards knew how long. The ground stopped moving, and then moved a little more, and stopped again, and in between the quiet came back by degrees, so slowly that she could not have said when she first heard it. They did not let go. Aino's arm had stiffened round her like a bar of iron, and the stake was cold, and the salt had dried to a crust on her face and hands.

Neither of them spoke for the first hour. Then Aino began to name the oxen, in a low steady voice, like a woman telling beads. "Registrar. Auditor. Guildmaster. Treasurer. Rule Forty. Mihkel. The Second Clerk of Works. The Deputy Under-Keeper of the Weights..." She had a great many more than thirty-six. She was inventing them. She went on for some while. At the Deputy Assistant Surveyor of Sluices her voice cracked in the middle, quite suddenly, and she said in a different voice, "You stupid girl. You stupid, stupid girl."

"I know," said Reet.

"I heard you go. I counted ten and then I came."

"I know."

"If you ever—"

"I know, Aino."

They lay in the cold, holding the iron. And through the long hours, with her face against the salt-crusted grass and the great black bowl of stars turning overhead, Reet thought.

She had been wrong. She had come out to measure a lie, and she had measured the truth. The ground moved. A hill had come down upon her like a cloth, and a lake had stood up out of a dry field, and every yard of it had been done in the dark by something that could be told what to do; and somewhere in the Hollow, in a vault, in a Keeper's gloved hands, a pen had been lifted. She thought of the Gullet, stopped like a mouth. She thought of Kuld Mere, set down on a heath as carefully as a hand sets a cup. She thought of a river, led off the edge of a leaf in four minutes.

It had drowned Soo. That was the first thing, and it was true, and it would be true when she was old. But the other half of it lay against it like a coin's other face. A hill could be asked to stand. A river could be led away from nine hundred people on forty bridges. If it could be done to kill, it could be done to save, and the only person on the whole continent besides Kaie Tammaru who knew how it was done lay, at that moment, in a cart two hundred yards off, with a bad knee, and a milky eye, and a stain on his heart that she could not forgive.

She did not need him to be good. She needed him to know.

At first light the sky went grey and then rose, and she saw what had happened. The camp lay two hundred yards to the west, on its square of rope, whole, with its oxen standing and its lantern still burning. Between her and it was a lake. It was about a mile long, flat and pale as a plate of milk, with salt heaped white on the shores and a few dead reeds standing in the shallows, and the whaleback hill lay beyond it, in a new place, with its turf peeled back like a lid. It was perfectly still. A single wading bird stood in the middle of it.

She let go of the stake, finger by finger. Her hands would not at first open. She sat up, and Aino sat up beside her, and the two of them looked at the lake. In her fist was her field book, soaked, and in her glove, she found, still corked and intact, the vial. Without thinking, as every rider does, as she had done since she was fifteen, she knelt at the edge and filled it with the new water, and sealed it, and wrote the label with a wet pencil. *Unnamed lake. First light.*

Toomas was waiting at the shore. He had walked round the long way, on the bad leg, with the staff, in the cold, and he stood there with a tin mug of tea in his hand that he had carried the whole distance and had not spilled. He did not say a word. He held it out.

She took it. She drank. It was sweet, and too strong, and she supposed Taavi had made it.

"Teach me how it's done," said Reet.

She said it without a stammer, in a level voice, to the lake. She had not looked at him.

Toomas was silent for some time. The bird lifted off the water, and wheeled, and went away to the south. "Not yet," he said.
