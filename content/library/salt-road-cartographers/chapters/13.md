# The Blank

The wind gave out at a line you could have ruled.

Toomas felt it go from his moustache first. All morning it had come at the cart out of the west, thin and cold and persistent, picking at the tarpaulin and combing the grass into long yellow stripes, and he had borne it as he bore the knee, by pretending it belonged to somebody else. Then the grass thinned to grey stubble, and the stubble to bare grit, and a pace beyond the last scrape of lichen the air simply left off. It was like a door being shut in a house of many rooms.

Aino hauled on the reins. Nobody had told her to. Registrar and the ox called Mihkel stood in the yoke with their heads low and their flanks going like bellows, and the cart rocked forward on its axle, and settled, and was still.

Ahead of them the plain began.

He had been told it was white. He had been told so by a ferryman's song, and had made the usual allowance for song, and still he was not ready. It was white as the inside of a mussel shell. It lay flat as a plate and so bright that the horizon had no edge, only a place where the white thinned to a paler white and was called sky. Salt crust over chalk, he judged, and nothing on it: not a stalk, not a stone, not a bird. Six miles across, if Veikko's grandmother had paced it honestly, and she had walked in on a bet, which is not a condition that favours honest pacing.

"Walk till you hear the quiet," said Reet.

She sat in the tail of the cart with her heels hanging, as she had sat since the lake. It was the first thing she had said that morning that was not a number, and she did not say it to him.

"I don't hear anything," said Taavi, from among the sacks. "Technically that's the point of the— no, wait. I do. I can hear it. It's the absence of the— oh, that's horrible. Can everyone hear that?"

"There's nothing to hear," said Aino.

"That is what I'm *saying*."

Aino clicked her tongue, and the oxen went forward onto the white.

The wheels bit the crust with a short dry sound like a thumb on a drumskin, and the sound did not go anywhere. It was struck and it was over. Toomas tried his voice. "Taavi," he said, at the ordinary pitch, "would you be so good as to see to the tea?" The words left his mouth and dropped on the board at his feet. They did not carry. They rang off nothing. A voice on the Blank, he discovered, was a thing you set down like a parcel, and it stayed where you set it. Behind him Taavi said, "Of course," and it came out flat and close, as though the boy had spoken from inside a cupboard.

By the first mile the light had become a difficulty. It came up off the crust as well as down from the sky, so that the eye was struck twice, and Aino, who drove with her scarf pulled to her nose, had begun to weep from the glare, steadily and without drama, like a tap left running. Taavi produced a stoppered horn from his bandolier and said that technically it was an almanac remedy for snow-blindness, that he saw no reason salt should be any different, and that nobody was obliged. He put a thumb of lamp-black under each of his own eyes. Then, because he could not bear a job left unfinished, he did Aino's, and Reet's, with the grave tenderness of a man blacking a stove.

"I look like a widow's cat," said Aino.

"You look like a person who will not go blind. Technically."

"Do the old man."

"Mm," said Toomas. "I have one eye that cannot be dazzled. It is the first employment I have found for it in six years, and I shall not waste it."

Reet had got down to walk. She went beside the near ox with her chin up, counting, and after the first mile she stopped doing it aloud, because her voice fell out of her and lay on the crust behind like something dropped by a child. He watched her small straight back. He did not speak to it.

* * *

He had thought of little else since the lake. Leaf Two-oh-six lay in the very middle of the Atlas, as near as four hundred and twelve leaves can be said to have a middle, and it was the only leaf in the vault that a Keeper was forbidden to touch. The man of the sparrows had told him so on the day he handed over the keys. *It has never needed correcting,* the old man had said, *and so it shall never be corrected, and if you find yourself reaching for it, Veskimae, go and lie down.* It was bare vellum, creamy and a little foxed. In its centre was a small square with a dot in it, and under the square no name. For years of mornings he had looked at it and taken it for a courtesy to a legend. It had needed twelve years in Tinta Row, and a girl asking to be taught, before it occurred to him that the one place in Orrin that never moved was the one place on which the Atlas said nothing at all, and that this was not a coincidence either.

He had said *Not yet* at the lake because he wished to see the thing before he opened his mouth. A man who has learned everything by breaking something has no business teaching by the same method. He wanted a witness better than himself. The difficulty was that the witness might not be there. He had a verse, and a blank leaf, and an old man's habit of listening to ferrymen, and on those he had staked the only trust anyone had offered him in twelve years.

At the third mile Registrar faltered. It was a small thing, a hitch in the great slate-coloured shoulder, but Aino saw it, and her hands went tight on the reins.

"Rest them," said Toomas.

"At the stone."

"What stone?"

"The one in the song." She did not turn her head. "There's always a stone at the end of a song. That's what they're for." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she cleared it with a noise like a boot in gravel, and drove on.

It was Reet who saw it. She stopped dead in the middle of the white and he watched her shoulders come up, and then she said, quite clearly, in a voice with no echo in it anywhere, "Six thousand two hundred paces from the lichen. And it's a stone."

* * *

It was black. That was the first wrong thing about it, in a country of white: there was no dark rock within sixty miles of this spot, and so somebody had brought it, on something, across the crust, and had not thought the labour worth a remark.

"How?" said Taavi. "It must weigh a ton."

"A ton and a half, I should say. On an ox, I imagine." Toomas lowered himself from the cart, which took a while. "Which she then named."

"Something rude," said Aino, with feeling, and for the first time since the lake she looked a little like herself.

It lay on the white like a loaf on a cloth: long and low, knee-high, the top dressed flat by someone with better tools than patience. At its centre sat a brass stud, green with three hundred years, of the pattern every Guild datum had worn since. From the stud a groove ran straight to the south edge, a hand wide and a finger deep, dry as bone, its floor furred with salt. And along the north edge, facing the way they had come, were the letters.

They were cut deep in square capitals, each as long as a thumb, without a flourish. The A's had no crossbar and stood like tents. The R's closed with a tail that flicked out at the foot like a cat's. He knew the hand. He had signed his name beneath it on the Founding Charter every year of his Keepership, because Keepers did, as a courtesy to a woman three hundred years dead, and he had never once looked at an R and thought *a living person made this.*

"That is her," said Toomas. "Hilja Orm's own hand. I have been copying that R since I was twenty-six."

He read it aloud. The voice dropped dead at his feet, as every voice did here. "'I stood here. I drew only what I saw.'"

Beneath the line, smaller, two hands were carved in low relief. The upper one held a pen above a plain rectangle, which might have been a leaf. The lower was pressed flat against a wavering line, fingers spread, which might have been ground or water. Between them stood a few words more. "'The far hand writes,'" he read. "'The near hand holds.'" And last, at the foot, a name, *H. ORM*, and a single upright stroke that could only be the numeral one.

"Year one," said Reet. "She dated it before there was a Guild to date by."

"Mm. The Guild dated itself by it, I think. Afterwards."

"That's Veikko's song."

"Or Veikko's song is that. I should like to think his grandmother's grandmother, poling the Ford that year, was listening to somebody."

She had not looked at him since the cart stopped. She stood with her field book shut in her hand, reading the capitals the way she read everything, as though they might yet be wrong. Then, without turning, she said, "You said not yet."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because I was frightened, certainly. But also because everything I know I learned by breaking something." He leaned his weight on the chain-staff and set its iron shoe against the black stone, lightly, as a man touches a sleeping animal. "I did not wish to teach you by the same method. I wanted a better witness than myself. You have had a great deal of my word, Reet. This is not my word."

She turned her head at last. The lamp-black under her eyes made her look like a very serious owl. "Then ask me something," she said. "You always do."

"What is a benchmark?"

"A fixed point. A mark of known height that everything else is measured from."

"And what makes it fixed?"

"Someone sets it and swears to it."

"Swears. Mm. And when the next man's level disagrees?"

"Then the next man checks his level. The mark is the mark."

"Why?"

She opened her mouth to say *because it is*, and he watched her not say it. He watched the surveyor in her go and look, as she had been trained, for the thing underneath. "Because she stood there," said Reet slowly. "And he didn't."

"Because she stood there. Now. At the Hallik stone, where the Atlas had drawn a lake and the boundary was creeping toward it. You drew it as you saw it, and said you stood. Who won?"

"I did."

"Because?"

"Because I was nearer."

"Because you were *there*." He tapped the staff once on the stone. It made no ring. "I spent thirty years under the impression that the Atlas ruled the ground as a king rules a county. That is how a Keeper is taught to think of it, and the Guild's own teaching is the cover over that: that the Atlas merely records, as a clerk records. I learned the lie and then the secret, by accident, with a drain at the Hollow's foot, and I thought the secret was the whole of it. The Atlas does not merely record. It decides. A king decides, you see. I thought I had found the king. And here is an old woman's hand, saying that she drew only what she saw." He looked at the lower carving, the palm flat against the wave. "A king does not stand anywhere. A clerk does not stand anywhere. Neither is believed by a hill."

"Say that shorter," said Reet.

He had said it to her a dozen times. He found it was a different thing to have it said back.

"The ground believes whoever stood on it," said Toomas.

For a while nobody spoke, which on the Blank was no different from speaking, since the words did not go far in either case.

"That's," said Taavi, in a very small voice, "technically a remarkable thing to build a continent on."

"It's a remarkable continent," said Aino.

"But the vials," said Reet. She had gone quite still. "The assay shelf. Every rider fills one on the spot and writes the place on the label. We think it's for chemistry. The Guild says it's for assay."

"Thousands upon thousands of small witnesses in glass. A far hand cannot write on a place unless it holds that place's water, because it is the water that remembers who stood. The vial is a rider's *I stood here*, kept on a shelf." He heard his own voice go flat. "I used a vial of Palu's, twelve years ago. I never asked whose it was."

"And Kaie's using them up."

"Yes."

She nodded, once, as one notes a bearing. He did not say the rest of it, which was that three hundred years of Keepers had taken a woman's *I stood here* and turned it into *I, the Keeper*, and that somewhere in the saying the witness had become the throne, and that he had sat on it. She would arrive at that by herself. She arrived at most things by herself, a little before he did.

* * *

Taavi spent the next hour on his knees. He scraped the crust into a twist of paper and tasted it, which he said he had been warned never to do, and announced that it was chalk and salt and a thread of iron and nothing living, not a spore, the cleanest ground in Orrin, and that it was a dreadful thing to say about ground. He cleaned the groove with a feather. He examined it with a glass. And at last he sat back on his heels and said, "A gutter cut this carefully has to carry something. I'm sorry. That's a very obvious sentence. But it has."

"It's a noon-line," said Reet.

She was lying on her stomach with one eye on the brass stud and a pencil laid along the groove. "Due north and south. It's true to the minute; I'll have it to the second when the sun's up. It's a meridian. She cut it so the sun would stand over the length of it at noon." She looked up. "How long?"

"An hour," said Toomas, who had been watching the sky since they arrived. "A little more."

"Then it fills at noon."

"There is no water within six miles."

"That's what a stood-water vial is for," said Reet, "isn't it? The place's own. She can't have thought it would be kept anywhere else."

They waited. Taavi laid out his glass vials in a row on the crust, and in one of them he put a thimble of water from the cart's barrel, good water, drawn at the well in Soo and drunk by all of them for two days. He touched a speck of Salme's base to it on the point of a pin. It went the colour of old rust and sat there, a scum on the top, a stain going nowhere. "That's a failure," he said. "Technically it's a perfectly good failure. It needs to be *theirs*. The ink does. I thought it was only that it wanted to be clean."

The oxen were unyoked and hobbled. Registrar stood a moment with his great slate head hanging, and then he knelt, front legs first, and then the back, and lay down on the crust with a groan so long and low that even the Blank could not quite lose it. Aino went to him at a run. She put her hand flat on his ribs and counted something under her breath. When she was sure of it she took her hand away and walked, rather quickly, to the black stone, and sat down against its north side in the last narrow strip of shade, with her knees drawn up and the salt-hook laid across them.

Toomas, from where he sat with the knee stretched out, saw it begin. She had her face in her scarf. Her shoulders went up once, and a sound came out of her that was not a sob, because a sob goes on; it was a single enormous noise, torn off the top of her like a lid. The Blank took it without a mark. It lay on the crust at her feet, small and private, and was not repeated. When it was over she wiped her face on the red wool and looked at the back of her hand as though it had said something rude.

He did not go to her. He looked at the ox.

"He is breathing," he observed, to nobody. "Eight miles without a halt is a day and a quarter for an ox. He will forgive you by supper. Oxen are not subtle."

"Nine," said Aino, from the stone.

"Nine."

"I wanted to be somewhere that wasn't—" She stopped. "I know what they're like."

"Mm."

"It's not the ox."

"No."

"Don't ask."

"I did not," said Toomas. He had been wondering for four days about the hens, which ate grain and never laid, and he put the thought away carefully, as one puts a letter in a drawer to be answered later. After a time he took the tea Taavi had made and set it on the crust beside her, within reach, and went back to his place by the groove.

* * *

At noon Reet said, "Now," half a second before he saw it.

The head of the groove darkened. It was exactly the way blotting paper takes up ink: a dark that began under the stud and crept south along the channel, then thickened, then was not dark but wet, and then it was water. It rose from nowhere. It came up out of a hairline crack under the brass, clear as air, so cold that the dry light above it seemed to smoke, and it filled the groove from end to end and stood level with the lip, trembling very slightly, with the whole white sky laid in it like a coin in a palm.

Taavi was already moving. He drew a thimbleful with a pipette and dropped it into an empty vial beside the first. A speck of the base, on the point of the pin. This time there was no rust and no scum. The water went black at once and all the way through, the way the pupil of an eye opens in a dark room, and sat there perfectly clear, perfectly black, ink and nothing else, thin enough to run and dark enough to read.

"It's the same base," said Taavi unsteadily. "It's the same *pin.*"

"It is the same everything," said Toomas, "except who stood."

Nobody said anything else. They looked at the little vial in Taavi's fingers, a thimble of ink that a clerk could not have made, from a place nobody lived, on the word of a woman three hundred years in her grave.

Reet had got to her knees at the south end of the stone. She took off her left glove with her teeth. The hand underneath was small and square and stained grey-blue to the wrist, and he saw it shake once, quite slightly, and then not. She laid it in the groove.

It fitted. He saw that before she did. It was a hand's width exactly, no more, a hand's width and a finger deep. Not a Keeper's width, not a width for a glove. A hand, such as a ferryman has, or a child, or a girl who counts. The water covered her to the knuckles. She looked at the carved palm on the stone above, flat on its wavering line, and at her own flat on the water, and her face did what it did over a hard piece of ground when the figures at last agreed.

"It's a hand," said Reet. "That's all it says. There's no Keeper anywhere on it. There's no vault, there's no seal." She lifted her eyes. "Toomas. It's just a hand."

"Mm."

"Anyone could do this."

The plain took it and set it down at her feet, flat and unechoed, where all of them could see it.
