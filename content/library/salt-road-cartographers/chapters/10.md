# Eleven Stones

Everything in Soo-on-the-Slope was built to look downhill.

Reet saw it from the top of the track, in the thin grey light before sunrise, with the Company's wagons grinding up behind her. Forty cottages of grey fieldstone climbed the hillside in three rough terraces, each with a slate roof and a deep-set door and a single window, and every window faced the same way, south, toward the bottom of the slope, where something lay flat and silver under a lid of mist. Nobody had hung a curtain on the downhill side. She counted the windows without meaning to. Forty-one. A woman in a doorway was shaking out a rug, and as the wagons passed she turned her whole body away, so that she did it with her back to the valley.

"Slope market," said Hendrik Loo, riding up on his cob with the hat pulled down. "Every sixth day, at dawn. Eggs, eel, wool, sedge baskets. We take their salt order and sell them our remainders. An honest little place. Don't ask them about the pond."

"What pond?" said Taavi.

"Precisely," said Loo.

The market ran along the top terrace, under a row of lime trees whose leaves had gone the colour of toast. It was a poor affair of trestles and handcarts. There was a man selling smoked eel off a string, and a girl with sedge baskets stacked up to her chin, and a woman in a brown shawl with two wooden crates of eggs, brown and speckled, laid in straw. At the end of the row, on an upturned bucket, sat a man with a lame dog across his knees and a hat on the ground for pennies. He was selling nothing that Reet could see. But there was a ring of people about him with their heads together, and as she came past, a voice said, "—and that's why they won't drink from the well, the well's the same water—"

Rumour sold itself in Soo at about two coppers the piece. She stopped, and listened, and gathered it up like a surveyor taking soundings, and it was all of it much the same. The pond took a lamb every spring. The pond was warm in the middle, even in January. A man in a grey coat had come last winter with a brass tube and a boy, and taken readings, and been seen off by the dogs. "The Guild calls it a lost leaf," the man with the lame dog was saying. "That's what's written down. *A lost leaf.* We call it Soo."

"Eggs," said Aino, who had followed her up with a sack of salt on her shoulder and the red scarf pulled up to her nose. "You want eggs. You've gone the colour of a candle."

"I'm perfectly well."

"You're white as a *sheet*. Go on. Buy a dozen. It's the only advice I'll give you today."

* * *

The egg-woman was somewhere past fifty, with forearms like a baker's and grey eyes set deep in a face that had had a lot of weather. She counted twelve eggs into Reet's satchel one at a time, in straw, without being asked, and named the price, and Reet paid it. Then she looked up over Reet's shoulder and her face did something.

It did not change. That was what Reet noticed. It went on being a face, a plain, tired, middle-aged face, but a certain quality that it had had a moment before simply went out of it, like the colour from a pan taken off the fire.

Toomas had come up behind her. He had been lagging at the bottom of the terrace, on Clerk, and he had got down to walk the last of it. He stood with both hands on the chain-staff, and the white eye turned on the crates, and the brown one on the woman, and he said, in his mildest voice, "I should like a dozen myself, if I may. For the road."

"Veskimae," said Aino, helpfully, from the next stall. "Tinta Row. Binder. He's with us."

The egg-woman looked at him. She said nothing at all. She turned her head a little to the side, and spat.

She did not spit on him. It would have been easier if she had. She spat on the stones, a hand's breadth short of his boot, a neat dry little spit, and the grey stone took it and went dark. Then she bent, and took up a dozen eggs, and began to lay them in a straw nest with exactly the same care as she had laid Reet's. She named the price. Toomas laid the coppers on the trestle. She picked them up with two fingers and put them, without counting, back into his palm. She closed his hand around them, firmly, like a woman closing a book.

"I'll not take your money," she said. "Take the eggs."

"Madam—"

"I said take them."

He took them. He thanked her. His voice was exactly as it always was. He turned and went away down the terrace with the eggs held in the crook of his arm, leaning on the staff, and as he went the people in the market parted for him, not hurriedly, not with any air of making room, but in the manner of a hedge that has been told a man is coming. Reet stood with her satchel against her chest. She looked at the spit on the stones. It was already drying, from the edges inward, in a shape something like a coin.

"Well," said Aino quietly, "that's a thing."

"He didn't say anything."

"Didn't he?" said Aino. "Funny. I thought he'd said a great deal." She hitched the sack. "I'll see to the salt. You go and look at your pond, if you're going. It'll be there. It's not got anywhere else to be."

* * *

It was a quarter of a mile down. The track dropped through a stand of birch and a field of stubble and then came out above the water, and she stopped at the edge and stood with her hands at her sides, and did not at first do anything at all.

It was a shallow, silver, windless pond about three hundred yards long. In the east it was fringed with reed. At the west end a ridge of raw yellow gravel stood out of it like a dam, and that, she knew without being told, was the place where the new water had come through. And in the pond, under two or three feet of water as clear as glass, was a village.

She had not been ready for that. She had read the pre-flood Dawn Sheet of Soo a thousand times, the three bends, the nine-stone ford, the fourteen roofs. She had thought of it as a drawing. She had drawn it from memory on the floor of her loft at sixteen, to the link, in the lamplight, with a ruled pencil. And now it lay beneath her, laid out in the water in the true colours of drowned things, green and brown and dull gold, with the weed streaming over the doorsteps, and she saw that her drawing had been right. Every roof was where she had put it.

She walked the shore. She walked it slowly, with her eyes on the water, and she counted as she went, the way she counted everything. The chimneys came up out of the shallows one after another, squat stone stacks, some of them still wearing their pots, with the thatch long gone from between them and the rafters standing like ribs. The miller's. The weaver's, with its wide low door. The one with the green shutter that she had marked on the sheet as a bakery, because of a little drawing of a loaf. In the middle, on a spit of sand, stood an ash tree. It was dead and had lost its bark. It stood up white and bare from the water, and in the shallows at its foot, with only one end showing, there lay a bench.

"Fourteen," she said aloud.

She had walked the whole shore and counted fourteen stacks, and she knew with a perfect cold clarity, as she knew the number of her own fingers, that she had counted them correctly. It was the same fourteen as on the Dawn Sheet. Nothing had been lost but the people.

She stood a little longer. Then she turned and looked up the slope.

* * *

The stones stood on a rise above the east shore, in a half circle, facing the water. They were not tall. They had been rough-dressed out of the grey fieldstone of the hillside by somebody with a hammer and no great skill, set upright in the turf, each a different height, and each cut, deeply, with a name and a mark. It had been done in a hurry, and with love, and afterwards, over a good many years, by many hands. She could see that there were three or four ways of making an *R*.

She went round them from the left. She read them without touching, as she would have read a sheet.

*Marten Kõiv.* A loaf, round, with a cross cut across the top. *Ilse Kõiv*, below it, a ladle. *Ode Raidma*, and the hoop of a barrel, with the staves splayed. Two stones side by side, the same height, the same grey, *Toivo Pärn* and *Tarmo Pärn*, and over each a single oar, one slanting left and one right, so that when you stood between them the oars crossed. *Rein Aavik*, the eye of a millstone. *Anni Kuusk*, a shuttle. *Jakob Mets*, a crook. *Helmi Vaher*, a milk pail, with the handle drawn too big. *Oskar Lepik*, a wheel with all its spokes.

She counted ten. She stopped before the eleventh. It was the smallest of them. It stood a little apart at the end of the arc, no higher than her knee, and it was newer than the rest, the cut cleaner, and from the lichen she estimated it had been set a winter later. She did not read it yet. She looked, instead, at the little piles of things that had been left on the grass before each stone: a heel of bread, a sprig of rosemary, a cooper's nail, a handful of grey feathers. Someone had come this morning. Someone had come every morning. The grass was trodden to a path, round the half circle, and the path was worn into a groove, and the groove was the exact width of an old woman with two sticks.

* * *

The mill was a hundred yards farther on, on the high ground at the north end of the pond, where the village had once had the sense to stand. It was the only building that the water had not wholly taken. It stood above the shallows with its feet wet, a tall roofless shell of grey stone, with the three sluices still in the wall beside the wheel-pit, and Reet, who had read of the crooked middle sluice in the Dawn Sheet and had never thought to see it, stood with her head on one side and measured it by eye. Seven degrees off true. Exactly as drawn.

She was not looking for anything. She was only taking the bearing. But the door of the mill had a stone lintel over it, a single long slab, pale and weathered, and as she passed under it her eye fell to the left end, where the lime wash had flaked away, and she stopped.

It was a scratch. A tick, with a short hook on the downstroke, and two small dots beside it. It was cut deep, much deeper than a pencil-knife would go, deep enough to hold a thumbnail, and the edges were clean. It had been recut. Somebody had gone over it with a nail, again and again, a good many times, so that the grooves were as sharp as a new wound.

She knew the tick. Every Surveyor's tick was lodged with the Roll in the Day-book room, and she had read the Roll for amusement at sixteen. A hook on the down-stroke, two dots. It was a tick that had been entered in Guild Year 271 by a young man from the south country, and she had seen it since in a different place: in the corner of a map, drawn out of memory, in a bookbinder's workshop, in the pencilled margin of an error he had planted on purpose.

She put out her ink-black hand and set the tip of her finger in the groove.

It was cold. It fitted her finger exactly. She stood a long moment under the lintel and felt the whole ground of her inward map turn quietly over, like a page. He had been a Keeper. He had been dismissed in the year after the flood. He had told her it was a matter of an Assembly minute. The man on the bucket had said, *that's what's written down*. And here was the tick. Here it was, cut on the mill of the drowned village, by the man who had been Keeper when the water came, and kept sharp by somebody, every year since, so that no one in Soo should ever forget whose hand it was.

*Lost leaf*, she thought. And then, rather slowly: *Whose?*

* * *

She found them on the rise.

She had come back round the shore, unhurried, with the eggs in her satchel and nothing in her head that she was ready to look at, and she saw them from some way off: two figures at the end of the half circle of stones, one sitting on the grass and one standing, and then the one standing sat down. She stopped under the birches. She did not go nearer.

Toomas was on the turf with his bad leg straight out in front of him and the chain-staff laid across his knees. He was sitting by the smallest stone. There was a tin mug beside his hand on the grass. She could see from where she stood that the tea in it had gone cold; there was no steam, and a leaf of birch had blown onto the surface and lay there, turning slowly. He had not drunk it. He was looking at the water with his good eye, and the white one, as ever, at nothing.

Taavi had found him. He must have come down the track at a half-run, because his bandolier was still swinging and clinking, a small agitated sound that went on for some time after he had sat. He sat down on the grass beside Toomas, not near, a good three feet off, with his knees drawn up and his grey-blue fingers laced round them. Reet saw him open his mouth. She saw him shut it. She saw him consider, with the whole of his freckled face, a number of things he might say, and put them all down one after another. He took a twist of paper from a pocket and held it out. Mint.

Toomas looked at it. After a moment he took a leaf, and put it in his mouth, and chewed. The two of them sat side by side and looked at the pond. Neither said anything. The wind came up, and moved the reed, and turned the birch-leaf in the mug.

She did not go to them. She went to the smallest stone.

She had put it off long enough. She crouched in front of it with her satchel in her lap, and the grass was wet through her knees, and she looked at the heap of things that had been left there. A tin whistle. A bit of bread with a bite out of it. Four white pebbles, laid in a row by a hand that did not know how to count to five. The mark on the stone was a top, the kind a child spins with a string, cut with a very careful chisel, and cut again, and cut again.

"Mikk Kõiv," said Reet, aloud. "Aged six."

She had spoken quietly, and she did not think anyone had heard. But as the last word left her, something at the back of her head let go.

It was not like the Gullet. It was not a country going; it was something smaller and quicker and more exact, a gentle sweep of cold across a single page. She felt the pond go out. The shore, the fringe of reed, the gravel dam, the ash tree standing white in the shallows with the bench at its foot, the fourteen chimneys she had counted one by one, all of it went dark in her mind in order, like lamps being put out down a long street, and where it had been she felt nothing but a smooth, cool, pleasant blankness, the quiet of a sheet of snow.

She had drawn nothing. She had touched no ink. She had only come, and looked, and read a name.

She sat on her heels in the wet grass and remembered that there had been a pond. She remembered the number. She remembered the names, all eleven, perfectly. And she knew, quite clearly and without any pleasure in the knowing, that the ground had taken a toll for the looking, and had not asked her leave.
