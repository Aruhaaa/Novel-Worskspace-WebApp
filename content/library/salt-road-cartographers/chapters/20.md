# The Assembly of Bridges

The bell of Palu had a voice like a cracked pot, and Jaan Pold rang it the way other men split wood. Reet counted the strokes. She had counted bells since she was four, and by the ninth she knew that this was the wrong number for a fire and the wrong number for a funeral, and by the thirtieth she had stopped, because Liisu, beside her in the tower doorway, said flatly, "It doesn't finish till he's tired."

"Why not?"

"So they know it's not a drill."

The square lay below them like a dish of people being filled. They came in feast clothes still, the red sashes and the embroidered waistcoats, stained with plum down the front and creased from sleeping in them; they came with their hair unbrushed and their eyes small, carrying children on their hips and quarrelling in undertones, down the stairs from the upper bridges and along the rope walks from the north bank, until the beaten earth at the tower's foot was full and the overflow had climbed on to the Bell Bridge itself and stood along it rail to rail. Thirteen of them had gone up on the roof of the carters' stables. Reet counted them as well. She could not have stopped herself if she had been paid.

She had been in the tower since the fourth bell. At first light she had stood at the little south window with her cheek against the stone and watched a rider go up the east road to the upland, a small rider on a pale grey horse with a rope of silver down her back, followed by eight in grey coats. The rider did not look at the tower. She sat very straight, as one sits who has been told she is being watched, and then the road turned behind a shoulder of rock and there was only the road.

"Gone," Jaan had said, at her back. "Asked me at the door. I said noon. She said naturally."

Now Reet came out of the doorway and down the steps with her satchel, and a murmur went over the square and died. She had washed her face in the bell-tower cistern. It did not help. She was five feet tall, and had a notch through her right eyebrow, and had been awake for thirty hours, and in the whole of that great quiet crowd she could see exactly one face that she was sure was friendly, and it was eleven years old and had no front teeth. Beyond the last row, against the wall of the stables, stood Mihkel Org in his mustard waistcoat, tall hat in one hand, eating a sugar pastry in the manner of a man at his own execution. He was watching them. She had never in her life been so glad of a person who was watching her.

"Quiet," said Jaan. He did not raise his voice; the bell had done that. "Sit if you can't stand. Veskimae first."

The drummers' barrel from the feast stood on the earth in front of the tower, with its skin split. Toomas climbed on to it. It took him some time. He stood on it at last with his two hands on the chain-staff and his white moustache combed, which Reet had not expected, and his milky left eye turned a little away from the crowd, as it always was, so that he seemed to be addressing the people on one side of him with some severity and the people on the other with some doubt.

"Good morning," said Toomas. "I am Toomas Veskimae. Some of you know the name. Those over twenty will know the other thing I am called, and have not said it to my face. Is there anyone here who remembers the flood of the year two hundred and ninety-nine?"

Hands went up, a great many, slowly, like reeds.

"Do you remember what stopped it?"

"Somebody did," said a voice, from somewhere in the middle. It was an old woman's, dry and flat. "My gran said. Somebody did, the day it came. We put the line on the east wall."

"She was right," said Toomas. "I did."

Reet had thought she knew how quiet nine hundred people could be. She had not. It was not an absence of noise. It was a held thing, like the stillness of the Blank, but with a pulse in it.

He told it plainly. He told it as he had told it in the cold yard at Soo, slowly, in the questions that were his only way of saying a hard thing: Did they know why their river lay in a bed as wide as a street, and ran as thin as a bootlace? He had drawn it. Did they know that he had been Keeper then, and that his sister had lived in this town? He had loved her. He had stood in the vault with a vial of Palu's own water, and he had redrawn the river off the face of this valley with one stroke of a pen, and it had worked. He had not asked where the water would go. "I didn't ask. Do you understand that? It was not a mistake of arithmetic. It was a question that I did not put. It went to Soo."

And then he said the names. All eleven, with their trades, in order, from Marten the baker to Oskar the wheelwright. It took less than a minute. It was the longest minute Reet had ever stood through, and she counted every second of it, because it was the only thing she could do with her hands.

Somebody on the Bell Bridge spat.

It was not the spit of a person making a point. It was the spit of someone who has held a mouthful for a long time. "Twelve years," said a woman's voice, high and unsteady. "Twelve years I've put a candle in the window on the flood-day. For whoever it was. For *whoever*."

"Yes," said Toomas. "I am sorry that it was me."

Elsewhere an old man was crying, quite openly, with his hat over his face. A child asked loudly who had died, and was hushed. Reet looked for Jaan, who stood at the bell-rope with his arms folded and a face like a shut door. He did not look at his great-uncle. He did not look away from him either.

Toomas climbed down off the barrel. It took even longer than getting up. He stood beside Reet with his hands shaking on the staff, and said without turning his head, in a voice meant for her alone, "Your turn. Mm? Say it shorter than I did."

She had prepared three sentences in the tower. She forgot them. She went up on the barrel, which wobbled, and said the first thing in her head.

"I make it eight hundred and ninety of you, and thirteen on the stable roof."

Somebody laughed. It was a startled laugh, and it ran along a row and died, but she felt the square give an inch.

"My name is Reet Sallik. I'm a Dawn-Surveyor of the Guild, or I was. I count things, so I'll count this. Tonight, between midnight and first light, the valley you are standing in goes under forty feet of water. It is not a flood. Nobody is going to open a sluice. The Keeper of the Atlas has drawn it that way on a leaf in the vault, in a good ink, and the land will do what the leaf says. It does that. It has done it eight times, in eight other places, and you've been told the Atlas only records. It doesn't. It decides." She heard her own voice go quiet and level and clear, the voice she used for a bearing. "You'll want proof, and I haven't any I can carry up here. I can only tell you that I walked a lake to the yard nine days ago, and it was where the Book said, and I—"

"That's the skipping song," said a small voice from the front.

Reet stopped. A girl of about seven, with a rope-burn on one elbow, was looking up at her with extreme patience. "The far hand writes," said the girl, "the near hand holds. It's the skipping song. You jump on *stays*."

"Stand and say," said somebody behind her, slowly, "and the water stays." And a woman in the second row put a hand over her mouth.

She did not know who began it. It went through the square in a rush, like wind through barley, a line here and a line there: not sung, but muttered, rattled, the way one repeats a thing one has never once examined. *The far hand writes, the near hand holds.* Children had it. Their mothers had it. An old man at the back who must have been ninety was saying it under his breath with his eyes shut, his lips moving on the *stays*. It had been jumped on every bridge in Palu for three hundred years. It had been a set of instructions all that time, and they had used it to keep the rope turning.

Reet found that she had to wait for her voice.

"It means what it says," she said. "A drawing made far away is beaten by a drawing made on the spot, by the people who stood there. The Keeper's lake is far. We can be near. I can draw the ground, on East Rise, as you mean it to be, and the ground will believe whoever stood on it. That's you. It isn't me. I'm only the one who can draw to the yard." She made herself look along the faces. "There's a cost. The one who draws forgets the ground drawn, in proportion. A boundary stone is a yard of memory. A town is all of it. I'll lose every road I've ever walked. I'll know your faces. I don't know what else I'll know." Her left hand had found the cord at her neck and the brass plumb-bob on it, and she held on. "I'm not asking you to like it. I'm asking each household to say where it wants its house, standing in the place, aloud. That's all the drawing is. And you may say no. If you say no I won't draw. The Keeper has carts on the upland, and a place called Orav with three hundred roofs standing empty, and she'll take you there, and you'll live. I'm telling you that because it's true."

"Can you promise it'll take?" said a man, from the Bell Bridge.

She opened her mouth to say *yes*. She heard, a long way inside her, the little hitch that comes before a stammer.

"No," said Reet. "I've never drawn a town. Nobody has since Hilja Orm."

* * *

It took two hours. Afterwards she thought it had been like trying to carry water in a sieve, and the sieve had been the happiest she had ever been in.

"Orav!" That was Epp Mänd the cooper, a woman built along the lines of her own barrels, who spoke as she hooped, with a bang. "Three hundred roofs, standing empty, and paid for. I say take the roofs. I'd like a house I didn't have to fix."

"And be what, in them?" said someone. "Guests of the Guild? Charity cases?"

"It's grain-relief," said the cooper.

"It's charity with a better coat."

"My barley," said Old Ruus, who farmed the long field below the east terrace and had not been heard to speak above a murmur in the whole of Reet's acquaintance with him, which was one night. "Can't carry a field. Can't carry a ditch. I've a split-oak gatepost with an iron ring in it that my father hung. What do I do with a gatepost in Orav?"

"The notice said *precautionary*," said a woman with a baby. "Precautionary. Every autumn it's precautionary. The year before it was a landslip, and the year before that it was the survey drill, and I stood in the road in my nightdress."

"It wasn't a wolf this time," said Jaan, from the rope. They were the first words he had said since the bell stopped.

"A wolf's a wolf. I never knew a sheep to ask its name."

"And if it doesn't take?" said another. "If this girl's ink doesn't take? Then we're on a hill in the cold, and the lake has my kettle."

"Then you're on a hill," said Reet, "and the lake is where it would have been."

There was a pause, and then an appreciative groan. It was the groan of people who have heard a hard thing said plainly, and are not quite displeased.

It went on. Ivo Tamm and the widow Saar, whose goat shed had stood across their common line for nine years, fell upon each other with a joy that only long enemies feel, over whose goat was in whose shed. The goat in question had come to the Assembly as an interested party and was eating a ribbon. The Lauris and the Mänds, both of whom had a claim on the mulberry in the lower square, and neither of whom would say what it was, argued over who might pick its fruit; the mulberry stained the Lauris' roof and the Mänds' washing, and had done so for seven years, to the equal satisfaction of both. A boy of twelve wanted to know if it was true that there would be a school. A man who had never in his life crossed the Bell Bridge without touching the rail three times for luck wanted to know about the bridges.

"Palu without bridges isn't Palu," said Jaan.

"It's a name."

"It's a name because of the bridges."

"There'll be bridges," said Toomas, from the foot of the barrel, in the voice of someone laying a stone on a wall. "There is a seep in the birch hanger on the north flank of the Rise that wants a channel. If you mean to cut one, you may mean to cross it. That is the sort of thing the ink will take."

"And the dead?" said an old woman.

It was a small voice, and it went through the square like a needle. She was sitting on a stool at the edge, with a stick across her knees and her hands over it, very upright.

"Thirty-one behind the tower," she said. "And Helju Pold under the white stone, and my mother, and my man. I'll not leave them to the fish. I'll not go up a hill and leave them."

Nobody answered. Reet looked at Toomas, who had gone quite white, and then at Jaan, who had let go of the rope. He walked across the beaten earth to the old woman and stood in front of her. He was a very big man. He crouched, so that his face was level with hers.

"Where I go, they go," said Jaan.

"It can't be done, Reeve."

"The ground believes whoever stood on it," said Toomas, slowly. "Your mother stood, Mistress. Every one of them stood, and there is no one in the whole of Orrin who stood longer. I do not know if it will. I think the stones will. Reet?"

"Draw them," said Reet. She heard herself say it, and knew, with the part of her that went on taking notes, that she had just promised to put thirty-one graves on a hill. "Tell me where each stone goes. I'll draw the stones."

"Wrong," said Liisu.

She had climbed on the barrel, which seemed to be where the day was being done. She stood in her grey smock with its ribbons and her hands at her sides and her chin up. Reet saw several hundred people turn their faces to her with the helpless attention of the audience for a child's recitation.

"You've left out the school," she said. "The Guild says children can't draw maps. We're going to have a school where they have to. It's got a room with a table big enough to put the whole town on, and it's got a window that looks at the Rise, so we can tell what's changed. And a well by the mulberry. And I don't care if the Lauris and the Mänds don't like it. It was a good well. It's the best place to meet." She considered. "And the goats can't see into the kitchens. That's all."

There was a very long pause. Somebody at the back said, "Well, that's fair."

"Hands," said Jaan.

He had gone back to the rope. He stood with his big shoulders squared against the tower wall, and his face did not tell her anything. "One to a house. For the Rise and the girl, a hand up. For Orav and the Keeper's carts, a hand. Aino Rebane counts. Because someone must."

Aino climbed on the lowest step of the tower, with her hook slung across her back and the red scarf unwound, six feet two of salt-caravan guard, and surveyed the nine hundred of them with the look of a woman at the start of a long drive. "I count the way I count oxen," she announced. "In tens. And I name every tenth, so I don't lose my place. Hands up for the Rise. Keep them *up*."

She counted. She walked the length of the square counting, with the hook swinging, pointing a long finger at each raised hand, and her voice filled the gorge like a second bell. "Eight. Nine. *Ten*, Registrar! Twenty, Auditor! Thirty, Guildmaster!" It was a great deal more efficient than it sounded. People began laughing at the second ten and could not stop. "Fifty, Rule Forty! Sixty—"

She turned round, a little pink, and found the right word. "Sixty, *Mihkel!*"

"Present," said Mihkel Org, from the stables, and then clapped his hand over his mouth.

At a hundred and eighty she ran out of oxen and began on cabinet-makers. At two hundred she had Reet's whole attention, and Reet was holding the barrel steady with both hands and did not know that she was.

"One hundred and ninety-three for the Rise," said Aino at last. She was out of breath. "Nine for Orav. The Lauris abstain, on the grounds that Marta hasn't been consulted." And, to the crowd, in a different voice, quietly: "That's it. That's all of them."

Jaan Pold took hold of the bell-rope with both hands. He pulled it down once, steadily, with all his shoulder in it, and let it fly, and the bell of Palu spoke a single stroke over the town and the gorge: yes.

It would have been the end. That was what the stroke was for. But as the note rolled out and began to die, a small hand closed on the rope above his own, the girl with the burnt elbow, and pulled, and the bell rang again. Then her mother put a hand on it, and a man behind her, and a woman with a baby on her hip, and the old man who had said the skipping song with his eyes shut. A line of hands went up the rope like beads on a string. The bell rang and rang. It rang until the tower hummed, and the planks of the Bell Bridge, and the cables of the thirty-nine bridges after it, and the red sashes shook on the people's shoulders. Jaan could have let go. He did not. He had hold of the rope with the rest, and he was laughing, in short hard breaths, as a man laughs who has not done so in a year.

Nobody would let go of it.

Reet did not count them.
