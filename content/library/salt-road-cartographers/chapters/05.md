# Ilves Black

In the vat-house it was always night. The shutters had been nailed shut since Salme Ilves's grandfather's day, because daylight turned gall-liquor the colour of weak tea, so the lamps burned from the first bell to the last and the vats stood in their rows in the yellow dark, breathing sulphur. Toomas had smelled that breath from the corner of Tinta Row, as he had smelled it on the way to every dealing he had ever had with the Ilveses. It got into the back of the throat and stayed. It was the smell of forty years of Guild business, and it had not improved with keeping.

"Mind the third plank," he said over his shoulder. "It has been loose since the Assembly last sat."

Reet minded it. She walked behind him with her satchel held against her chest and her eyes going from vat to vat, counting, and he could have told her how many without looking: thirty-one, in four rows, each the height of a tall man and stoppered with a wooden lid on which some Ilves of the last century had burned the batch-date with a hot iron. There were no clerks. The vat-men had been sent home early, which in itself told him something, since Salme Ilves had never sent a man home in her life without being threatened first.

She was at the far end, with her sleeves pushed up and a long paddle in her fist, stirring a vat the colour of a bruise. She was short and wide and stood like a woman who has planted her feet and defied the floor to move them. Her forearms were blue-black to the elbow. Her grey hair was crammed under a leather cap, and in the corner of her mouth was a thin black cigar, burning at a pace that suggested a grievance.

"Veskimae," she said, without turning. "I heard you'd died."

"The reports were exaggerated."

"Twelve years. I sent a wreath to the Row, and the florist said you hadn't any need of it, you'd been seen buying thread." She looked round then, and her small black eyes went over him, over the stained moustache and the white eye and the chain-staff, and over Reet, and came back. "You've brought me a child."

"I have brought you a rider."

"She's the Sallik girl. With the head." Salme put down the paddle. "Sit. Not there, that's acid. Taavi!"

A noise came from behind the third row, a clink of glass, a thump, a sound of someone apologising to a shelf. A young man came round the vats at a half-run, holding a pestle like a candle. He was twenty and all angles, with a freckled face, ears that stood out like the handles of a jug, and fingertips stained the blue-grey of a pigeon's wing. Across his chest crossed a bandolier, and in every loop of the bandolier sat a tiny stoppered vial, so that when he moved he rang softly, like a small and nervous church.

"Stand up straight," said his mother. "You look like a question." She seized him by the ears, which she could reach by holding them, and kissed him on the forehead with a smack that rang off the vats. "My son. Best nose on the Row. Worst taste in waistcoats. He can tell you which oak a gall grew on, and which side of the tree the wasp was on. Say hello, Taavi."

"Hello," said Taavi. "I'm so sorry. Technically I don't know any of you."

"You will," said Toomas. "Reet, the sliver."

She took it out of the thumb of her glove with great care. It was a speck smaller than a grain of rice, a fleck of black with a dull gleam on one side. Taavi took it on the blade of a spatula, and Toomas, watching his hands, saw the hands go still. He saw Salme not look at them.

"Where did it come from?" said Taavi.

"The lower corner of a leaf," said Reet. "The new ink. It hadn't dried."

"Hadn't dried." He licked his lips. "That's, technically, not possible. Iron-gall dries in an afternoon. Even Ilves Black. Unless it's—" He stopped himself. He laid the sliver on a white tile, put one drop of water on it from a dropper, and watched the drop go dark, not at once, but in a slow spreading bloom, like smoke finding the edge of a room. Then he reached up to a shelf without looking and brought down a stoppered flask of tincture. He added a bead. The black went through a colour Toomas had not seen in twelve years, a clear violet that deepened and would not settle.

Taavi bent down. He put his nose to the tile, and then, the poor boy, his tongue to a speck on the end of his finger.

"It's, technically, an iron-gall," he said. "In the sense that it has iron and gall in it. Technically it isn't ours. I mean, it isn't anybody's. It has, technically, the right sulphur in it, but there's the salt as well, the Pale Flats salt, in proportion, and the lamp-black has been ground so fine that—" He stopped. He wiped his finger on his apron again and again. He had gone the colour of his own fingertips. "It isn't Black," he said, in an entirely different voice. "It's Founding."

The vat-house was very quiet. A lid ticked in the dark.

"Don't be a goose," said Salme.

"It's Founding grade. Nobody has made it, Mother. Not for three hundred years. It's in the book, Great-grandmother's page, you showed me once as a joke, and nobody could make it up because the base wasn't—" He stopped. "It was made by someone who *knew*. I've never smelled it in my life and I know it like a face."

Toomas looked at the sliver on the tile and thought of a stoppered jar he had once emptied, to the lees, with a steady hand and a bad reason; and he put the thought where he put such thoughts, as a man puts a coin in a boot.

"There is more," said Taavi, unhappily. He had bent over the tile again, because he could not help it. "It's got water in it. It was mixed with water, and the water was local. It's bog-iron. There's sedge ash in it, and peat, and a bit of... here." He held out the tile. "Smell."

Reet smelled. She said at once, "That's the drain behind Kalda's mill."

Taavi stared at her. "Is it?"

"Sora Crossing. It's the marsh drain. I grew up two hundred yards from it." Her voice was entirely level. "The leaf had been re-inked, and the ink was made with water from the place that was moved. It was made *there*, or the water was taken from there. Either way, the ink was made with Sora's water, and you can taste it."

"Don't," said Taavi. "Please. I don't want to."

Toomas turned his head. "Salme."

The ink-mistress had taken the cigar from her mouth. It was a very small gesture. "Rubbish," she said. "The boy has been at the sulphur. He does this. He once told me a barrel of Black was haunted."

"It was haunted," said Taavi faintly.

"I have sold the Guild forty-one barrels of Black a year for twenty-six years," said Salme, "and not a pint short, and not a drop of anything else but, roughly, the odd cask of something special years back, that's all, to a clerk, nothing like that. I've not made that stuff in my life. I couldn't make it if I tried." She put the cigar back in her mouth. "Who is the Sallik girl to tell me what I make?"

Toomas watched Taavi.

He did not know, afterwards, why he watched the boy and not the mother. It was an old habit, perhaps; in the Atlas a man learned to look for the thing that moved against the grain of the page. And Taavi had flinched. It was the smallest flinch, a tightening of the freckled face, as if a thread had been drawn through it. His mother said *roughly*, and *years back*, and *the odd cask*, and the boy's eyes went to the floor, and Toomas understood that Salme Ilves, who loved precisely and counted exactly and had never in her son's hearing said *about*, had lied. And that her son had heard it.

Taavi did not look up. He pressed the pestle to his chest. And Toomas saw him see it, and saw him decide to be a person who had not.

"Mm," said Toomas, very softly, to nobody. "A wall remembers."

The knock at the front of the works was three heavy blows.

"In the Guild's name," a man's voice said, through the door and the vat-house and the thickness of the sulphur. "Open to the Surveyor's office."

Nobody moved. Then Salme took the cigar from her mouth and said, in an entirely different voice, quick and dry and without a trace of a joke, "Dye room. Taavi, the ladder. Veskimae, you'll go over the plank, and mind your knee. *Move*."

The dye room lay behind the vats, down three steps, a long low shed lit by one skylight set in the roof, with a copper vat running the length of it, as long as a boat and full of a green so deep it looked solid. A plank crossed it at the middle, and at the far side stood a ladder to the roof hatch. Toomas looked at the plank, and then at the green, and then at his knee.

"I will not be—"

Reet had him by the arm. "Plank," she said. "Quickly."

He took two steps. The plank, which had held generations of Ilves dyers and every sort of confidence, took exception to the third. It tipped. He went in to the thigh with a sound like a cow stepping off a cliff, and the green closed round him, warm and thick and smelling of vinegar and copper, and for a moment he stood in it with his chain-staff held over his head like a man fording the Pale. Reet hauled. He came out onto the far side dripping, green to the hip, with the boots squelching and his dignity somewhere in the vat.

At the dye-room door a man in a grey coat burst through from the back lane and stopped. He had a warrant in his hand. He had the look of a man who has been told this will be easy.

Salme Ilves was behind him with a bucket.

She flung it with the whole of her shoulder. The lamp-black went through the doorway in a single dense wave, took the watcher full in the face and chest, and left him a black pillar with two white eyes, standing perfectly still. "Rule Eighty-Four," she said, "of the Ilves house: you don't come in the back." And she spat out her cigar and trod on it.

"Up," she said to the others. "Up. The roof. Go."

Reet went up the ladder like a squirrel. Toomas followed, one rung and one considered breath at a time, with the green running out of his trousers. The hatch opened on white sky, and a roof of slates, and a gutter, and then the next roof, and the next, tilting down the terraces toward the Wharf Gate like a staircase built for cats.

"Taavi!" Salme's voice, from below. "Don't be a—"

A clatter. The ladder shook. A pair of long arms arrived over the lip of the hatch, then a freckled face with its ears alight in the daylight, then the rest of him, ringing like a bell all the way out of the hatch with the bandolier tangled in his arm. He sat on the slates, panting.

"I'm so sorry," said Taavi to the sky. "I took your brass funnel, Mother, and the good indigo. I'm sorry about the indigo. Technically they're mine, the funnel was a present, but I should have—"

"Keep the funnel!" Salme shouted up from the dye room. "Keep it clean! Go!"

He looked at Reet, and at Toomas, who was green to the waist and was leaning on the chimney-pot, and Toomas saw that he did not know whether he was allowed. A boy who had waited twenty years for someone to say *come*, and who was too well brought up to ask.

"I'm not, technically, invited," said Taavi.

"You are now," said Reet.

They went down the roofs in the flat white light of early afternoon, across the terraces and along a ridge-tile and down a drainpipe that held, with the Hollow opening under them like a bowl of lamps. A watcher shouted in the lane. Nobody looked. At the bottom of the lane the Wharf Gate yawned, and the barges, and the ducks, and Salme Ilves herself, red-faced, standing at the quay with her hands on her hips, as if she had never left it.

"You went the long way," she said, and she was hardly out of breath.

"Mother, how did you—"

"I know the back streets better than the Guild knows its own Rules, which is not hard." She put her hand in the pocket of her apron and brought out a flask. It was small, stoneware, no longer than a hand, stoppered with a plug of wax and bound at the neck with red thread. She did not look at it. She pressed it into Taavi's palm and closed his fingers over it with both her own, hard, so that her knuckles whitened. "Don't drink it," she said. "Don't lose it. Don't tell me where."

"What is it?"

"A flask. Is a flask a conversation?"

"Mother—"

"Go and be useful." She kissed him on the forehead, and then, swiftly, on each of his ears, and then she turned and walked back up the lane with her short wide stride and did not look round, and the green light of the arch swallowed her.

Taavi stood with the flask in his fist. His hand was shaking. "I need to do up the bandolier," he said, to nobody, "I've lost a loop," and he held the flask out to Toomas without looking, the way a person passes the baby while he ties a shoe.

Toomas took it.

It was warm. Not the warmth of a thing left in the sun, which comes from outside; it was warm all through, as the heart of a loaf is warm, and in the stone there was a faint living pulse of it, the warmth of a thing that had been held in a hand against the body for a very long time.
