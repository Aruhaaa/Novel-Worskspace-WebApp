# Naturally

The Keeper's summons came on a card the size of a playing card, carried by a lamp-boy who would not look at her, and said in a hand like a row of pins: *Second bell. My rooms. Bring the draught.*

Reet read it on the loft landing with the smell of last night's frying still in the stairwell. She had not slept. She had lain in her bunk with her eyes open and Toomas Veskimae's shop laid out in her head, the press, the two cups, the pencil moving over the back of the endpaper, and at some hour of the morning she had found that she could not remember what he had said to her at the door. She could remember the words. She could not remember whether he had meant them kindly. It was the first time she had been unable to put a face to a sentence, and she disliked it more than she could say.

What he had said was this. *If they send for you, go, and say nothing about where you have been. If they send for you twice, run. There is a gate in the south wall where the wharves are. I am generally there at noon, feeding a duck.*

She had not asked which duck.

The Keeper's rooms lay along the vault antechamber, three small chambers in a row like beads on a string, with a door apiece into the passage. Reet knocked at the first. At the end of the passage the iron door with its three locks stood shut in the light of its caged lamp, and she did not look at it.

"Come."

It was a low room with no window but a lightwell, a fire the size of a hat, and shelves of Guild ledgers bound in the same oxblood calf as far as the ceiling. On a table covered with green baize stood a tray: a silver pot, two cups so thin that the firelight came through them. Behind the tray, with her feet not quite reaching the floor, sat a very small woman.

Kaie Tammaru could not have been five feet tall. She was the smallest Keeper in the Guild's history, and her hair, which was silver rather than grey, was plaited into one rope that came over her shoulder, lay across her lap and, when she stood, hung to her waist, thick as a wrist. Her face was thin and tired and kind, with the fine lines at the eyes of someone who had read by lamplight for thirty years. Her hands lay folded on the baize. They wore gloves: white kidskin, thin as a second skin, buttoned at the wrist with seed-pearl buttons.

"Reet Sallik. Come in, and sit, and take tea. You take tea, naturally."

"Thank you, Keeper."

"Kaie, in this room, if you will. The title is for the stairs." She poured, with the gloved hands, and did not spill a drop. "You are looking at my gloves. It is a Keeper's habit. The vellum is older than either of us, naturally, and skin is a poor thing to lay on it."

The tea was black and smoky and had a pinch of salt in it, as they took it on the Pale Flats. Reet drank some to have something to do with her mouth.

"Master Org brought me your draught last night," said Kaie. "He was a little alarmed. I told him that alarm is the correct response to good work. Your pin-sheet from Kuld came up with the frame as well. The Hallik stone." She said it as she might have said the weather. "Day-ink on a stone that is already walking is a difficult thing, I am told. I have always admired riders who can do it."

"It was only going into the lake."

"And it stopped."

"Yes."

"Naturally," said Kaie, and her gloved fingers closed on the edge of the baize and opened again. "Your draught, please."

Reet took the sheet from her satchel. It was long and narrow, with fourteen columns ruled on it in her square left-handed script, and Kaie unfolded it on the baize and smoothed it flat with her palm, as if it were something that might wake. Then she reached to a roll of green baize at her elbow and took out a pair of brass dividers.

They were old. The pivot was worn bright where a thumb had turned it for years. And one leg had a knee in it, a little above the middle, so that when Kaie stood them point-down on the paper they leaned: one leg true and the other kicked out at an angle, like a dancer's foot at rest.

Reet noticed the lean the way she noticed a wrong number, without wishing to, and put it away to look at later.

Kaie walked the dividers along the draught. She set the points on the first entry and then on the first Shift, and lifted, and set them on the next, and the steel tapped the paper with a small clean sound. "Nine nights," she said. "And nine. And nine." Each step landed on its mark. She went down all fourteen without a word, and at the end she laid the dividers on the baize, where they sat at their slant, and looked at Reet with great and genuine warmth.

"It is very exact," she said. "May I ask what you believe it means?"

"I don't know what it means. I know that it's there."

"That is an honest answer, naturally." Kaie smiled. "You have found a rhythm in the Book that no one in this House has troubled to look for in eight years. Not Master Org, who audits it for tidiness. Not I. That is a gift, and I do not use the word loosely. The Guild's habits are old and their reasons older, and a rota of re-inking kept by clerks who have long forgotten why—it is not impossible. I shall look into it myself." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer. "I should be grateful if you would speak of it to no one in the meantime. A place like this lives on rumour, and the Hollow has forty thousand people who like to think the ground is steady."

"Yes, Keeper."

"Kaie."

"Yes. Kaie."

"Good." She was still smoothing the draught with one gloved hand, in small even strokes. "May I ask you a different question? Have you spoken to Toomas Veskimae?"

It was asked so lightly that Reet had already begun to answer before she understood what she was answering.

"I— I— I have not."

The words came out of her in pieces, one after another, like beads off a broken string. She heard each one land. In the lightwell a drip of water counted four. Kaie did not look surprised. She nodded slowly, once, as a woman nods at a figure in a column that has come out as expected, and took her hand from the paper.

"Thank you," she said.

Reet's face was hot. She could have said it again, steadily, and it would not have helped; the Guild knew about her tongue. Everyone knew. She had thought of that as a harmless peculiarity, like the notch in her eyebrow, and it had never occurred to her until this minute that a thing everyone knew could be used.

"He was my teacher," Kaie said, to the teapot. "I think of him often. He was a very fine Keeper, until he was not, and I should hate to learn that anyone had been troubling him."

"Is he in trouble?"

"Not from me." She lifted her eyes. They were a pale brown, and there was nothing in them but concern. "Reet, you are tired. You have ridden every dawn for four years. A mind like yours is a lamp, and lamps are not meant to burn all day. I am giving you leave of absence, on full pay, for as long as it takes to rest. You will report to Master Org at the fourth bell each morning, so that I may be sure you are resting. And he will sit outside your door, for your protection."

"From what?"

"From yourself, naturally. You are the sort who does not stop."

"I don't want leave."

"No. Naturally you don't." Kaie rose, and the braid slid with her, and she was no taller standing than she had been sitting. "Leave the draught with me. It will be kept safe. You have extraordinary hands, Reet, and I should like to see them used well."

At the door Reet looked back. The Keeper had sat down again. She was drawing the dividers toward her across the baize, in her gloved fingers, and they walked toward her slowly, one leg after the other, with the same lean, like something that had learned to move that way and would not be corrected.

Mihkel Org was sitting on a stool outside her room in the riders' loft, with a copy of the Rules open on his knee and a sugar bun he was trying to eat with the book between it and the corridor. He half-rose, shut the Rules on the bun, and sat down again.

"Sallik. I am to—"

"I know."

"Rule Ninety. A Surveyor on leave shall be watched for her own good." He looked miserable. "I do not think it is a good Rule."

"You could have said so."

"I am a Master-Surveyor, I do not *say* so, I merely—" He stopped. "I am sorry for it. There. Rule One Hundred and Four, which I have just made up, says an apology is in order."

She nearly liked him. That was the trouble with Mihkel.

"May I fetch clean linen, Master?" she said. "Rule Sixty."

"Rule Sixty is the weight of Sheet-paper. Linen is Sixty-One." He waved the bun. "Go on. It's in the cupboard. Don't be long."

The linen cupboard stood beside her door, a tall closet of shelves and sheets that smelled of camphor and of lamp-oil, because its back wall had a hatch in it. She had known about the hatch since she was fifteen. Everyone in the loft did, and none of them admitted it. It gave on the lamp-boys' stair, a spiral cut inside the Hollow's wall, where the boys went up and down with their cans in the dark and nobody who mattered ever went at all.

She had packed the satchel before the Keeper's card came, from an instinct she had not examined. It was on the bunk. The chain hung on its peg by the door. Under the pillow lay her mother's last letter, the one about the drain, and she took that too, though she did not need it; she could have said it aloud from the date to the postscript about the plum tree. But a thing she could say was a thing only she could say, and she was beginning to understand that this was not the same as a thing being known. She took them all into the cupboard, among the sheets, and shut the door on Mihkel's voice saying, "Fresh sheets, mind, not the good ones"; and she pressed the thin onion-skin tracing flat against her ribs under her shirt, where it crackled at her breastbone like a small dry animal, and put her shoulder to the hatch.

The stair was black and narrow and smelled of oil. Her feet found the steps by habit. Below her a can clanked; a small pale face looked up at her from a landing, soot-black to the eyes.

"Sallik?"

"Ants. You haven't seen me."

"Nobody sees anybody on this stair," said Ants, comfortably. "That's what it's for. The Wharf Gate's down and left, the third landing, and the step's gone, jump it."

She jumped it. She went down for what felt like a long time, past the thin slits of light where the stair opened on the terraces, and at each one the Hollow went by in a strip: a woman shaking a rug; a stack of barrels; a dog on a roof. She tested herself as she went. Cairn. Hedge. The Guild stair, four hundred and nineteen steps. All were present. It was only the yard at the stone that was missing, a smooth hole she could feel with her mind like a tongue feels a gap.

The Wharf Gate was a broad arch in the south wall, where the Cold Stream left the Hollow through a cut in the rock. Flat-bottomed barges lay along the wharf under a tide-mark of moss, and a flock of ducks went about the stone quay with the air of a committee. Beyond the arch the daylight lay white on the plain, and the road ran out into it. The gate-sergeant, a man with a pie, had looked at the chain on her shoulder and the satchel on her back as she came through the guard-room and said, "On leave, then?" She had said yes, which was true, and he had said, "Lucky," and gone back to the pie, and she had walked on feeling that her face had been written on in large letters.

Toomas Veskimae sat on a bollard at the edge of the quay. He was in his boots, and he had a canvas bag at his feet that sagged on one side, and the chain-staff across his knees. He was breaking a crust into small pieces and dropping them to a duck, which regarded each piece as an insult.

"Bread," he said, before she had asked. He nodded at the bag. "Only bread."

"It's very heavy bread."

"It is a very good loaf."

She stood in front of him on the wet stones. There was a place in her chest where something had been wound too tight for too long, and it began, quite without her permission, to come undone.

"I stammered," said Reet.

Toomas did not look up from the duck. "I know," he said. "I heard it from the next street."
