# The Lake That Walked

The lake had moved in the night, which was ordinary. What was not ordinary was that Reet had known where it would go.

She sat the grey mare at the crest of the heath with the chain coiled over the saddle-bow and looked at the water the way a gambler looks at a coin that has come down on the called side. Yesterday Kuld Mere had lain east of Dry Cairn, a long brown sheet that went gold at sunset and had been named for it. This morning the east was bracken and a tumbled sheepfold, and the west, where yesterday there had been a drovers' track and a hawthorn hedge, held half a mile of slate-coloured water, steaming in the cold. Mist lay on it in strips. A line of fence posts marched down the track and into the lake and carried on under the surface, like a sentence nobody had told to stop.

Plover blew out through her nose and declined to be impressed.

Reet got down. Her knees complained. She had been in the saddle since the third bell, riding out from the Hollow in the dark with a satchel of blank Sheet frames and a bag of empty vials knocking at her hip. She unhooked the chain. A chain was sixty-six feet and a hundred links, and most riders on the Seam's edge used theirs, by late autumn, for tying up the horse. Reet used hers.

She began at the cairn, because everything began at the cairn. Dry Cairn was a heap of grey limestone as high as a man's belt, put up by some dead Surveyor who had understood the value of a thing that stays where it is, and it carried a brass stud in its capstone for a datum. She set the first link against the stud, took her bearing, two degrees south of west, and sent the tally-tag out across the frost.

Pacing would have taken a third of the time. She knew her pace as she knew her own name; the Guild had checked it twice against a standard rod, and the Guild did not check twice for just anyone. But there were numbers you paced and numbers you chained, and the difference was whether you meant to be asked about them afterwards. Lay the chain, stick the pin, lift the chain, walk to the pin. The frost cracked under her boots like sugar. The heather snagged her cuffs. Ahead of her the water lay perfectly flat, as if it knew it was being measured and meant to behave.

At the fortieth pin the lake was still ahead, a dark lip fringed with rushes. She laid out the odd links. Eighteen. The brass tag on the last of them touched the first wet rush and sank a little, and the water closed over its edge with a small, ordinary sound.

Forty chains and eighteen links, two south of west. To the yard.

She did not write it down. Writing was for the Hollow and for Mihkel Org's red pencil. She let the number go to the place where she kept numbers, which was large and orderly and had never lost anything in nineteen years, and she noticed, standing there with a wet cuff and a cold nose, that her hands were not quite steady and that it was not the cold.

Nine mornings ago she had said that number aloud. She had said it to a bowl of porridge in the riders' mess, after reading the Correction Book the way she read it every Thirdday, front to back, because it was public and dull and she remembered what she read. *Leaf 31*, the clerk's round hand had said. *Kuld heath, lake basin re-inked. Tidy.* Re-inking was what the Guild called the upkeep of the Atlas: vellum cracked and ink flaked, and every so often a leaf was gone over with a fresh pen, and the Book recorded it, one line at a time, for the sake of the audit. Nobody read it for pleasure. Reet had read it for four years, and twice in that time, in the privacy of her own head, where she could be as easy on herself as she liked, she had set a line from the Book beside a Shift in the ground nine nights later and called the match chance.

The third time she had not been easy. She had asked what a tidy hand would do with Kuld heath: where the lowest ground lay, where a lake could sit without drowning the drovers' track or the Hallik fields, where a clerk with a ruler and a feeling for neatness would put the shore. Forty visits to this heath had told her. She had worked it out in the time it took the porridge to cool, and then, because she wanted to hear how it sounded, she had said it.

It sounded better out here.

"Hoy! Sallik!"

Pille Raud came down the heath from the east on the piebald mule she called Biscuit, because it was hard, dry and nobody could remember choosing it. Pille was twenty-three and built like a door, with a plait down her back like a tow-rope and a nose that burned in any weather. She had a way of leaning on a conversation as though it were a gate. She reined in at the shore and looked at the lake for some time with her mouth slightly open.

"Well," she said. "That's wet."

"It's forty chains and eighteen links from the cairn."

"Is it. You'd know." Pille dug in her coat and came out with a heel of bread, which she tore at with her teeth. "Guild bread. Baked Seventhday, issued Fourthday, like they do it to hurt us. You could build a wall with it. Want some?"

"No."

"Wise. I'm eating mine as a penance." She chewed, and her eyes went from the water to Reet and back again. "You said that. At breakfast, nine days back. I remember because you said it to your porridge and I told you the porridge couldn't help."

"Nine mornings."

"And I said a week of my crusts against a week of yours that nobody could name where Kuld would land, not even you."

"Then you owe me a week of crusts."

"I owe you nothing. I haven't measured it." Pille swallowed. "Guess or luck?"

"Neither."

"Those are the only two."

"Forty chains, eighteen links, two degrees south of west," Reet said. "The northern spit against the Saar hedge, the shore along the thirty-eight-foot contour. I said it nine mornings ago and I'm saying it now, and it's the same number."

She did not stammer. Pille listened for it with her head on one side. Everyone in the riders' loft knew about Reet Sallik's tongue, which was the only piece of gossip she had ever supplied them with, and they treated it as a sort of public weights-and-measures. She told a lie and it came out in pieces, and the pieces were how you knew. Nobody laughed at it. They checked their purses by it, and asked her whether the stew was fresh.

"Huh," said Pille at last. "That's the worst of it, then. If you'd stammered I could've gone on thinking you were a normal sort of liar."

"I'm not any sort."

"I know. It's wearing." Pille wheeled the mule. "Keep your crusts. I'll do the south shore; the sheep have all gone there to be sad." She went off down the margin, still chewing, and called back without turning her head, "Don't tell Mihkel you called it. He'll want it in triplicate!"

Reet turned north along the water with a Sheet frame on her knee.

She had gone twenty paces when she heard it: a dry grinding, like a cart wheel in a rut, from a place where nothing was driving a cart.

The Hallik stone was a block of grey granite as high as her ribs, shouldered into the track where the Hallik sheep-run met the Kuld commons, with an H cut into one face and a K into the other and a century of lichen to say it had never in its life gone anywhere. Now it was going. It slid across the turf behind a bow-wave of torn heather, a hand's breadth at a time, toward the lake, and the ground around it did not stir. A yard of heath under the stone and a yard of heath behind it were on the move, and the rest of the world sat by and watched.

Reet stood very still, which was how she thought. Last night's Shift had put the lake here. It had not put the stone anywhere; the stone had been left to find its own way, and the way it was finding ran, by her reckoning, eleven feet to the water and eleven minutes. When the lake closed over it the Hallik line would be a fiction, and in a season there would be a lawsuit and two families of cousins who didn't speak. That was one way of looking at it. The other way was that something was pulling the stone, steadily, as a hand pulls on a rope, and she held that thought at arm's length and looked at it and put it down again.

"That's our stone!"

A woman was coming up the track from the farm at a run, in clogs and a sacking apron, with a milk pail swinging from one hand and her hair half pinned. She addressed herself to the heath at large. "Leelo Hallik, and that's the Hallik stone, and it's going in the lake!"

"Yes," said Reet.

"My husband's grandfather hauled that stone up from the quarry with two oxen and a quarrel. It's the line. If it goes in the lake the Kulds'll say the lake's theirs and we'll be at it till the Hollow freezes over."

"I can hold it."

"Then hold it, girl. Why are you stood there?"

"Because it holds for a night. It's day-ink. One night, no more. Tomorrow it goes where the ground wants it."

The woman looked at the stone, which was nine feet from the water now, and then at Reet. "One night's more than that stone's had in a hundred years," she said. "Hold it."

Reet knelt on the frost. She unhooked the horn cup from her belt, dipped it in the lake, which was cold enough to ache, and tipped into it a thimble of Ilves Black from the steel flask at her hip. Ilves Black was what the Guild bought by the barrel, and on its own it was only ink. Mixed with water from the ground it was meant to hold, it was something with a short temper and a long reach. She laid the pin-sheet on her knee, a hand's breadth of stiff vellum, and took up the pen with her left. Her right, steadying the cup, was dyed to the wrist already; the left, which wrote, had ink only to the second knuckle, and a good deal of rider's pride about it.

She drew the stone. A few lines: its top face, the slant of its flank, the track beside it, the nearest fence post for a reference, and the small tick of contour grammar that said *falling toward water*. It took less than a minute. The knack was to draw the thing as it was at that instant, with the stone in the middle of its stride; you did not draw where you wished it were. Then she stood, because you had to stand, that was the whole of it, with both boots on the turf and her shadow across the page, and said the words every rider learned in the first week and said a hundred times without hearing them.

"I stood here. I drew only what I saw."

The grinding stopped.

It was the quiet that startled her, more than the noise had. The stone had been mid-lurch; it finished the lurch, rocked once on its base like a man caught by the sleeve, and was a stone again, with a furrow behind it and a hand's breadth of dry heather ahead.

"Well," said Leelo Hallik, after a time. "That's all?"

"That's all."

"I'll tell them at the Hollow they're not all useless."

"Thank you, I—"

Reet closed her eyes, as she did when she wanted to be sure. It was an old habit. She walked the heath in her head from the cairn outward, yard by yard, like a woman going down a passage of lit rooms. The cairn, with frost on the capstone. Forty chains of heather, every pin. The hedge, the posts, the rim of the lake. And at the stone, one room with no light in it. She knew the stone was there. She knew the track ran past it. But the yard of turf she had knelt on, the yard she had looked at with her own eyes not a minute ago, was not in the passage at all; there was only the place where it should have been, smooth and exact, like the gap where a tooth has been, and her thought went into it again and again and found nothing to bite.

She had never forgotten anything in her life. It had not occurred to her that this was a thing a person could lose.

She opened her eyes. Leelo Hallik was peering at her.

"You've gone the colour of tallow."

"It's the c— the cold."

"Mm," said the woman, and looked at Reet's mouth for a while, as people did. "Sit on my wall and I'll bring you something hot."

She did not bring it, as it turned out. She stood beside the stone with her pail and patted it now and then, the way you would pat a dog that had come home, and Reet did not blame her.

Every other rider on the Seam's edge pinned a yard or two before breakfast and thought nothing of it. Reet found she was thinking about that. They did it every morning and never noticed what it cost, because nobody remembers a yard. She walked down to the water, stepping where the heather was whole, and crouched at the new shore with her bag open.

The last duty of any Shift was a vial. Every rider drew one at every site: a thumb-length of thick green glass, filled from the place itself, corked, sealed with a drop of wax and labelled with the date and the name. They went to the assay shelf in the Hollow, where they stood in rows by leaf number among thousands of others, and she had never known anyone to take one down. The custom was older than the rule that required it. She held the glass under the surface until it stopped speaking, wiped it on her sleeve, sealed it, and wrote on the label in her small square hand: *Kuld Mere, north shore, first light.* Then she sat with it in her palm, a very small amount of lake, and did not feel like a woman who had just done something ordinary.

She rode north with the sun coming up on her right hand and the lake steaming behind her. Plover took the drovers' track at a walk, and for once Reet let her. The Hollow's rim stood ahead as a black ragged line, with a thread of lamp-smoke going up from it straight as a plumb-line, and she went over the matter as she went over everything, in order, from the first fact.

A leaf is re-inked when it has worn, or when it has gone wrong. That was what the Guild taught: the Atlas records. The ground moves, and the Atlas follows after, one line behind, like a clerk with a pen. Everyone knew the order of things. The ground first; the record second.

But the Book had said *lake basin re-inked* nine nights before there was a lake. A clerk who is one line behind does not arrive nine nights early. And the stone, this morning, had not been dragged by a clerk. Something had held it by the rope, and she had cut the rope with a thimble of ink and nine words, and the ground had listened to her because she had stood on it and the other thing had not.

She thought of the lake, laid so exactly in the lowest ground, with a neat straight shore along the fence. Tidy. The word had been written down by someone who knew what was coming.

Plover's ears went back and then forward. Reet watched them.

"If the Book knew," she said, "then someone drew it first."

The mare walked on, which was perhaps the most sensible reply anyone had made all morning.

"Someone drew that lake," Reet told her, "before it was there."

Behind them, a long way off by now, the water went on being exactly where it was.
