# Under the Floorboards

In sixty-two years Toomas Veskimae had been whistled at by dogs, by watch-sergeants, by the wind in a hundred chimneys and once, memorably, by a heron. He had never before been whistled at by a floor.

He lay where he was on four barley sacks, with Reet's grey scarf still wound across his mouth against the dust, and considered the sound. Three notes, rising, rising, falling. It was not a rider's signal, which was two short, and it was not a lamp-boy's, which went up at the end like a question. Somebody small had made a tune of her own and was using it with the confidence of a person who has never been told that an idea was bad.

Across the barn Reet had sat up. He heard it rather than saw it, the crisp small rustle of a girl turning over in barley. She had laid her bare left hand flat on the planks and was listening with it.

"Third board from the wall," said a voice, very near, muffled by earth. It was flat and clear. "The one with the knot like an eye. I'll push. Don't pull, it sticks."

Out in the yard the rider on the door had been singing to himself for an hour, a mournful ballad about a mule who loved a widow, with more verses than the widow had earned. It covered a good deal.

The board lifted an inch, then three, with the dry complaint of a nail not asked to move in forty years, and rose clear and was drawn down out of sight. In the oblong of dark where it had been there came a faint yellow gleam, and above the gleam a smudge of face. It was a child's face, filthy to the eyebrows, with a gap in the front teeth. Her hair had been tied back with what Toomas realised, after a moment, was a length of feast-ribbon.

"Somebody was crying," said Liisu Pold. "I waited till they stopped. It seemed rude."

"That was—" said Taavi thickly, from the dark by the door. "Technically I was only—"

"It was fine. Come on."

Reet was already on her knees at the hole. "How wide is it?"

"Two feet and a bit. I measured with Peep Laan's bread-board."

"The roof?"

"Barrel staves. They hold."

"How do you know they hold?"

"I'm still alive." The gleam moved: a tin lantern, shuttered to a slit, with a stub of tallow inside. "It goes eleven yards to Peep's woodshed. Peep's got a hip and can't do the stair, and Papa's clerk weighs the tithe loaves, so Peep's was always a loaf short. So I made a back." She looked past Reet into the barn, at the long shape of Toomas on the sacks, and her face did what it had done on the bridge on the night of the feast. It took a measurement. "The big one might stick."

"The big one," said Toomas through the scarf, "has been called a number of things in his time."

"You'll stick under the wall. Breathe out."

He went last. He lowered himself through the floor feet first, like a man entering a very cold bath, and the knee spoke to him at length on the subject. Under the planks was a space the height of a hearth, floored with powdered barley and mouse droppings, where the staddle stones stood about in the gloom like a congregation of toadstools. At the far end a round black mouth had been cut into the footing and ringed with the staves of a cooper's reject. Out of it came a smell of wet clay, tallow and, quite unmistakably, yeast.

"Eleven yards," said Reet's voice ahead of him, small and flat in the earth. "And a foot. She's right."

"I'm always right about yards," said Liisu, further on. "Mind the root."

He minded the root. His hands found, one after another, the cold ribs of the staves, each worked smooth by the hands that had set it there, and he thought with some distinctness that in thirty years of Keeping the Atlas he had gone everywhere by stair, upright, in a good coat. Now, at sixty-two, he was leaving a prison on his belly, behind an ink-maker's boot-soles, down a hole that a girl of eleven had dug with a spoon. A man should not be rescued in the same posture as a dog.

But it was Helju's way. He knew it as the clay crept past his ears. She had always gone into a thing head first and without discussion: into the Hollow's cold schoolroom at nine, with her plaits like two ropes; into a marriage with a bridge-wright she had known a week; into last winter, forbidding anyone to write. Whatever her granddaughter had dug had been dug in exactly that spirit. There was no use his saying it was not safe. He had said a good deal about safety once, on a certain night, with a lamp held for him, and they had been excellent arguments.

Under the wall he stuck. The clay closed on his shoulders and the footing stones leaned on his back, an enormous patient pressure, as if the whole of Palu had decided to sit down on him, and for some moments he lay with his cheek in the cold and thought of nothing, which for him was rare.

"Out," said Liisu, from somewhere ahead, quite calmly. "All of it. Then push with your feet."

He breathed out until there was nothing left. Hands closed on his collar from the front, a small pair and a larger, Taavi's grey-blue at the fingertips, and the wall let him go with a sound like a cork.

* * *

His head came up through a trap in a floor of wood-chips into a smell of split birch, and a glow, and the first thing he saw was a pair of black buckled shoes, polished until they shone like wet slate. His eye travelled up from them. It passed over a pair of stout calves, a mustard waistcoat done up one button out, a round pale face above the waistcoat with a walnut-coloured fringe standing on end, and, held out at arm's length like a man offering a dead bird to a cat, a lantern.

"Veskimae," said Mihkel Org.

"Org." Toomas was on his hands and knees with clay in his moustache. It did not seem a position from which to say much else. "Good evening."

"Rule Forty."

"Yes?"

"Rule Forty," said Mihkel again, with a great effort at steadiness, and the light ran up and down the log wall. Reet and Taavi had got themselves upright behind Toomas, shapeless with clay, and stood against the stacked birch looking at him as one looks at a man who has put a pistol on the table. In a corner Liisu sat on an upturned bucket, eating something. "'No Surveyor sleeps unwatched in a shifting place.' It was framed in the year fourteen, after Heron Weir, and has never been amended. I have been through the Rules. Twice. By this lantern. I wish it understood that I am here under Rule Forty and under no other authority whatsoever."

"I should like to understand it," said Toomas. "Slowly. I am sixty-two."

"The Keeper has placed four persons in a barn, under Rule Forty. The barn stands in a valley which the Keeper, by her own account at supper, has entered in the Atlas as a lake. A valley so entered is a shifting place. The persons were, by my reading, sleeping, and were not watched. The rider on the door faces the yard, which I observed, and the yard is not the persons." He swallowed. "That is a breach of Rule Forty, and I am compelled to remedy it. I intend to watch you. I intend to watch you very closely. I shall be at your elbow, at a distance of no more than thirty feet, from now until the lake. That is Rule Forty."

"Technically," said Taavi, "I'm an ink-maker, not a Surveyor, so I don't think—"

"Rule Forty is to be construed generously, Master Ilves."

"You're letting us out," said Reet.

"I am watching you. Under Rule Forty."

"By way of a hole."

"Rule Forty is silent on holes."

"Org," said Toomas, gently.

"Rule Forty," said Mihkel. It came out quite quietly. He tried again, to the lantern, in a voice gone small. "Rule Forty."

"That," said Reet, "is the eleventh time."

He looked up. "I beg your pardon?"

"You've said Rule Forty eleven times since we came out. I counted."

Mihkel Org looked at her. In the shaking light the round face seemed to come a little loose at the corners, like a loaf left in the rain. "Have I," he said. "Well. It is a good Rule." And then, quite simply: "I did not know what else to say."

"He was sitting on Papa's herring heads," said Liisu, with her mouth full. "In the yard, after supper. He looked like he needed a job. I asked if he had the keys and the rota and he said what was it to me, and I said it was my yard."

"She remarked," said Mihkel, "that Rule Forty did not sound like a barn."

Toomas got himself up by the log-pile, one joint at a time. The lantern would not keep still in the Master-Surveyor's hand. Beneath one arm he held a small book bound in black oilcloth, the Rules of the Guild, one hundred and twelve, clasped as a drowning man clasps a spar. Toomas knew the look. It was the look of someone who has kept a very large clock all his life and has just been told it was telling the time to someone else.

"Did you find it, Org?" he said. "The Rule for when the Keeper is wrong."

"I looked under Keeper." Mihkel's mouth worked. "There is a Rule about her gloves. She has kept every one, Veskimae. I checked. The custody order was signed bare-handed, as Sixty-two requires. She has broken nothing. She has not broken one single Rule, and that is what I cannot—" He stopped. "I do not know what to do. I am a Master-Surveyor. I know what the Rules say. I find I do not know what they are for."

"Mm. Then do the one you have in your hand," said Toomas gently, "and see."

* * *

"Here," said Liisu.

She slid off the bucket. From between two logs she drew a long object wrapped in a flannel petticoat and held it out to Taavi in both hands, the way one offers a baby to a stranger. "It's gone hard. I wanted to eat it and I didn't."

Taavi turned back the flannel. It was a rye loaf of the heavy dark kind, split along the top and lidded with its own crust, and when he lifted the lid there was a hollow, and in the hollow a crock of grey stoneware no taller than a man's hand, stoppered and sealed with red wax. He did not take it out. He held the open loaf against his chest and shut his eyes, and said, in a voice from which every hedge had been removed, "It's all here."

"What is?" said Toomas.

"The base." Taavi opened his eyes. "Half of it. The half I kept back. I couldn't tell you in the barn, there was a man at the door with a lantern, and the flask was a decoy. Technically a half-decoy. It was half the real thing and half my family's honest black, with the clove put in so it would smell right, and then you said all those things to me, and I let you."

Toomas regarded him. He regarded the loaf, and the child with the ribbon in her hair, and Reet, who had found something of great interest in the toe of her boot. "Let me be sure I have this," he said. "In the dark, with great feeling, I made an apology."

"Yes."

"A good one."

"It was very good," said Taavi miserably.

"And the finest ink in three hundred years was at that moment in the arms of a child, in a hole, in a loaf."

"And the only one in the barn who did not know was myself."

"And Aino," said Taavi. "Aino didn't know either."

"I should like it noted," said Toomas, "that it was an excellent apology."

"It was the best I've ever had," said Taavi. "I'd like to keep it, if it's all the same."

Toomas laughed. It came out of him without warning, a dry creak like a gate not opened since spring, and Reet looked round at him as though a dog had spoken. He leaned on the log-pile and laughed until the knee hurt, and went on a little after. "Keep it," he said. "Keep it, you terrible boy."

* * *

They went round by the lane, in single file, along the foot of the yard wall, Mihkel in front with his lantern held high as men carry them at funerals. The wind had got up in the gorge. Palu was never silent at night. Its forty bridges worked at their cables like ships at anchor, and the whole town ticked and groaned and sighed in the dark, taking every sound that was made in it and giving it back as a piece of the creaking. Toomas had loved that, at twenty.

At the yard gate a rider sat on a stool with a cloak over his knees. He was on his feet before the lantern reached him.

"Rider Joosep. Rule Sixty-six: horses are counted at the third bell. It is past the fourth." Mihkel let that sit. "You will take Rider Anu from the barn door. It is a two-man count. The south lip, twice, and write the number down."

"They're all there, sir. I saw them tied."

"Then it will be a short count, and you will have the pleasure of being right. Go."

They went, arguing amiably down the lane over whether a mule counted. Toomas could not remember a Rule Sixty-six about horses, but he could not remember most of them, and Mihkel had never in his life forgotten one. The singing at the barn door stopped, and the quiet that came after was larger than the singing had been.

The gate opened before Mihkel could touch it. Jaan Pold stood behind it with a lantern hooded under his coat and, in his other hand, a hammer with a head the size of a loaf. A cold chisel was thrust through his belt.

"In," he said. "Quiet."

The yard had gone to charcoal and silver under a sky with no moon: the rail over the gorge, the upturned buckets, the grain store squatting on its stones with nobody at its door. At the far corner stood the reeve's grain cart. Against its near wheel, with her long legs out before her and a chain round her ankle, sat Aino Rebane, and she was whispering. She was giving a speech about the Deputy Assistant Surveyor of Latrines, in a low and level and devoted voice, and she did not stop when they reached her. She said, in the same breath, without turning her head, "You took your time."

"Hold the wheel," said Jaan.

Reet held the wheel. Jaan knelt, laid a folded grain sack over a link of the chain and set the chisel on it, and then did a thing Toomas had not seen a man do since he was a boy in Palu himself. He waited. He tilted his head toward the gorge. A moment later a gust came up out of the dark and every bridge in the town groaned together, forty ropes taking the strain at once, and Jaan said, "Wind," and struck.

The blow went into the groan and was gone.

"She came to me at the second bell," he said, in the gap, so low that Toomas had to lean to hear it. "With the map. Hers." He listened. "Wind." He struck again. "And the clerk's. The lake. Laid one on the other with a rule."

"And?"

"My porch step's forty feet down." The third gust came, and the hammer, and the link parted with a sound like a dropped coin. Jaan sat back on his heels. He was not looking at Toomas. He was looking at the chain. "I can argue with a Keeper. I can't argue with my own front step."

"Jaan—"

"Not now, Veskimae."

"No," said Toomas. "Of course."

Jaan only got to his feet and held out one big yellow-palmed hand, and when Aino took it he hauled her up as if she were a sack of meal.

"Right," said Aino. She stamped each foot in turn. She rubbed the wrist, which was raw. "I'd like it noted that I wasn't frightened for a moment, and that I've named every man in that yard after somebody at the Guild, and that I need my hook, and an egg, and the privy, in that order."

"Item," said Mihkel.

From beneath his cloak he produced a canvas bag, and from the bag, one by one, with the air of a man reading out a will: a long salt-hook, its steel dark with oil; a chain-staff; a leather satchel; a surveyor's chain wound tight in its brass cuffs. "Held in my tack-room under Rule Fifty-one, which provides that impounded implements shall be kept dry and sharp. They have been kept dry and sharp."

Aino took the hook. She ran her thumb down the edge. "You oiled it."

"Rule Fifty-one."

"You great round—" She stopped. Toomas watched her search for the officer, and not find him, and give it up. "Thank you, Mihkel," she said. She had said the name of the man, not the ox, and it sounded strange in her mouth, and she let it.

The chain-staff was warm from the bag. He took it in both hands. The knee felt, at once, that it had been given a friend.

* * *

"Then what?" said Jaan.

They had drawn together at the end of the yard, under the eaves of the reeve's house, where the platform of black timbers jutted over the gorge and the floor hummed beneath their boots. Mihkel told them what he knew, standing very upright, as he might have reported to a board. The Keeper slept in the east room, at the reeve's own invitation, over the gorge.

"The best room in the house," said Jaan, to no one. "It hums."

She rose by habit at the fourth bell. She would ask the reeve for her answer at first light, as she had said, and thereafter ride up to the upland camp to see to her carts, with eight of the twelve, and she would not be back before noon. She had told Mihkel so herself, and had left the disposition of the prisoners to him.

"Then I'll say noon," said Jaan. "I'll say the town answers at noon. She'll like that. It's tidy." He glanced at Mihkel, who did not flinch. "I ring after she's gone. Second hour of day."

"And then the town," said Reet. She was looking at the east, where nothing yet showed, only a deeper black along the top of a ridge. "All of it, on East Rise, by the afternoon. With Taavi's ink, and every household standing where its house will be. They'll want to know why. I'll tell them." She stopped. "I've never told nine hundred people anything."

"No," said Toomas.

They all looked at him.

"Before you tell them a word of ink or Atlases, I shall tell them what I did. In the square. Standing up." He had his two hands on the chain-staff and his weight on them. "I should rather they heard it from me than from a clever girl who has been kind enough to leave it out. If they are to stand on a hill and trust her with their houses, they must first know what sort of man is standing beside her. Mm?"

Reet did not argue. She had spent a good part of the last week arguing with him about the order in which true things ought to be said. She looked at him, a small exact girl with clay to the elbows, and she nodded once.

"They'll spit," said Jaan.

"I should think less of them if they did not."

* * *

They crossed the Bell Bridge to the tower, because the bell was there and because the reeve wished to be with his rope. It was two hundred and ten feet of oak and cable over a drop of two hundred, and the planks answered every step like the keys of an instrument. At the north end Aino stopped.

"That one's the thirtieth," she said.

"Thirty-first," said Reet. "You did the Cutler's span at dusk."

"I did not."

"You did. I counted."

"Count loud, then." Aino took a handful of Reet's sleeve in one fist and the salt-hook in the other. She was grey about the mouth. "Count out loud and don't stop and don't be clever."

"Yard one," said Reet. "Yard two."

She counted them across. She counted in a plain, even, small voice, a yard at a time, and Aino went over behind her step by step with her eyes open, which Toomas thought a braver thing than any hammer. Taavi followed with the loaf. Mihkel, a pace behind, said "Rule Forty" once under his breath and appeared startled to have done it.

At yard thirty-five, which was the middle, Toomas stopped.

Nobody asked why. He put his hand on the cable. The little map was still there, pinned by its loop of string with a pebble sewn into each corner, swinging in the draught: the bell-tower with its window turned south, the forty bridges each with its small drawing, the mulberry in its corner, and in the margin, in a clumsy careful hand, *PALU. Liisu Pold. Her map.* He had stood here on the night of the feast and seen it hang there, and thought, with great clarity: *and I nearly.* He thought of his sister under her white stone. He thought of the porch step.

Below him the gorge was filling with grey. The black ridge in the east had a rim to it now, a thread of something paler than itself, and above the ridge the stars were going out one at a time, like lamps put out by a patient hand.

"I have never been so frightened," said Toomas, "or so glad."
