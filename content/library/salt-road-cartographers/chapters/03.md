# A Binder in Tinta Row

The thread was wrong again. Toomas Veskimae held it up to the lamp and watched it spin on its own twist, which good linen thread did not do, and told the empty shop what he had told it every Fifthday for forty years.

"Six-ply, waxed. Is that so much? Is a man asking for the moon, or for a thing that was in every shop on the Row when I was a boy?"

Nobody answered, because nobody was there. The shop answered in its own way: the press ticked as it cooled, the glue pot sighed, and the ledger on the sewing frame lay open on its cords with eleven years of herring in it, waiting. Lill & Sons, Smoked and Otherwise. The leaves smelled so strongly of fish that a cat had waited outside the door all afternoon in a spirit of faith. Toomas had been asked to make the thing look important. He had been trying for three days, with a bad needle, a worse thread and a right knee that had opinions about the stool.

He leaned on the frame and rubbed the knee. It was a good knee, as knees went, and it had served him for sixty-two years. It had also served him on the Guild stair, once, when it was not required to, and had never quite forgiven him. His chain-staff stood in the corner by the door. He used it to get about and not to measure, and he had not measured anything with it for twelve years.

The tea at his elbow had gone cold and grown a skin. He drank it anyway, on principle, the principle being that heating it would mean standing. Over the stove stood the two cups, where they stood every evening when he lit the lamp. He drank from one of them. The other stood mouth-down on its saucer all day, and he told nobody why. A man might lay a cup for a visitor who had never come; there was no law against it, though in Tinta Row, which was the street of ink-sellers and binders and had laws against most things, he sometimes looked for one.

No letter had come at Midwinter, either. His sister Helju wrote every Midwinter, one page, her cramped hand going downhill at the end of every line, to say that the bridges had held and Jaan's boy had a cough. This year there had been nothing. The post was slow, he had told the second cup. It was a bad road, in the Seam.

The knock was three raps, a pause of exactly equal length, and three more.

Someone who counted. He felt it in the back of his neck before he had set the needle down.

"It's open. It is always open. A binder who locks his door has something to bind."

The girl who came in was five feet tall, with cropped dark hair that had been hacked at by survey shears, a notch in her right eyebrow and a satchel strapped across her like a bandolier. She was ink to the wrist on both hands, and there was a brass plumb-bob on a cord at her neck. She looked at him once, quickly, and then at the room, and he watched her take it in, from the press to the cups, and put it all somewhere. She did not look at his left eye. That was manners, or training.

"Master Veskimae?"

"Binder," said Toomas. "Master of nothing since the year three hundred. Boards, spine, a title in gilt? I do not do love-letters."

"I'm Reet Sallik."

"I know who you are."

She had not expected that, and he took a small mean pleasure in it, and put it away again. "You are the girl the Guild keeps in a drawer and takes out for the Assay Fair. They rang a little bell for every mistake. I stood at the back and counted the bell."

"It didn't ring."

"No. That is why I counted." He picked up the awl. "I am sewing herring, Rider. If you want something bound, I am at your service on Thirdday."

"It's about the Correction Book."

The awl stopped.

"Fourteen entries in eight years," she said. She had not sat; she stood with her satchel held in front of her, and her voice had gone quick and level, like something read from a slate. "Each a leaf re-inked. Tallow Moor, the north hill. Kask Ford, the river bend. Sora Crossing, the marsh drain. Kuld heath, the lake, yesterday morning. Nine nights after each entry, the ground moves to match what the leaf says. Nine nights, fourteen times. And yesterday evening the Book says Leaf 163, the Palu valley, re-inked." She stopped for air. "Nine nights after yesterday."

"Go away," said Toomas.

"I haven't—"

"You have said enough. Correction Book. Atlas leaves. Go away, Rider. I am a bookbinder and a bookbinder is a wall, and a wall remembers who leaned. I have leaned all I mean to."

She did not go. She stood on his worn boards with the lamp on the side of her face, and the thing he noticed was that she did not look hurt. She looked as though someone had told her a number she could use.

"I know you were Keeper," she said. "I know you were dismissed over a lost leaf. I know what the Guild says and I've never been able to find what's true. So I'll tell you something else, and you can throw me out after." She closed her eyes. "Soo valley, from the last Dawn Sheet before the flood."

He said, "Don't."

"The stream comes in at the north-east corner at the hundred-and-forty-foot contour and runs south-west in three bends. At the second bend there's a ford of nine stepping stones, and the fourth stone is a different grey from the others. The mill's on the west bank, forty yards off the stream, with a race cut from the first bend; the race has three sluices, and the middle sluice is drawn crooked. There are fourteen roofs below the mill, in a curve like a hand cupped, and the ash tree stands at the cupped place with a bench under it. Above the village the slope goes up in four steps. The first has a stone wall—"

"Stop."

She stopped. She opened her eyes. Her mouth was slightly open, and she was a long way off, in a valley.

Toomas found that the awl was shaking in his fingers, the point jittering on the board like a bird's foot. He laid it down very gently, where it would be less honest. He did not say what he knew. He had not said it in twelve years to anyone, and he did not say it now to a child with ink to her elbows who thought she was giving him a credential. But he could see it. She had drawn it back into his skull like a nail, the ash tree and the bench and the crooked sluice, and the worst was that she had it right. She had the fourth stone right.

"How old were you?" he said.

"Sixteen. It was in a bundle of waste Sheets in the Day-book cellar, lining the bottom of a drawer. They burned the rest."

"Sixteen." He drew a breath through his moustache. It rattled. "And you remember all of it."

"I remember everything I've ever looked at." She said it flatly, like a fact about the weather. "It isn't an accomplishment. It's an ear."

He did not answer that. He took a pencil from behind his ear and a spoiled endpaper from the waste-basket, and turned it over to the plain side, and began to draw.

It was Palu. He did it from memory, which was a good memory, hung with the dust of twelve years: the gorge, the river at the bottom of it, the forty bridges strung across the gap like washing, the bell-tower, the long bare ridge on the east side where goats grazed. He drew fast, with the pencil held like a chisel, the way he had once drawn a hundred things a day for the Atlas, and as he drew he planted his mistakes. He put the sixth bridge on the wrong side of the bell. He gave the river an extra bend. He put fifty bridges where there were forty. He added a well where there had never been a well and left the mill off altogether, and when it was done he pushed the sheet across the bench without a word.

She read it the way he had seen a very good musician read a score, with her head still and her eyes going. "There are forty bridges," she said. "You've fifty. That's a flourish, I think. The sixth is on the north side of the tower, not the south. The river doesn't have that bend, and the well is the old well, which was filled in after—" She frowned. "Eight years ago, I think. The mill's on the third terrace. And the ridge." She tapped the east side with her left forefinger. "You've drawn it bare. There's a birch hanger on the north flank. It was planted nine years ago."

"I did not plant it."

"No. But it's there."

"And the tower?"

"The tower is what?"

"I left it as it was." He heard his own voice. "I put it facing east."

She looked at the sheet again. For the first time she looked uncertain. "It faces south. They rebuilt it. It was in the Sheet for—"

"I know it was." He had not known. He took the paper back, and looked at his own tower, facing the wrong way, and the girl who had found the four mistakes he had put in and a fifth that he had not known was a mistake. A sensation went through him that he did not quite know what to do with. It was delight, at the plain, extravagant fact of her. And behind the delight, like the shadow of a wall behind a lamp, something else. He knew what the Guild did with instruments. He had been an officer of the Guild.

"Mm," he said. "Say that shorter."

"The Guild's been using me as a party trick."

"Better."

She was the Atlas, he thought, if one cared to put it that way: a living leaf, in a satchel, with a notch in her eyebrow. He wished he did not.

She had gone to her satchel and was drawing out a folded piece of onion-skin so thin it clung to her fingers. "This is Leaf 88," she said. "Sora Crossing, the lower margin. I traced it yesterday in the copy room." She spread it on the bench. "Look at the pinhole. The dividers always go in five millimetres from the gutter. Always. When I traced this leaf two years ago it was five. On Leaf 88 yesterday it was four."

He looked.

He found, to his considerable annoyance, that his eye went straight to the four and sat there, like a dog on a doorstep. A pinhole. A small circle of pricked vellum on a sheet of paper that weighed less than a breath. He had made a hundred thousand such holes, and had seen them go in, and had never in forty years thought of one as a signature.

A tool with one leg bent, he thought. It would not lie straight in its box. A girl he had trained, a quiet girl with quiet hands, who had dropped her dividers on the Guild stair in her first winter and refused to have them mended, because she said the slant suited her. He did not say the name. He did not say it even in his head, which he found took a good deal of effort, like holding a door shut against a wind.

"There's more," said Reet. "At Kuld, yesterday morning. There was a boundary stone moving, being dragged, as if there were a rope on it. I drew it in day-ink with the lake's water and swore the oath, and it stopped. Dead." She drew a hand across her eyes. "It cost me a yard."

"A yard of what?"

"Of what I knew. I can't remember it." She said it very evenly. "I've never forgotten a thing in my life, Master Veskimae, and I have spent a day and a night walking round the place where one yard used to be."

He did not move. He sat on the stool with the awl before him and the herring on his lap, and the lamp lit the side of his face where the eye was clear and the side where it was white, and he thought of a valley with nine hundred people in it. A town costs everything. Nobody had told her that. He was not going to be the one to tell her tonight.

"Then you have found it out already," he said, slowly. "A far hand can only be beaten by a near one. Whatever is done at a distance, by a hand in a drawer, is only a rumour of the ground. A hand that has stood on it is a witness. A witness beats a rumour. Your stone knew it." He rubbed his knee. "Someone must stand in Palu."

"I told Mihkel Org."

"And what did he say?"

"Rule Eleven." She gave a short humourless breath. "The rider reports, the Keeper interprets. He said it twice. Then he went to find his pastry."

"Ah."

"There isn't anyone else." She said it to the floor, quickly, as if it were a debt. "There's the Rules, and there's him, and there's the Keeper, and the Keeper is—" She did not finish it. She raised her grey eyes to him, and they were not a child's. "I've nobody else to tell."

Nine nights, he thought. Nine nights was a hand's width of time, and a girl with a perfect memory and nowhere to put it, and a valley he knew better than he knew the back of his own hand. His sister's valley. His sister, who had not written at Midwinter.

He got up. It took a moment; the knee had to be consulted. He went to the stove, and picked up the kettle, and found it light, as it had been for twelve years, and went on holding it.

"Nine nights," he said. "Sit down. How many are left?"

Reet sat. She sat on the edge of the customers' stool as if it might be taken from her, with the satchel on her knees, and her ink-dyed hands closed on the strap.

"Eight," she said.

And Toomas Veskimae, for the first time in a very long while, filled the kettle at the jug and set it on the ring, and took the second cup from the shelf and turned it the right way up.
