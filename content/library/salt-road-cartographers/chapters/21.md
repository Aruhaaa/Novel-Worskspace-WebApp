# Everyone Stands

The goats reached East Rise an hour before anyone could account for them.

They came up the cart-road in a brown stream, nanny and billy and kid, with their bells clanking, and spread over the bare turf at the top as if they had been told to, nosing at the thistles; and behind them, in no order at all, came Palu. It came with stools. It came with cradles on its heads and grandmothers on door-planks, carried four to a plank like kings, and bread under its arms and kettles in its fists and a bridge-wright's whole family in a hay cart pulled by a donkey who regretted it. A man had brought his bed. He carried it up on his back without comment and set it down at the edge of the ridge and sat on it. Someone had brought the feast drum. By noon the ridge had a town on it, nine hundred strong, crowded and loud and disputatious, breathing hard in the thin air, with a cold wind going through it from the east.

Reet stood in the middle of the bare ground with her chain in her hand and looked at it.

"It's very big," said Taavi, beside her.

"It's six hundred and forty yards by two hundred and ten," said Reet. "At the widest. It's not big. It's a small, steep, windy ridge with a birch wood on one end, and nine hundred people are going to live on it." She heard her voice come out brisk and exact and was grateful for it. "Is the ink ready?"

"Technically it's the best ink in Orrin." He had a copper wash-boiler set on three stones over a fire of birch twigs, borrowed from Mistress Saar with her washing still in it, which had been removed with ceremony and was hanging from a goat. "I mean, in three hundred years. It's the best ink in three hundred years. I'm sorry, that's a ridiculous thing to say, but I made it, and I stood on the Rise at six this morning and dipped a cup in the seep in the birch hanger, and it's the place's own water, and it went in as if it had been waiting."

He held up his thumb. The nail was black. It was not the dull black of Ilves Black, which looks like soot, but the black of wet slate and deep wells and the inside of a closed eye, and as she looked it dried, in the length of one breath, into a hard even gloss like glass. Taavi scraped it with the nail of the other thumb. It did not so much as dull.

"It's going to take," he said. He looked at his thumb as at a child that has said its first word. "It's going to take, Reet."

"Everybody brings a cup," said Liisu.

She had appeared at Reet's elbow, which she did. She had a tin cup in her hand, and in the other a sheet of yellow vellum as big as a table-top, rolled and tied with a ribbon, which she carried as though it were hot. "Of their own water. From their own well. Taavi says the ink wants the place's water, and the place is all of us. So everyone brings a cup, and tips it in."

Reet looked at Taavi, who looked at his boots. "She asked," he said. "She asked if it would work and I said, technically, it couldn't hurt."

So it was Liisu's idea, the line. Within the hour there was a queue at the wash-boiler that went the length of the ridge, the longest Palu had ever stood in willingly: each household came up with a cup, a jug, a thimble, a wet palm, from the Bell Bridge well or the lower well or the rain-butt or the Wet Stair spring, and tipped it in over Taavi's steady spoon, and said whose it was. "Lauri, the well by the mulberry." "Mänd, the cooperage tank." "Tamm, the goat trough," which drew a look from the widow Saar. And Taavi, who had been apologising to every customer in advance since he was fourteen, said "Thank you, it's wonderful, I'm so sorry about the smell," and went on stirring.

The vellum was the Charter of Palu. Jaan had brought it up from the chest in the reeve's house and handed it to his daughter without a word. It was a calfskin four feet by three, with the great seal of the Guild hanging off the foot of it in green wax, granting the town of Palu the bridges of the gorge in perpetuity, in the year one hundred and fourteen. On the other side there was nothing. Liisu unrolled it face down on the turf and weighted its corners with four stones, and the clean back of it lay in the sun like a field after snow.

"Right," said Reet. She took a breath, a plain one, to the bottom of her lungs. "Aino."

"Ma'am."

"I need stakes."

"You need *iron*," said Aino. She was already at the cart. She hefted a sheaf of iron tent-pegs on her shoulder and took a mallet in the other hand. "Where do you want the first?"

Reet looked at the ridge. She did not know. She had stood on a thousand yards of ground in her life and known where it was in her sleep; she had walked into the Seam with a map in her skull that no one else on earth could read. And this, which was bare, which had nothing on it but turf and wind, she could not tell. She had no idea where Palu should be.

"Here," said a voice.

It was Old Ruus. He was standing a few yards off on a patch of grass no different from any other, with his hat in his hands and his boots together, like a man at a graveside.

"Here's my gate," he said. "I'd have the field behind me, down that way, falling off to the east. There's a thorn hedge on the north side of it, and a ditch, and the gatepost is split oak with an iron ring, and there's a stile of two flat stones. Fourteen steps from the gate to the house. That's a long way, I know, but I like the walk." He turned his hat. "You drew it once, I hear. Before you fainted. Seems a pity not to have it."

She had drawn it. She knew she had, the way one knows one has been in a room; the field was a clean cool blank where it should have been. She did not remember a thorn hedge. She did not remember a stile.

"Say it again," said Reet. "Slowly. I'm writing it down."

She wrote it. She wrote what he said, and not what she knew: *gate, here; field behind, E; hedge N, thorn; ditch; post, split oak, iron ring; stile, two flat stones*. She found it was not the same as remembering. It was better. It was a thing that someone else had given her to hold, and she would be able to give it back.

"The first stake," said Reet. "Ruus's gate."

Aino drove it. Three strokes of the mallet and the pin stood in the turf, a hand-span of black iron in a green field, and she leaned on the mallet and looked at it and said, to nobody, "It's still just so we know where we woke up. Only tonight we say it first."

* * *

The Keeper came at noon.

Reet heard her before she saw her, hooves on the cart-road and the clip of shod horses on scree, nine of them, and straightened from the vellum with the pen in her hand and a spray of ink down her wrist. She saw the heads turn along the ridge. It spread like a ripple, as the news of a death does in a market, and the voices went out of the crowd, and the line at the wash-boiler stopped with a cup half-tipped.

They came over the lip of the Rise at a walk, eight riders in grey coats, and in front of them on a pale grey horse a small woman with a silver braid. She drew rein at the edge of the people and sat looking.

Mihkel Org stepped in front of her horse.

He did it quite suddenly, from nowhere, with his hat in his hand and a crumb of pastry on his waistcoat. He did not raise his voice. "Keeper," he said. "Rule Forty."

"Master Org." Kaie Tammaru looked down at him. She was wearing the white kidskin gloves; she sat with her hands folded on the pommel, buttoned at the wrist, and she did not look surprised, or angry. She looked as she had looked in the reeve's dining room, which was attentive. "I understood the prisoners to be in a barn."

"They are in my sight, Keeper. I am watching them. Rule Forty does not require that the watching be done in a barn." His round face was the colour of cold porridge, and his hat was shaking, and he did not move out of the way. "I have not lost sight of any of them for a moment. I shall not do so. You will find that I am extremely thorough."

She considered him for some seconds. Then she looked past him, over his shoulder, at the Rise.

Reet watched her do it. She saw the small head turn, slowly, as a heron's does, and take in the ridge: the copper boiler smoking over its birch fire; the queue of cups; the stakes, in a ragged line like a fence not yet put up; the grandmothers on their planks and the bed on the edge of the world; the goats; the nine hundred people, standing, with their eyes on the ground, as if they had been put there as seed.

"What," said Kaie, "are they doing?"

"Standing, Keeper," said Mihkel, with honesty.

"Mm," said Toomas Veskimae, from the stake line. He was a hundred yards away, leaning on his chain-staff. He had not raised his voice either. It carried on the wind. "It's the hard part, I'm told."

For a time nothing happened. Then Liisu Pold put down the corner of the Charter, walked across the turf with her hands at her sides and stopped in front of the pale horse. She was carrying a milking stool. She set it on the ground at the horse's feet, and looked up.

"That's for you," said Liisu. "It's guest-right. Papa says."

The Keeper looked down at the child. In the long pause Reet could hear the wind in the birch hanger and, somewhere, a goat. A gloved hand had left the pommel and was pressing the other, hard, as if to hold it still.

"Naturally," said Kaie.

She got down. She sat on the stool, in her white gloves and her spotless riding habit, with her small back very straight, and somebody, a woman Reet did not know, came and put a mug of milk in her hands. She held it. She did not drink. She lifted two fingers from the mug and the eight riders behind her stood easy.

She sat there the whole afternoon. It was a good deal longer than a moment.

* * *

By the third hour Reet had stopped thinking in words.

She worked by household, in the order they came, Ruus's gate and then the Lauris', and then the Mänds', and then whoever came forward out of the crowd. She asked each of them to stand on the ground where their door would be. They did it with their feet, shyly, shuffling a little on the turf like children on a stage. *Here's our door. Here's the hearth. We'd want the window east, for the light.* A boy of seven put his heels together and said, with enormous seriousness, "Here's where I sleep." Four chainmen, borrowed from Mihkel's riders, who had received the order in the form of a Rule, took the measure to the nearest stake. She wrote the number. Jaan walked behind her with the second chain, and Aino drove stakes, and the line of pins ran down the Rise in a curve like the track of a snake, and it was the strangest survey that she had ever worked, because there was nothing there. There was only what they meant.

She drew on the back of the Charter with Liisu on her knees at the other side of it, holding the edge flat with both hands and her chin. The ink flowed off the nib like silk. It was a pleasure, a distinct one, of the kind she had not felt since she was fifteen: a line laid down the first time right. House by house the plan grew on the vellum in black, the long street of the Rise and the cross-lane and the square; and at her elbow Toomas squatted on his bad knee and fed her the contour grammar, murmuring, as he had once murmured it to a boy at the Guild school. "Short tick for a gentle fall. Mm. Longer here. The ticks go down."

"Mine go up," said Liisu.

"In the Guild's grammar, they go down."

"That's wrong." She considered the page. "It doesn't matter. The ground can read."

Reet drew the ticks Liisu's way, uphill, in the margin, and in the field below the stile of two flat stones, and the ink took them. It took everything. It lay down on the calf and was gone into it, glossy and set. She had never worked with ink so honest.

Only once did it refuse. She had set the line of the goat shed on the common boundary of Ivo Tamm and the widow Saar, as both had said, firmly, in a pair of voices that sounded like a quarrel in the next room, and the line beaded. It drew back on the vellum into a row of shining black drops, like water on a greasy plate, and would not lie.

"Oh," said Taavi, from the pot, aghast. "That's not— I tested it. It's not the ink."

"Mm." Toomas bent over the page. "It is not the ink. Reet. Do they mean it?"

She looked up. Ivo Tamm was scratching his neck. The widow Saar's mouth was a hard line. Between them, on a length of rope, the goat watched the two of them with its flat yellow eye.

"A promise you don't mean," said Toomas, very low, "is a lie told forward. The ink knows."

There was a pause which seemed long. Then Ivo Tamm cleared his throat. "She can have the shed," he said. "I'll have the goat. And I'll build her a new one, out of my own larch, on her side, with the door the right way up." He did not look at the widow. "I'm tired of it, Marit. Nine years."

"I don't want the shed," said the widow Saar. She was crying, and furious about it. "I want it to stop eating my washing."

Reet drew the line again. It lay down like a cat.

At the edge of her sight, all afternoon, she was aware of the stool: the small straight figure on it, with the white hands in her lap, and the mug going cold on the ground at her feet. The Keeper said nothing. Once, towards four o'clock, when the plan was half done, Reet looked up and met her eyes across the whole width of the ridge. Kaie looked, as one looks at a very bad letter in one's own handwriting, and did not look away.

* * *

The sun went down behind the Palu gorge in a smear of red, and the wind dropped, and the ridge was quiet.

The drawing was done. It lay on the grass between four stones, black on yellow, a town that had never been, with its streets and its squares and its schoolhouse, its thirty-one stones behind a bell-tower, and its mulberry, and its well; and at its foot, below a small cut in the north flank where a seep would run, a line of little double strokes that were bridges. Not forty. Twelve. The longest was ninety feet. Around it, in an irregular ring, in the light of the dying sun, stood every living soul in Palu, each on the spot where their door would be.

She had not known that they would all do it. She stood in the middle of them in the cooling grass and her legs began to shake.

"Reet," said Toomas, gently. "The oath."

The oath on the Hearth-Stone had been cut for one. *I stood here. I drew only what I saw.* She had said it over a boundary stone and over a barley field, and she had known, without needing anyone to tell her, that it would not do. There was a thing they had not seen. There was a thing that they meant.

She knelt by the vellum. She dipped the pen. In clear square capitals, at the foot of the plan, in the true ink, she wrote what she had worked out in the tower between the fourth bell and the fifth, with Toomas saying *shorter* at every draft: *We stood here. We drew what we saw. We drew what we mean to make.*

"Say it," said Reet. She stood. Her voice was not loud, but it carried across the quiet like a thrown stone. "All of you. Say it the way you mean it. If you don't mean a word, don't say it."

Liisu said it first, in her flat voice, with her hands at her sides. Then Jaan, and then Aino, in a roar. Then a swell of voices, nine hundred, ragged, cracked, in four keys and a dozen accents, all saying it at once. *We stood here. We drew what we saw.* There were old women saying it with their hands pressed to their chests and children saying it with their eyes on their feet, and it was not an oath of any Guild, nor any king. It was a ridge full of people telling the truth about what they were going to do.

*We drew what we mean to make.*

She said it with them. She felt the old hitch in her throat coming for her, the little catch before the stammer, and it did not come. It had never been for this. She said it level and clean and unhurried, and under her feet the whole ridge breathed.

The ink took.

She saw it go. Every line on the vellum, from the great square to the smallest stake-mark, lost its wetness at once, the shine going in and a deeper black coming up like a skin of glass, and Taavi, who was crouching by the Charter with his thumb out, made a small high sound, like a kettle taken off the fire.

"It's in," he whispered. "It's gone in. Oh— it's in."

Silence. The wind did not blow. Nobody moved, nine hundred people with their mouths open; only the goats went on with their grazing, in the dusk, with a small tearing noise.

"It will not hold against the Atlas."

The Keeper had stood up. The milking stool lay on its side. She had not raised her voice. She stood in her riding habit with her hands clasped before her, alone at the edge of the people, small and straight as a pin, and she was not threatening them; she was reporting it. "Naturally it will not, Surveyor. The leaf is drawn. I have done this eight times. I know what lands. At midnight the lake will go where it is written, and whatever stands on its path will be under it."

"It doesn't have to hold against the Atlas." Reet's own voice sounded like someone else's, level and tired. "It has to be nearer. The leaf is in a drawer in the Hollow, nine nights old. This is under their feet." She looked at the small figure. She had meant to say nothing else. "I found the line, you know. The wrong one. In your sum."

"Which?"

"The first. You wrote that the Seam has to land somewhere. It doesn't. It lands on what nobody holds. You never asked the Hollow to hold it."

"The Hollow has forty thousand people," said Kaie, after a moment.

"Yes."

"They would have to stand on every step."

"Naturally," said Reet.

The Keeper's mouth moved. Something crossed the small face and was gone. Then she bent, and righted the stool with a gloved hand, and sat on it again, and put her hands in her lap.

Reet turned back to the Charter.

At the foot of the plan, below the oath, a space had been left. She knelt and took the pen. Her hand was black to the wrist; the ink was drying in the creases of her knuckles. She wrote the first letter, the *R*, and as the pen left it the first of it began to go: not with a blow, but with a sort of unthreading, like a cord drawn through a hundred beads. The long white road to the Blank. The pass. Dry Cairn, its brass stud, the datum. It went from her like warm water from a cup. She could feel it go, and there was nothing to be done.

She stopped, with the pen on the vellum. She looked at the one letter, and the space where more should go. It was her name. She knew that it was her name, and that she had signed it ten thousand times; but she could not have said what it felt like. It was as though it were a word in a language she had spoken as a child and had put away.

*Sallik*, she thought. *Sora Crossing*. A marsh, a drain, a cellar, a woman's voice.

She could not see it.

She wrote the rest. She wrote her whole name, *Reet Sallik*, with her left hand, to the second knuckle, in letters so small and plain that anyone could read them.
