# Haldja Ford

The river had moved a hundred yards east since last month, and Veikko Pent's ferry had not.

Toomas saw the whole of it from the saddle of the pony Clerk, in the last copper light of the fifth day, as the Salt Company's wagons came down off the heath in a long creaking file. There was the landing stage, with its two weathered posts. There was the great cable, thick as a man's wrist, running out from the near post across a hay-meadow. And there in the long grass, hung upon the cable by its iron wheel, sat the ferry-scow itself, a flat black barge with a pole-rack and a bench, listing a little to one side, waiting for a river. The river was not there. At the lowest part of the meadow the cable sagged into a pewter puddle with a heron standing in it, and beyond the puddle, beyond the alder that had once been a bank, a hundred yards off across the grass, the Haldja ran down in its new bed, quick and brown and perfectly indifferent, with the sound of someone clearing a throat.

"Ah," said Toomas. "He is going to be insufferable."

He was, from the first. A small, brown, fierce old man had come out of the reed-thatched hut by the landing stage with a long boat-hook in one hand and a bowl of something in the other. He was seventy, and thin as a rail, and the left side of his head was bare wrinkled skin where an ear should have been. At his knee trotted a large grey nanny goat with a beard like a scholar's and the flat yellow gaze of a magistrate. Both of them stopped at the sight of the wagons. The old man's mouth opened. The goat's did not, but it gave the impression.

"No," said the old man.

"Veikko," said Toomas.

"*No.* You're dead. Rule, look at him. He's dead. I sang it at the Midsummer fires. I had a verse."

"The reports were exaggerated."

"You were *buried*. You were buried by the Assembly, in a minute, with a pension." Veikko Pent came across the grass at a trot and halted at the pony's shoulder and peered up with his one ear cocked. He smelled of tar and smoke and boiled fish. "God's teeth, Veskimae, you've got old."

"You were old when we met."

"I was *thirty-one*. I had a full head. And what is that on your lip, a dead badger?"

"It is a moustache. You will recall that you once admired it."

"I admired it as one admires a shipwreck." And then the old man did a strange thing; he put up his hand, with the boat-hook still in it, and touched Toomas's boot, once, where it hung from the stirrup. It was a very light touch. It lasted less than a second. Then he brought the hand down and rapped his own chest with the bowl, so that fish-broth leapt over the rim, and shouted at the sky. "Soup! There's soup! Nobody's to say I didn't feed the dead!"

* * *

It was a very small hut for five people and a goat. The goat, whose name was Rule, was in the hut because it had always been in the hut, and it took the best corner by the fire with the air of long and legal tenure. Veikko ladled pike soup into wooden bowls, thick with dill and barley and onion, and watched them eat with his single ear tilted forward, like a man listening to music. He had not had company since the thaw. It was in everything he did. It was in the way he refilled Taavi's bowl before it was empty, and in the way he said, over and over, *eat, eat*, as if any pause in the eating would be the pause in which they got up to go.

Toomas ate slowly. He watched Reet.

She had been quiet for the whole of the day. It was not her ordinary quiet. In the afternoon she had asked him, twice, in an even voice, how far it was from the gorge to the Gullet, and the second time she had asked it she had looked at him afterwards as though she were waiting for him to say that she had asked it before. He had said, "Four hours by pony." He had not said anything else. The hands on the spoon were steady now, the ink-black left hand and the ink-black right, but he had seen them tremble yesterday on the boulder at the Gullet's mouth, and he had seen what had been in her face when she stopped looking at the egg and looked at him.

"Master Pent," she said. "I've a question. May I?"

"Veikko! I'm no master. Ask."

"The river moved. The ferry didn't. Why not?"

"Ah," said Veikko Pent, delighted. "Ah, that." He put down the ladle. He wiped his hands on his coat. "Well, now. I'll tell you." And, to Toomas's complete lack of surprise, he did not tell her. He opened his mouth and sang.

It was a rough old voice, cracked down the middle like a bell, and it was perfectly in tune. He sang with his eyes shut and his chin raised and one finger beating the time against the bowl, to a tune that went up in the first line and came down in the second, like a boat riding a swell. The goat lifted its head.

*The river rose up in the dead of the night / and shook out its hair in the stranger's light, / and the ferryman rose and pulled on his coat / and cried, Who stood here? Who stood? Who spoke?*

*The far hand writes, the near hand holds. / Stand and say, and the water stays.*

"It is a ferrymen's song," said Veikko, opening one eye. "Older than the Guild. My grandmother had it off her grandmother, who had it off a woman who poled the Ford the year the Atlas was drawn, and she had a second verse that's not in any book." He closed the eye and sang it.

*A clerk in the city drew me a bank / and wrote me a willow where the willow sank, / but I took up a stone from the bed of the stream / and said, I stood here, and it was not a dream.*

*The far hand writes, the near hand holds. / Stand and say, and the water stays.*

Nobody ate. Taavi had gone very still, with a spoon halfway to his mouth, and a drop of broth ran down the handle and fell on his freckled wrist. Toomas had known the song for forty years, as a tune that ferrymen whistled, a thing for children. He had never once heard it as a set of instructions. He thought of the Hearth-Stone, and kept his face as it was.

"What does it mean?" said Taavi, in a small voice. "I'm sorry. Technically I realise that's an enormous question. But it *sounds* as if it means something. It sounds like—"

"It means what it says," said Veikko.

"But what does it—"

"It means," said the old ferryman, with great patience, "what it says. The far hand writes. A clerk, or a king, a woman in a tower with a good pen, whoever. She writes that the bank is here. The near hand holds. That's me. I stood in the water, and I said, I stood here. And the water stays." He leaned back, and the bench creaked. "Water remembers, lad. It remembers whoever stood in it. The whole river's one long remembering of every fool who ever got his boots wet. It's not hard. A child could do it. Most do, before they learn to doubt it."

"Water doesn't have a memory," said Taavi, with the sad firmness of the trained.

"Doesn't it?" said Veikko. "Then why's it keep coming back to my landing?"

There was a pause. Toomas found that he had set down his spoon.

"Mm," he said. "And the song has a third verse, I think. About the white."

"Has it? Has it?" Veikko's one ear swung round like a compass-needle. "Well, I'll be sheared. Veskimae, you've been *listening*." He sang it, then, in a lower voice, very softly, and the fire had burned down so that the words came out of the dark like fish out of a pool.

*Where the white begins the wind lies down. / Walk till you hear the quiet. / Hilja set her hearth on the whitest ground, / and nothing moved, and nobody died, and she kept it.*

"Walk till you hear the quiet," said Reet. She had said it aloud, to herself. She did not seem to know that she had.

"That's what the old women say," said Veikko, contentedly. "It's the Blank, girl. Six miles of it, in the middle of everything. No wind. No Shift. No bird. My grandmother walked into it once, for a bet, and came out three days later and said it was the loneliest place in the world and she'd stay there if only it had a decent pie."

* * *

Aino came in from the dark at about the time the bowls were scraped. She came in sideways, with her head lowered under the lintel, wet to the thighs. She had left the table half an hour before, with a scowl, to look at the ferry-rig, because a woman who is afraid of bridges likes to see for herself what else is trying to kill her, and the goat Rule, who had slipped out behind her, followed her in at her heel with a look of vindication.

"It holds," said Aino.

"I told you it holds," said Veikko.

"You told me a *song*." She stood dripping. "I put my whole weight on the cable. I hung from it. I hung from it by one arm like a ham in a smokehouse, and I want you to know that I'm going to say it was fine, and I'm going to be right. The new anchor on the east bank's good. Who did it?"

"I did. Last week."

"By yourself?"

"With Rule."

Aino looked down at the goat, and the goat looked up at her. "I owe you an apology," she said to it, in an altogether different tone. "I called one of my oxen after you. Rule Forty. He's a great lump. He eats his own rope. I shouldn't have done it, you're a clean-living animal with a fine moral character, and it was a liberty." She bent from the waist. "I'm sorry."

The goat considered this. Then it turned its back and lay down on Toomas's foot.

"She accepts," said Veikko.

"It's not a she," said Aino. "It's a Rule."

"Both."

Toomas laughed. It surprised him. It came out of him like a cough, a short rusty bark, and Reet looked round at the sound with her lips parted, as though she had not heard it in some time, and then, very slowly, she smiled.

He saw what she did next. She drew her field book from her satchel, the small dark one with the swollen spine, and a stub of pencil, and turned to a clean leaf. She held the book on her knee and the pencil in her left hand, and she said, "Master Pent. Veikko. Would you sing the first one again? Slowly."

He sang it. She wrote.

She wrote fast, and exactly, bent over the page, and he watched her do it because it was a thing he could look at. The cropped dark head. The notch through the right eyebrow. The left hand, black to the wrist, running steadily from word to word, with the small hooked letters of a surveyor who writes for a reader that will not be present. It was a pleasure to see work done well. It had been a long time since he had seen anybody do that, and it was a girl of nineteen, copying a ferryman's song in a hut on a dry river, who had in her skull at this moment the whole mapped skin of a country, and a rapidly growing country of silence beside it. He thought: she is writing it down because she's afraid she will not have it by morning.

He thought: she will find Soo.

It was not a thought he had had before, not in this form. He had known that Soo existed. He had known where; he had always known where, to the half-mile, as one knows where a bad tooth is. Soo-on-the-Slope lay a morning's ride from the Ford up the Haldja, on the Company's own road, and he had been telling himself since the Hollow that there was no help for it. But he had been thinking of the road as a thing that passed, and not of the girl on it. A girl who could recite the valley of Soo from a stolen Dawn Sheet, bend by bend, to the crooked sluice of the mill, was a girl who would stand on the slope above it and know to the yard where each house had been. She would count the roofs. She would count the roofs that were not there.

He opened his mouth to tell her. He shut it. It was a very quiet shutting.

"One more," said Veikko. His voice had altered. He had picked up the boat-hook from beside the fire and was holding it upright between his knees like a staff, and he had put his head back and his eye was a good way off. "There's a last verse. We don't sing it at Midsummer. I put it in myself, the winter after the flood, and I've sung it every night since, for the company. I pulled three of them out of this ford, Veskimae. Did you know that? Three. They'd come a long way down."

Toomas did not answer.

"It's only the names," said Veikko gently. "That's all it is. It's a ferryman's trade to know the names."

He sang.

*Marten the baker and Ilse his wife / and Mikk who was six, who was six, who was six, / Ode who made barrels, the Pärn twins, the two, / Toivo and Tarmo, who never were—*

There was a sound in the room of a bench being pushed back. It was Toomas's own. He heard it as one hears an event in another person's house, with a faint, polite surprise, and he found that he had got to his feet, and that the chain-staff was in his hand, and that the hut was too hot and the goat had shifted off his boot. Reet had looked up. Her pencil had stopped on the fourth name. He saw her lips move over *Ode*, saw her not turn her head, and knew that she had begun to count.

"Excuse me," said Toomas. "The knee."

He went out. He closed the door behind him with great care, so as not to make a noise.

The yard was cold and very clear. The first stars had come out above the alder, and below it the new river muttered on in the dark, going to a place that it had settled upon by itself, and nobody had asked it. He stood with his back against the wall of the hut and his eyes shut. Through the planking he could hear the old voice going on, rough and gentle and entirely steady, laying one name after another into the quiet like stones into a wall. Rein the miller. Anni the weaver. Jakob. Helmi. Oskar.

He knew them all. He did not need to hear the rest.
