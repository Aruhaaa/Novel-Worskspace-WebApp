# The Grain Store

There were forty-one sacks of barley in the store, and Reet had counted them twice by touch, because there was no light to count them by.

The building stood on staddle stones against the mice, a long timber barn with a plank floor a yard above the ground, and its single window was a slit under the eaves too high to reach and too narrow for anything but the night. A thread of lantern light lay under the door. By it she had found the sacks, and the stacked flails, and the scoop, and a coil of old rope, and the place against the north wall where the barley dust lay thickest and Toomas had settled himself with the chain-staff taken from him and his bad leg out in front like a plank. He had been coughing for an hour. It was a dry small cough, polite, the cough of a man who does not wish to be a bother in a house that has locked him up.

She took off her scarf and put it round his mouth. It was the grey wool strip she had worn since Soo.

"Mm," said Toomas, through the wool. "It is not necessary."

"Hold it there."

"I am sixty-two. A man of sixty-two has the right to cough in a granary."

"You have the right to cough with a scarf," said Reet, and he said nothing more, and held it there.

From the yard came a whisper. It was a very loud whisper, and by now Reet knew its rhythm as she knew the sound of her own chain. Aino had been chained to the near wheel of the reeve's grain-cart with a length of ox-chain through the hub, and had decided, evidently, that if she could not break the chain she could at least dispose of the men who held it. "And the one at the gate," breathed the voice, "is the Under-Keeper of the Weights, and he's a cousin of Treasurer's, and I can tell because he stands like a man waiting for a pardon. And the fat one with the cough is Auditor. And that one, with the *hair*—" It paused. "That one's the Assistant Registrar of Sluices, and if he doesn't take his boot off my salt-hook he will find out what a Registrar's mother is made of."

"Eleven so far," said Reet.

"Technically she's inventing some of them," said Taavi.

He had not said anything since the door was barred. He sat against it, in the thread of light, with his knees up and his arms round them. His bandolier was still on his chest; Reet had noticed that, and noticed that the riders who had brought him in, a boy found under the sacking in the cart with a perfectly serene expression, saying that technically he had only been counting, had been told by someone not to touch his things. She had wondered who. She knew now. It was an odd thing, to be afraid of a knowledge you have not yet been given, and to have it arrive on the other side of a door.

* * *

The bolt went back a little past the second hour of the night.

She had been lying on her back across two sacks with her eyes open, doing what she did instead of sleeping. It was an exercise she had never needed before, because she had never before had so much of a country gone out of her: she went to the Hollow's gate, and found the gate and not the road to it; she went to the Wharf Gate, and the barges, and the ducks; she went to the pond at Soo, and there was a smooth cool silence the exact shape of a pond. She had begun to keep a ledger of what remained. It was not a long ledger. The door opened, and the lantern came in, and she sat up.

Kaie Tammaru had brought it herself.

She came in alone and shut the door behind her. She wore a plain grey wrapper over her riding things, as though she had been about to go to bed and had thought better of it, and she carried in her left hand the lantern, and under her arm a folded blanket, and in her right a stone bottle with a cork. Her hands were bare. In the yellow light the stain on them looked almost purple. She set the lantern on a sack, laid the blanket across Toomas's knees without a word, and put the bottle in his hands.

"Salted tea," she said. "Hot. The cook at the house was most obliging."

"Keeper," said Toomas. It was all he said. He drank.

She sat down on a sack opposite Reet. She was so small that her feet did not quite meet the floor, and she crossed her ankles to hide the fact. She said, "Surveyor Sallik. I have read your fourteen columns eleven times."

"I made them so that you would."

"They are the finest piece of work done in the Day-book room since I came to the Hollow. Naturally I mean that as the Keeper and not as a woman who has been caught." She spoke very quietly, in the voice of the Guild House, every consonant set down. "I have been looking for a long time for a Surveyor like you, and I will not insult either of us by pretending that it is chance that I found you at nineteen. I am forty-eight. Each lake costs me a little of the ground I draw. By the end of the thirty-one years I will have very little left of myself, and there is no one in the Hollow I would trust with the keys."

Reet said nothing.

"I should like you to take them," said Kaie.

It was so plain that for an instant it did not seem to be a sentence. Reet heard Toomas's bottle stop.

"You cannot forget," said Kaie. "That is the first thing. In the vault, with the leaves open before you, you would hold every one of the four hundred and twelve at once, and see how they lie against each other, as I cannot. You can do in a week what takes me a season. And you cannot lie." She said it with a kind of tenderness. "You stammered at me over the tea. I have thought of it every night. Do you understand what that is worth in a Keeper? An Atlas written by someone who is unable to deceive it, or herself, or the ground. I have wanted that all my life."

"I've lost a road," said Reet. It came out before she had decided. "And a pond. And a field."

"I know you have. Org told me of the pass, and I have eyes. You keep looking at the place where a door was." The pale brown eyes did not move from her face. "Out there, with the ink on your hand and your heart in it, you will pay for every yard, and you will pay all of it in the end. You have seen what it did to him. Six miles, from one stroke. In the vault you would lose almost nothing, because you would be drawing from the leaf and not the ground. I am not offering you a throne, Sallik. I am offering you a way to do this without being emptied."

She had thought she would be angry. She waited for the anger and it did not come. What came instead was a slow clear cold, such as she felt over a piece of ground that did not close. She sat with her hands on her knees, in the thread of light, and made herself look at what was there.

"Your numbers are right," said Reet.

"Thank you. Few have said so."

"I did them in my head while you ate. The Seam, the rim, the Assembly. Forty thousand is a real thing. I'm not going to tell you that it isn't, because it is, and I can't tell you what I don't believe." Her voice was level. "But you didn't ask Palu."

"Palu would have said no."

It was said gently. That was what Reet remembered afterward: not the words but the kindness in them, the sorrowful patience, the tone in which one explains to a child why the fire is hot. "Palu would have said no, Sallik. I have asked the Assembly, and it said no. I asked the reeve tonight and he said no. No one in the history of the world has said yes to being moved. Naturally I stopped asking."

"Then that's what's there."

Kaie's head tilted.

"A no is information," said Reet. She heard herself say it with a great, odd, growing wonder, as though she had heard it said by someone else. "It's a fact about the ground. They like their bridges. They've got thirty-one of the reeve's family in the yard. That's *there*. You can't draw a lake around it and call the place empty. You've done the sums of the Hollow and left off the other column."

"And the other column is to be given a vote? Forty thousand against nine hundred?"

"I don't know. I don't know the sum." Her own hands were cold. "But my mother lives in Sora."

Something moved in the Keeper's face.

"Leaf Eighty-eight. The drain behind Kalda's mill. It moved last spring, and my mother wrote to say her cellar had been dry for the first time in twenty years, and she wondered if the Guild had done something about it. She was pleased."

"Then she is the happier for it."

"She's pleased because she doesn't know." Reet's voice cracked at last, a hair's breadth. "She'd have said yes, perhaps, if you'd asked. Or no. I don't know. I'll never know. I only know that I don't know what she'd say, and neither do you. Nobody does, because nobody *asked*."

For a time the Keeper looked at her. She sat quite straight on the sack, small, silver-braided, with her stained hands lying in her lap like two things that belonged to another woman. "The seventh place," said Kaie, at last, quietly. "Your mother's marsh. I moved the drain a quarter-mile into the basin. The cellar was a consequence, not a purpose. I am sorry for it. I did not know whose leaf it was until after."

It was the nearest thing to an apology Reet would ever hear her give, and she was almost sure it was meant.

"You may think about it," said Kaie, and rose. "I shall not ask you before dawn."

* * *

At the door, with her hand on the latch, the Keeper stopped.

"There is one other matter. Master Ilves."

Taavi had not moved. He sat against the planks, a foot from her skirts, with his knees drawn up. Reet saw the freckles stand out on his face like dropped seed. "Technically," he said, "I'm not sure I'm—"

"Your mother gave you something. A stoneware flask with a red thread. I imagine you have it on you." She did not make it a question. "Stand up, please."

He stood up. He stood, and she came across the floor to him in three small steps, and put out one bare hand and turned him a little by the shoulder, as one turns a coat on a peg. She found the flask at the back of the bandolier, in the angle where the little vials did not hang. She slid the red thread off its hook. It came away easily.

"She has never told me," said Kaie, weighing it in her palm, "that she kept back a measure. But she does, naturally. She has been honest with me in every particular except that one, and I have honoured her by not enquiring." She drew the stopper and held the neck beneath her nose. "She still puts in the clove."

The smell reached Reet across the room, warm and sweet and wrong. Kaie looked down at the flask for some seconds. Then, quite calmly, she tipped it.

It came out black. It ran out of the neck in a thick glossy cord and struck the boards, and spread. It went into the cracks between the planks and along the grain, and where it touched the barley dust it drank it in and darkened, and the dark crept outward in a long spreading lobe like a coastline. The smell of cloves came up out of it, strong enough to taste. Reet watched it as she had watched Kuld Mere, and Soo, and the black stone on the white. She found that the stain had a shape. It was the exact shape of the Pale Flats on the Guild's great map in the Day-book room, and she did not know why her mind should have done that.

She did not look at Taavi. She looked at the ink.

Kaie shook the last drops from the neck. She stoppered the empty flask and set it carefully on the sack beside the lantern. The satisfaction in her face was small and private, like a woman's at a letter written.

"Is that the whole of it, Master Ilves?"

"It's the only flask I carry," said Taavi, in a very small voice.

The pale eyes turned. "Surveyor?"

Reet felt her heart go over once, like a stone. She had been a girl who could not lie for nineteen years, and she had been taught by a bird, a basket and an old man's remark about sentences that there was a space between a lie and the truth where a person might stand and be neither. "That was all that was in the flask," she said.

It went out of her mouth level, and clean, and unstammering.

Kaie Tammaru studied her for a count of three. Nothing in the small face altered. "Naturally," she said. She went to the door. "Good night, Master Veskimae. I did not mean it to be so cold."

The bolt dropped behind her.

* * *

Nobody spoke until the sound of her steps had gone down the yard.

Then Taavi sat. He slid down the planks until he was on the floor against the door with his legs out, and he laid his hands palm-up on his knees, and they were empty, and he looked at them. His shoulders were shaking. He did not make a sound.

"Taavi," said Toomas.

"I'm sorry."

"No."

"I lost it. It's all gone, it was the last of it, and my mother said not to lose it, she said *don't lose it*, and I—"

"Taavi." Toomas had put down the bottle. He put his two hands on the chain-staff that was not there, found nothing, and laid them on his knees. He had never looked so old. "I should like to say something to you, and I should like you to let me say it to the end."

Taavi's head came up. Reet saw his eyes find hers, once, across the dark, and saw what was in them. It was the look of someone who has been given a gift that he cannot spend, and who has to sit in the room with the person who is sorry.

"I should have stopped it," said Toomas. "Twelve years ago, and every year since. I should have taken the notebook to the Assembly, and my neck with it. I should have burned the key. I did neither. I went to Tinta Row and bound fish ledgers, and while I bound them your mother sold a woman a thing I had made possible, and she had a customer, and you had a flask. It is the sort of arithmetic I am good at." His voice was dry, even, entirely without self-pity. "I am sorry, Taavi. I do not often say it. I am told I am out of practice."

"It's not— technically—" Taavi had gone grey. "It wasn't you, it was my mother, it was—"

"It was a good many of us. I begin with myself." He leaned his head back against the sacks. "That is all."

Reet looked at the floor. She would have told him. She would have said it out loud, there and then, with her whole heart: it's in the loaf, Toomas, it's safe, he's not lost it, he's cleverer than any of us. But the barn had ears; it had a man outside it with a lantern; and she had learned in the past hour exactly how narrow a ledge she stood on. So she sat with her hands on her knees, and her face still, and let an old man apologise to a boy who had not done anything, for a loss that had not happened, and she was ashamed.

She heard Taavi weep after that. It went on for some time.

Out in the yard Aino's whisper had faltered. It went on, now and then, in bursts: *Deputy Assistant Surveyor of Culverts, you great lump*, and then not.

* * *

The town had gone quiet.

She had not noticed it happen. The drums across the gorge had stopped, and the singing, and the sound of feet on the planks; the lanterns on the bridges had been put out one by one, and the dark had come up out of the river. Through the slit in the eaves she could see a single star. The floor beneath her had its slow breath in it, the bridges settling, and somewhere a long way off a dog barked once and thought better of it.

She lay down again. She put her hands behind her head and looked at where the ceiling would have been.

She had never forgotten a thing in her life. It was her one gift, and her one comfort. And now the ground went out of her whenever it pleased, a road, a pond, a field of barley, and in the places where it had been there was nothing, a clean cool snow, and she had thought that was the worst that could be done to a person. She saw now that it was not. The ground left. What people did to you stayed. A white kidskin glove coming off a finger at a time. A small bare hand tilting a flask. A boy's face. An old man's voice saying he was sorry, in the dark, to the wrong person.

She wanted, very badly, to forget this. She lay there and tried. She went to the place where she kept things and she took out the whole of the evening and set it on the snow and looked at it, and it did not go.

It would be there in the morning. It would be there when she was old.

She had nearly slept when it came.

It came from below. It was very small, and it came up through the planks beneath her back with the faint dust of the barley: three notes, rising, rising, falling, the sort of whistle a child makes to call a dog that is hiding. It came again.
