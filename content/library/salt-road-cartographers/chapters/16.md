# Stood Water

Taavi had tied the bucket to forty yards of washing-line, which Reet thought optimistic, since the river was a hundred and ninety feet down.

"Three hundred and four steps," said Liisu, leading the way. "It's called the Wet Stair. The rope-walk uses it to take the hemp down to soak." She said it with her back to them, in the flat voice. Reet counted the steps as she descended, because the steps were there and she had never in her life been able to leave a stair uncounted, and she made them three hundred and four, and was displeased to find that she had been checked by a child and had come out equal.

It was dawn, a cold clear one, and the gorge held the blue of the night in its throat. On the bridges above them lamps were going out, one after another, and a few late dancers lay among the ribbons where the plum brandy had put them. Toomas had gone before it was light. She had heard the chain-staff on the boards of the reeve's loft, and then the stair, and had lain awake in the dark with her eyes open, hearing him go out toward the bell-tower with a sprig of rosemary from the kitchen in his fist. He had not come back. She had not gone after him. She had told herself she was letting him be.

Down on the third bridge from the bottom something moved on all fours, slowly, between the handrails. "Twenty-two!" bawled Aino, in a voice cracked with effort. "Twenty-three next! Count them, Sallik!" Reet lifted a hand and counted them.

At the foot of the stair the river was smaller than she had been led to expect. It was a brown, quick, self-contained thing, thirty feet across, rolling stones along its bed with a sound like a man cracking nuts. The cliff on the east side went up above it in rose-coloured tiers, and high on the face, twenty feet over her head, was the red line with its date.

"That's the Line," said Liisu. "Dad says it's the day the Guild saved us. Gran said it was the day somebody did." She picked up a flat stone, weighed it, and skimmed it. It went four times. "She never said who."

Reet looked at the stone going across the water and said nothing. She was not obliged to. She had never in her life been so glad of a thing that was not a lie.

* * *

Taavi knelt at the edge with a brass-bound case open on a flat rock. He drew the bucket up hand over hand, and poured a thimbleful into a clean vial, and held the vial up to the light and looked at it as a man looks at a child's face when the doctor is about to speak. Then he took the pin from his bandolier and touched the point of it to the flask. Reet watched the speck go in. It was grey, a mere grain of dull metal-coloured stuff, and it fell through the water and spread.

The water went black. It was fast, and total, and clean, the whole thimbleful going dark from the top to the bottom like a lamp being turned down. There was no scum, no rust, no settling. It sat there in his fingers, as black as the stuff in the groove at the Hearth-Stone, as black as the sliver he had tested on the afternoon of the green vat in Tinta Row.

"It's the same," said Taavi. He was whispering, though no one could have heard him over the river. "Technically, it's the same black as Sora's. It's the same black as the Blank's. Reet, it doesn't matter which water. It has to be *the place's*, but it doesn't have to be a vial. It's the river. It's the river, and it's been the river all along."

"Row a hundred and sixty-three on the shelf," said Reet slowly. "A hundred and twelve vials. Bare."

"She has them."

"She has Palu's water in glass in the vault. That's how she draws it from there." She looked at the thin brown river, at the stones, at the red line high on the cliff. "She doesn't need the river. We don't need her vials. Anyone who stood here can dip a bucket."

"Anyone with the base," said Taavi.

"Anyone with the base."

"Which is, technically," said Taavi, "me."

He had gone quite white under the freckles. She saw that he had understood, in that moment, that he was no longer a boy who had come along to see a thing for himself. She did not tell him that she had known it since the green vat. It was not the kind of thing you said to someone before breakfast.

* * *

She did it that morning. She did not ask Toomas, who was at the grave. She told herself this was because he was at the grave. She knew it was because he would have said *not yet*, and she had had her fill of not yet; she had a clock in her, and it said that the Keeper was on the road with twelve horses, and that the ninth night was the night of the following day, and that the ground did not mind who was kind to it.

She laid it out for Taavi on the Wet Stair, step by step, in the voice of someone reading figures. A drawing made on the spot beats a drawing made far away. Therefore she would stand on East Rise, where the whole valley lay below her like a leaf, and draw it as it was, with the town in it, in true ink, and swear. Then the vault's lake would find the ground already spoken for, and could not land.

"That's brilliant," said Taavi, miserably. "Technically it's exactly right. It's the most correct thing I've ever heard. And it'll cost you the whole valley."

"Then it costs the valley."

"Reet, it's nine hundred people and a—a hill, and some fields, and a *river*—"

"Yes."

He gave her the pot. He stood with it in both hands a moment longer than he need have, like a man handing a child to a surgeon. It held a spoon of base and a pint of river water, and enough of the first to make the second black as the inside of a boot.

The east ridge was bare and brown and cold. She went up it alone, with the field book in her satchel and the brush behind her ear, past the Birch Hanger on the north flank, whose leaves had gone the colour of old brass, and at the top she turned and looked down, and there it was. The whole valley. The gorge, the green and yellow patchwork of the fields on the plateau beyond, the thin road, the smoke from nine hundred chimneys, and the forty bridges hung across the cleft like threads. She stood and let it come into her, and there was a moment, one clear bright moment, in which she thought: I could do it. I have the memory for it. I am the person who can.

She began with the field at the foot of the ridge. It was a plot of barley stubble, a long strip with a thorn hedge on the downhill side, a ditch, a gatepost of split oak with an iron ring through it, and a stile made of two flat stones. Ruus's barley, Liisu had called it. She knelt on the cold turf and said, "I stood here. I drew only what I saw," and the first line went onto the page black and cold and sure, and she felt it take.

She drew the hedge. She drew the ditch, the gatepost, the ring, the stile. And as she drew, the field began to leave her.

It was not like the Gullet, which had gone like a coat sliding off a hook. This was slower, and she could watch it. It was as though someone were carrying the furniture out of a room she stood in, one piece at a time, while she described it. The hedge went out of her head as the nib left the hedge. The ditch, the stile, the shapes of the stones. She went on drawing from the page, and from what was left of her eye. When she looked up for the next field she did not know where it began. There was a flat brown stretch of land, and a line in it that might have been a wall, and she could not have told you what it was for, or how many paces long, or what the soil was, and her hand, going on without her, made a line.

The line went grey. It spread on the page like a drop of tea on a cloth. It was a lie, because she could not see, and a lie would not take the ink, and the whole valley went sideways, and the sky tilted up toward her like a tray.

"You can't stand in all of it," said a flat voice, from a long way off.

She felt the cold grass against her cheek.

"We can," said Liisu.

* * *

She woke in a sheepfold.

It was a low round building of piled stone with a roof of turf, and there was straw under her and a sheepskin over her and a sheep, a large and contemplative sheep, standing in the corner looking at her with an air of waiting for her to explain. The light came in a slanting bar through the door, and in it was Liisu, sitting on an upturned pail with the stolen board across her knees, drawing on its margin with a stub of pencil. Her tongue was between her teeth.

"You were out two hours," said Liisu, not looking up. "Taavi wanted to fetch the bell. I said it would only frighten the sheep. I put the coat on you. It's Dad's."

Reet lay still. She tried the field. She went to the place where Ruus's barley had been, and found a smooth cool hush the exact shape of it, a long thin strip, with a hedge-shaped edge. She tried the valley around it, and the valley was there, whole, and it was nothing to her. It was a thing she had been told. She sat up slowly, with her head full of brass, and the sheepskin slid, and she looked past the girl through the low door.

The whole of Palu lay below her in the noon light.

She saw it as she had not seen it from the ridge. A woman was hanging washing on a line strung between two bridges, shaking out a sheet, pegging it, shaking out another. A boy ran along a span with a bucket and a very long pole. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys and leaned over the same way. Two men at a gate argued, on a field she did not know the name of, with their hands. A dog crossed a bridge. A girl sat on a roof. Nine hundred people were standing in this valley. They had stood in it all their lives, on every yard, in their own doors, at their own wells, in every field and ditch and under every gatepost. They knew it as she knew a leaf. They knew where the cow had fallen in.

*She is not the one who stood,* she thought. *They are.*

It was so simple that it was embarrassing. She sat in the straw with the lost barley field in her head like a missing tooth, and looked at the valley, and thought of all the afternoons she had spent in the Day-book room, alone, with her lamp, believing that the truth was a thing a perfect record could keep safe, and that she was the one to keep it. She had never once asked anyone. She had never needed to. She had had no one to ask.

"They were here first," said Reet aloud. "I'm only the one who can draw."

"Yes," said Liisu, as if it were a thing that had been settled some time ago. "That's what I said."

* * *

Taavi came at one o'clock with a loaf.

It was a round dark rye the size of a dinner plate, and he carried it as he had carried the basket, in both arms, against his chest. He sat down on the straw without a word and put it between them. Behind him in the doorway was Aino, filthy to the elbows, with a long raw graze across her chin, saying nothing, and swaying faintly; she had done twenty-nine bridges before breakfast and been asked to stop.

"I did the sums in the night," said Taavi. "I do that sometimes. Think. It's inconvenient."

He did them aloud, because it was the only way he could do them, and they came out in recipes. "Take one gill of base. Add sixty gills of stood-water. Steep an hour in copper, strain through a cloth. That's a pint and a half of ink, near enough, and it'll write a lot more than you'd think; the ink's mostly for being believed. A household is a gill of that. Take three hundred and twelve households. That's, I'm sorry, that's, technically that's under three pints of base. For the whole town." He swallowed. "My mother gave me, roughly, two quarts. Roughly is her word for a thing she doesn't want to measure, so I measured. It's two quarts and a gill."

"It's more than we need."

"It's four times what we need. Which means we can afford to lose a lot of it. And I hate having that sentence in my head. It's the kind of sentence you could do a lot of harm with."

"Why would we lose it?"

"Because she'll look for it," said Taavi. "Your Keeper. She's had eight years of my mother's casks. She knows my mother. She knows my mother would send a flask. Technically she'll have the whole inventory in her head." He took the loaf and turned it over, and she saw that someone had cut a neat round lid from the heel and replaced it, and glued it with a flour paste so exactly that the seam was a thread. Inside the loaf, packed in straw, was a small stoppered crock of grey stoneware. "Half of it goes here. This is Liisu's. She's agreed. Nobody's ever looked in Liisu's bread."

"Nobody has ever found it," said Liisu, "where I take it. I know a place under the grain store that nobody goes."

"And the other half?"

He had taken the flask from the back of his bandolier. It was the stoneware one, tied about the neck with a red thread, warm still from his body. He held it a moment in both hands. She saw him look at it as one looks at a thing one is going to do to somebody. Then he uncorked the larger of the Ilves Black vials from his belt, and with a hand that trembled very slightly, he poured it into the flask until it was brimming.

"It's going to look like it," he said. "And smell like it. There's enough base left in there for the clove. It's half the real thing and half my family's honest ink." His freckles stood out on a face the colour of paper. "That's a lie. I've never told one on purpose. I've omitted things, but I've never put it in a bottle. I don't know how people do it."

"They get used to it."

"Please don't say that," said Taavi.

He pushed the stopper home. The flask's red thread hung down. Reet looked at it, at his stained fingers, at the honest and wretched face, and found that she could not say any of the things she might have said, and so said the one she meant.

"That's the first brave thing I've seen anyone do in this valley."

"It's a bottle," said Taavi.

"It's a bottle that cost you something."

He looked at his hands, and nodded, and for a minute the sheepfold was entirely quiet except for the sheep.

* * *

When they had all gone, she took the board.

The light had shifted. It lay now across her knees in a clean white bar, and she held the tracing in it and did what she had said she would do. She took the ivory rule from her satchel, two inches long, marked to the half-millimetre, and laid it along the gutter fold. She pressed the end against the pin-point of light, and read the figure, and read it again.

Four.

It was four. It was not five. She had seen five on Leaf 88 two years ago, and four on Leaf 88 last week, and a five-millimetre pinhole was a thing the Guild did, to the hair, as it did its dividers and its lamp-wicks. And she had a theory, and she was sorry for it.

She had it whole, as she had everything that was not ground. She went to the library of her memory and took down the afternoon in the Keeper's rooms, a small neat table, a tray of tea, and a woman's gloved hands setting a pair of brass dividers to Reet's fourteen-column draught. She watched them step along the columns, one leg planted, the other swinging. Every interval landed. The right leg stood straight. The left stood off the true like a dancer's foot, bent in the long shank, so that it leaned as the Tower at Orav leans. She had noticed it at the time, because she noticed such things. She had not known what to do with it.

She took two pencils and a length of thread from the satchel. She laid one pencil along the gutter, and the other at the lean she remembered: three up, four across. She measured the thread against it, and the thread was five. A true leg, shouldered against the gutter fold, puts its point five millimetres in. A leg that leans three-four-five puts its point at the end of the slant, and the slant is five along, and four across.

She did it twice. She did it a third time, because she did not want it to be so.

*Four.*

The sheep shifted in the corner and breathed out. In her lap the board lay quite still, and the pin-point of light in the margin looked at her through the onion-skin, small and exact and bright as a star.

It was not a theory. A clerk had pricked it; a Keeper had made it. Every Shift for eight years, every lake and drain and boundary, had gone through the same bent leg. The Keeper's hand was in the Atlas, and she was sitting in a sheepfold with the proof of it in her lap, and she had suspected for a week, and had not been able to put a ruler to it.

She had begun to fold the tracing when the bell rang.

It was not the bridge-bell. It was the small cracked lookout bell at the head of the gorge road, and it rang once, and then again, and then in a quick hard run, as a bell does when the man at the rope has seen a thing that will not wait for him to finish his sentence. She went to the door of the fold, with the board in her hand.

The road came in from the north across the plateau, white in the afternoon light, and along it, small as seeds, trotted a file of horses. She counted them. She did not mean to. Twelve horses, in a file, at a smart even pace, without any hurry in them, as though the road were a corridor. At the head of them, on a pale grey no bigger than a cat at that distance, rode a figure so small that it would have been taken for a child; and down its back, catching the sun like a wire of silver drawn through the air, hung a braid.
