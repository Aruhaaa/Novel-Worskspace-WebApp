# Rule Forty

"Rule Forty," said Mihkel Org, "reads as follows."

He had not yet got down from his horse. He sat the heavy bay at the south mouth of the Gullet with his eleven riders drawn up behind him in grey coats, their mounts blowing, and he held up one finger in a buttery kid glove as if he meant to test the wind with it. Reet had known him for six years. She had never seen him out of doors. Away from the Guild House he looked like a pudding that had been put on a horse by someone with a grudge. The mustard waistcoat had been brushed, and there was a smear of dust across the front of it where he had plainly sat on a pastry.

"'No Surveyor sleeps unwatched in a shifting place.'" He let it ring. It rang rather well, in the cleft, and came back off the rock a moment later in a smaller voice that said *shifting place*. "You are in the Seam, Sallik. It is four hours to dusk. You are a Surveyor of the Guild, on leave, but a Surveyor, and the Rule does not distinguish. I am therefore obliged, as Master, to stop this caravan, and to see you to a held place, and to watch you there."

"Master Org," said Hendrik Loo, who had walked up with his hat on his head and his charter in his fist. "You'll forgive me. This is a Salt Company train under Article Nine. No officer of the Guild may detain—"

"Article Nine is subordinate to Rule Forty."

"It is not, sir. It is a different book."

"Then," said Mihkel, with perfect composure, "I invite you to raise it with the Assembly. In the meantime, would someone please move that animal?"

The animal was an ox. It had wandered up from the line to see what the noise was, a broad dun beast with a pale belly and a short, ill-tempered tail, and it had come to rest with its nose a foot from the bay's. The bay looked at it. The ox looked at Mihkel. It was one of those long, mild, appraising looks that an ox can give, in which the whole history of a creature's contempt for committees is held for an instant in the eye.

Aino arrived at a run, with the salt-hook over her shoulder. "Mihkel!" she bawled. "Come away from the gentleman!"

There was a silence.

"I beg your pardon?" said the Master-Surveyor.

"The ox, Master. His name's Mihkel." Aino's face was perfectly grave. She stood with her hand on the dun neck. "Very common name, in the Seam. We've had him since he was a calf. He's a good worker, mostly. A bit officious."

"I," said Mihkel, "see."

"Is it a name you know?"

"It is a very common name," said Mihkel, and Reet, who was standing in the dust with her satchel and the stained hand at her side, counted. She counted it because she could not help it; she counted four seconds of absolute rigid stillness in Aino Rebane's face, with the chipped tooth showing, before the whole six feet two of her broke like a dropped plate and she had to turn away and put her forehead against the ox. Behind her, among the riders, someone coughed in a manner that was nearly a laugh and was converted at the last into a throat condition.

Mihkel's ears went the colour of his waistcoat.

"Sallik," he said. "Walk with me."

* * *

They went a little way up the slope, where the track forked round a boulder and the wind had scoured the grass flat. Mihkel got down from the bay at last, with the effort of a man dismounting from a wardrobe, and stood with the reins over his arm. He did not look at her. He looked at the hills, and at the dust, and at the little rock-ringed pool beside the track where a seep of water came out of the mountain and lay black and still in a ring of ferns.

"You have been very foolish," he said.

"Yes, Master."

"You have left the Hollow without leave of absence being counted as departure. You have fallen in with a former Keeper who is, by the Assembly's own minute, not to be consulted on matters of the Atlas. You have gone into the Seam with a salt caravan and a baker's boy—"

"Ink-maker's."

"—and I am obliged by the Rules to bring you home." He took off his hat. It was a tall black hat with a flat brim, and he held it before his chest like a man at a burying. The walnut fringe stood up in the wind. "I have been your Master for six years. I have never had a rider who was so often right and so rarely tidy."

"Master," said Reet. "Rule Eleven. Rider reports. Keeper interprets. I reported. Nobody interpreted."

"You were not asked to report, you were asked to rest."

"Fourteen shifts."

"I know how many."

She looked at him. He had said it too quickly, and a different voice had come out under his own, a lower and unofficial one, and she saw the pastry-smear on his waistcoat, and the sag under his eyes. "I looked at your fourteen, Sallik," said Mihkel Org. "I looked at them last night with a lamp. I looked at them until the lamp went out." He turned the hat over. "I should like to be wrong. I should like it very much. But I am not the person who ought to say so on the side of a mountain."

"Who is?"

"The Keeper. Who left the Hollow at the first bell this morning with six fast horses for Palu. By the river road. With a notice sent ahead." He said it as if reading the minute of a meeting. "She will be there before the week is out. I do not know what she intends. She did not tell me. I will say," and here his voice dropped, "that it was a long time since she did not tell me."

"Then let me go to Palu."

"No."

"There's a valley, Master. Nine hundred people."

"There are nine hundred people in the Hollow's own third tier, and I should like them all to remain exactly where they are." He settled the hat upon his skull with a smack. "Rule Forty. I will take you back through the Gullet to the Guild wayhouse at Kesk's well, where the ground has been pinned for eighty years and a Surveyor can sleep as she likes. We start within the hour. The caravan will turn about. It is a very reasonable arrangement."

"Rebane," he added, to the air, as Aino went by below them with an armful of tethering rope. "You are engaged by the Company and not by the girl. I trust we understand each other."

Aino did not break step. "Do we know each other, Master?" she said over her shoulder.

"By reputation."

"Mine's terrible," said Aino cheerfully, and went on.

Reet filed it. She had not told him the guard's name. She did not think anyone had. She put it with the basket, and the clatter like shaken cards, and the north-pointing lantern, and she did not take the three out to look at them; it was not yet time. She said "Yes, Master," in a voice that had no stammer in it, since she was not lying, she was only agreeing to a thing she had no intention of doing, and walked down the slope.

* * *

Toomas was sitting on a wagon-step with his bad leg out and a face of settled and total innocence. He had watched the whole conversation from forty yards off. He looked, as she came, at her hands and not at her face.

"He wants to go back through the Gullet," she said. "To the wayhouse. Tonight."

"Mm. And what does the Gullet say?"

She had walked it at noon, in the long dry hour after the bridge. She had walked it as she walked everything, counting, and she had it still, in the place where she kept her ground: a cleft three hundred and eighty yards long through the shoulder of a mountain called Hob, with walls of grey limestone leaning in, and in the middle a throat where the cleft pinched to two yards and a foot. In the throat, against the east wall, leaned a slab the size of a cottage door. It had come off the cliff in the night. It stood braced at a slant against the opposite stone by no better means than habit, and the wagons had gone past it with a hand's breadth to spare on either side, and Taavi had walked through with his eyes shut and his hands in the air, saying that technically it was holding itself up by good manners.

"It'll move tonight," she said. "Everything in there will. If we go back in at dusk, with forty wagons—"

"Quite."

"If it isn't there at all, if there's no way back through the Gullet—"

"Mm."

"I need a quarter of an hour. And a rope."

"Rule Forty," said Toomas, gently, "is a fine thing. I wrote the fourth schedule to it myself. It says nothing at all about a pass that is shut." He did not move his eyes from the sky. "You will want Aino."

"I don't know whether I can ask her."

"No. You cannot. But you may stand where she will see you, and look at the slab."

She went to the water. That was the excuse, and it did very well; Mihkel's riders saw a young woman with a tin cup go up the track toward the black pool in the ferns, and one of them sighed in the voice of a man who would rather have been home. She filled a horn cup from the seep, and her hands were quite steady, and she crouched there in the ferns with the cup between her knees and looked up the throat of the Gullet, sixty yards off, where the slab stood in its slice of afternoon light. She did not turn her head. She looked at the slab for a long time.

She heard the boots behind her. They stopped.

Aino was breathing through her nose. She was carrying a coil of the Company's heavy rope on her shoulder, and she stood very still at Reet's back with her face turned up toward the cleft, and what was in it Reet could not see. For a long time she did not say anything. Reet thought, with some distinctness: she is deciding. Then Aino said, in a flat voice, "That slab's been wanting to come down since the cock crowed."

"I think it has."

"It'd want a hitch about the top. Four of the heavy oxen. A good man up the wall." She swallowed. "Treasurer's pulled worse. Treasurer once pulled a bridge-post out of a bog with a mouthful of nettles."

"Aino. If it falls, there's no way back."

"Then there isn't," said Aino. She did not look round. "I don't know what you've done, Sallik. I don't know what you are. I've been told a number of things about you by a number of people and I've not liked any of them." She hitched the rope on her shoulder. "Draw whatever you're going to draw. I'll be quick."

* * *

It took eleven minutes. Reet found, afterwards, that she could not account for the order of it, and this was not like her: Taavi up the east wall of the cleft like a squirrel with a loop of rope in his teeth; Aino roaring at the oxen by their names, Treasurer, Auditor, Guildmaster, Registrar, who put down his principles for once and leaned; Mihkel's voice coming from far down the slope in a rising and indignant series of rule-numbers; and then the slab, which had stood all night by good manners, rocked, tipped forward with a long grinding groan, and went across the throat of the Gullet like a door being slammed in another house.

The noise went on for some time. It was the walls, answering. A cloud of white dust came out of the mouth of the cleft and rolled down the slope, and the horses screamed, and all through the dust the echoes ran up and down the mountain's throat saying *shifting place, shifting place, shifting place*, until they ran out.

She was already kneeling. She had her field book across her knee, and the horn cup, and her stub of day-ink, and a brush. The throat, filled now to the roof with fallen limestone, stood sixty yards from her in a settling smoke. She drew it. She drew it quickly and exactly, left-handed, as she had been taught: the east wall, the west wall, the slab where it lay, the shoulder of the mountain above it and the long grey back of the ridge, because a drawing that left out the slope the rubble leaned on would be a lie of omission, and she had never wanted to find out whether the ink agreed. She stirred the day-ink with the stood-water of the seep. It went from grey to black.

"I stood here," said Reet. "I drew only what I saw."

Beside her, Toomas, who had made his way up the track on the chain-staff without her noticing, said very quietly, "Mm. A yard or two, for a night, is what the day-ink will hold."

"Then it'll hold the plug," said Reet. "That's two yards."

"It will take a great deal more than two yards from you."

"I know," she said, and had no idea, and drew the last line.

The stone settled. It was a small sound, a creak and a tick, like a joint going home. And then, as with the Hallik stone, there was a silence in the world around the heap of fallen rock, a stillness that lay on it like a hand. The dust fell straight. A pebble that had been rolling stopped rolling. She felt the Gullet *hold*, and she felt something else go with it, a long, cold, quiet slipping at the back of her skull, like a coat sliding from a hook.

Mihkel came up at a gallop and pulled the bay round in a spray of grit. His face was the colour of tallow. He looked at the rubble, and at the dust, and at Reet on her knees with a book in her lap, and at Aino with the rope still wound on her fist, and at the four oxen standing about with the benign and blameless air of beasts who have not been consulted.

"That," he said, "is damage to Guild property under Rule Ninety-one."

"It's a mountain, Master."

"It was a Guild mountain!" He looked wildly at the sky. The light had begun to lengthen. Low in the south the first dark was gathering along the horizon, and the shadows of the riders stretched out long and thin across the heath. He did the sum on his fingers. Reet watched him do it. He was a man who had spent thirty years in the Guild House in the belief that the Rules were a kind of weather, and she watched them close over his head. There was no way back through the Gullet. There was no held ground on this side of it. The sun would set in two hours, and there were twelve Surveyors of the Guild sitting in a shifting place.

"Rule Forty," said Mihkel, hoarsely.

"Yes, Master."

"There is a beacon-house at Hob Foot. Held ground. Eleven miles, round the mountain's east shoulder. If we start now and ride hard—" He stopped. His gaze came round to her, and he was not angry; it was something worse. "You stopped me with the Rule."

"You stopped me with it first."

"I shall be back at first light, Sallik." The bay fidgeted. "At first light, with a warrant. Do not move."

"I won't move, Master," said Reet, and did not stammer, because she was telling the truth. They would not move at all. They would be gone.

He mounted. He turned his men with a roar, and the eleven wheeled and went off at a smart trot to the east, along the foot of the mountain, round the long grey shoulder of Hob, turning back from the plugged pass to find another way. She watched the mustard waistcoat shrink until it was a button, a seed, and was gone.

* * *

Nobody spoke to her for a while, which she was grateful for.

She sat on a boulder with her field book shut on her knee. Taavi had come down the wall and was sick behind a rock. Toomas stood beside her with both hands on the chain-staff, not looking at her. The oxen were being led away. The Company's wagons had begun to form up again, in the last yellow light, for the long southward road, and she could hear Hendrik Loo giving orders in a voice that suggested he had done all this himself.

She put her hands in her lap. They were shaking. She watched them with interest. They were the left hand, ink-black to the knuckle, and the right, ink-black to the wrist, and they shook together like a pair of birds. She had never seen them do that before.

A shadow fell over her. It was Aino, who had come down without a sound, which for a woman of that height was a considerable feat. She looked at the hands. She looked at Reet's face. Whatever she saw there she kept to herself; she opened her mouth, and shut it, and reached into the pouch at her belt and brought out an egg. It was hard-boiled, still warm, and peeled in a single curl.

"Eat that," said Aino.

Reet took it. She ate. Aino stood over her and looked south, at the darkening road, with the red scarf stirring at her neck, and nobody said a word of the thing that had been said by the seep.

Reet finished the egg. She sat for a time with the taste of salt in her mouth, and because it was her habit, because it was what she did when she was frightened, she tried to take herself back down the road she had come. She began at the Wharf Gate, where the barges lay under the wall. That was there. She went out along the heath, across the wool-train's track, to the Three Pines, and—

There was nothing.

She tried again. She tried from the other end, from the bridge, working north: the gorge, the rim, the pony's flank. And behind the gorge, where the road ought to have run up the long back of the ridge to Kesk's well and out across the heath, where the milestones were, and the ditch, and the hawthorn on the wrong side of its wall, there was a smooth place, a hush, a pale soft expanse like a sheet of snow. Not a gap, as at the Hallik stone, a tooth's worth. It was a country. The whole of the north road had been taken out of her, and she had not felt it go, and she did not know what had been there.

She sat very still. She tried the Hollow's gate, and found the gate, and the stair; but she could not remember how she had come to it. She could not remember how she had come here. She groped, as one gropes in a dark room for the wall, and her hand closed on nothing but the day, the long bright afternoon, the heat of the egg, and a pair of broad brown hands holding a tin mug, and a white moustache, and a milky eye turned on her with mild inquiry. Toomas's face. It was the only thing in the whole country that she could be sure of.

She took hold of it. She held it as one holds a rope, with both hands.
