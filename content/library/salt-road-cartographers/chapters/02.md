# The Correction Book

The lamp-boys were still at it when Reet came down into the Hollow, though it was past the fourth bell and the sky over the rim had gone the colour of clean slate. They went up and down the stairs in pairs with their hooked poles and oil-cans, trimming wicks on every landing, because a city built in a sinkhole saw the sun for four hours a day in winter and did not mean to spend the rest of it in the dark. The lamps burned all year. The whole Hollow smelled of them: lamp-oil and wet limestone and, under both, sharp and brown and everywhere, the oak-gall tang of Guild ink, which got into the plaster, the bread, the hair of the children and, in Reet's case, every pore of her right hand.

She had left Plover at the rim livery and come down on foot, because there was no other way. There was the Guild stair, with its four hundred and nineteen steps to the floor, and there were the thousand unregistered ones that went everywhere else. She did not count the Guild stair. It counted itself.

"Sallik!" A lamp-boy hung from the rail of the eighth landing by one arm, eleven years old and all knees, soot to the elbow. "Mihkel's looking for you."

"Mihkel is always looking for me, Ants."

"He's got his waistcoat on."

Reet stopped with one foot on the next step. "Which?"

"Mustard."

"Oh, lovely," she said, and gave him the sugar lump she had been keeping for Plover.

The mustard waistcoat was for audits, funerals and visits from the Assembly's clerks, in that order of dread. She went the rest of the way down faster than she meant to.

The Guild House stood against the Hollow's north wall, where the limestone was driest, a long pale building with too many windows, and the Day-book room sat at the top of its east wing, up four flights of a stair that had been designed by someone who disliked clerks. It was a long room under a skylight, full of slanting desks and ink-stained racks, and it had one cupboard at the back that was Reet's. Inside were her copies: every Shift since she was fifteen, a fat ledger for each winter, the Sheets she had inked and the notes she had made at the edges of them. She had never needed to look anything up. She kept them for the same reason a man with a good memory for faces keeps a portrait of his mother.

She had the Kuld frame inked and set to dry on the rack when the door opened.

Mihkel Org was round, and bald as an egg except for a fringe dyed a walnut brown found in no living creature. He had been Master-Surveyor for nineteen years and had a face like a man holding in a sneeze. The mustard waistcoat strained at its buttons. There was a fine white dusting of sugar down one lapel.

"Sallik. Forty chains."

"Yes, Master."

"With the *chain*."

"Yes."

"Rule Fourteen. A rider's hour belongs to the Sheet. The Sheet goes to the printers at the sixth bell, it is now a quarter to, and I am informed by a lamp-boy that you were seen strolling to the livery like a woman with an independent income." He looked at the frame on the rack. It was done, it was dry and it was exact, and Reet watched him be disappointed by it, as a man is who has loaded a speech and finds there is no one to fire at. "Rule Eleven," he said regardless. "The rider reports the ground. The interpretation of it belongs to the Keeper. Since you have enthusiasm to spare, you may spend it on the quarterly audit of the copy room: every tracing against the leaf it was taken from, pinholes, ticks and all. The stove wants its ash taken down. And Pille's pencils."

"What's wrong with Pille's pencils?"

"I am told they are a disgrace." He turned for the door and put a hand, casually, over the pocket of the waistcoat, where something small and flaky crackled. "That will be all."

Reet looked at the sugar on his lapel. She did not mention it, which was what made it worse for him.

She did the ash. She sharpened Pille's pencils, and there were fourteen of them, and every one chewed. And then, because the Sheet had gone to the printers and nobody had said what she was to do with the rest of the day, she went to the cupboard at the back of the room and began taking out the ledgers.

She had meant to be quick. At the third ledger she stopped being quick. At the eighth she was on the floor with the long table cleared and the Sheets spread out along it like cards, and the afternoon had gone grey in the skylight, and she had not noticed.

The Correction Book lived on a lectern in the great hall, chained, and its old volumes in a rack beneath it, open to anyone who cared to read them. It was a public thing and a boring one. She had gone down at noon and read the last eight years of it in an hour and a half, not because she needed to take notes, but because Mihkel believed in paper the way other men believed in saints, and she wanted something to put in front of him. Now she copied it out, strip by strip, in the clerk's round hand, onto slips as long as her palm. She laid each slip beside the Dawn Sheet for a day nine nights after.

*Leaf 56, Tallow Moor, the north hill re-inked, minor.* Nine nights on, the hill at Tallow Moor had got up in the dark and walked a quarter-mile east, and every rider in the loft had sworn at it for a week.

*Leaf 71, Kask Ford, bend of the river re-inked.* The river at Kask Ford had taken a new bend, to the great distress of a miller.

*Leaf 88, Sora Crossing, the marsh drain re-inked.* Last spring. Her mother had written: *The drain's moved, can you imagine, four hundred yards, overnight, and the whole street's in a state.* Reet had read the letter and thought: that's a Shift. It had not occurred to her to look at what the Book said nine nights before.

She laid them down one by one. Fourteen. Fourteen entries, fourteen Shifts, and every one of the fourteen nine nights apart, to the night. The first was eight years old. Before it, in the Book's earlier volumes, the re-inkings had been about damp and mice and a spilt inkwell in a wet March, and not one of them matched a thing on the ground. After it, they matched every time. Whoever had started doing this had not started cautiously.

She sat back on her heels. Her hands were cold, and the skylight above the table had gone the grey of old pewter.

"Sallik," said a voice behind her. "Why is my floor covered in fish?"

"It's not fish, Master. It's Shifts."

Mihkel stood in the door with a paper bag in one hand and the expression of a man who has come to eat something in private and found it occupied. He crossed to the table with his short quick steps and looked down at the row of Sheets and slips, and his eyes went along them, and she saw him add it up. He was not stupid. That was the thing people missed about Mihkel. He read from left to right and he got to the end.

"Fourteen," she said. "Each of them nine nights after an entry in the Book. The leaf is re-inked, and nine nights later the ground does what the leaf says. All in the last eight years."

"Re-inking is maintenance."

"Then it's maintenance that moves a river."

"The Seam moves every night of the year. Take eight years of Shifts and eight years of anything, Sallik, and you will find fourteen of something that agrees with the price of onions." He said it kindly, which was worse. "The Atlas records. When the ground changes, the Atlas is corrected to match it, and the Book makes a note. That is not a theory. It is Rule Three."

"Then why is the Atlas nine nights early?"

"Clerks are meticulous."

"Nine nights, fourteen times."

"Rule Eleven," said Mihkel, with some relief, as a man steps onto a good dry plank. "The rider reports. The Keeper interprets. You have reported, and I thank you, and I will mention it." He looked at the slips for a moment longer, and the bag in his hand crackled, and she saw him decide something. "You have told me you can see the Atlas is not what it says. Very well. Choose a leaf for the audit. Any leaf. It will be traced under watch, as the Rules direct, and you may lay your hand on the vellum and see that ink is ink."

"Eighty-eight."

"Your own Sora." He sighed through his nose. "Sentiment. Come."

She had never been inside the vault, and she was not inside it now. She stood in the antechamber below the east wing, a bare stone passage with a lamp in a cage and an iron door at the end of it with three locks, and watched Mihkel take a key from a ribbon under the waistcoat. There were three keys, he said, without being asked: the Keeper's, his own, and a third that lived in a sealed box in the Assembly's strong-room. It took two to open the door. The Keeper's had been left for him, as it was every quarter for the audit. He went in. The door closed on a smell of cold air and old vellum, and she counted forty seconds, and he came out with a flat case lined with felt, and handed it to her as one hands over an infant.

"Rule Nineteen," he said. "Two gloves on vellum." They were cotton, grey from other people's hands. She put them on, and the left one, over the ink, felt like a bandage. "Tõnis."

Tõnis was the vault porter, seventy-one and about as quick as a glacier, and he went up behind her with a lantern, watching her as a cat watches a bird it is too old to chase.

The copy room had a north window the size of a barn door and a long table of frosted glass, lit from underneath. She laid Leaf 88 on it and the room went quiet.

Sora Crossing was not large on the vellum. A marsh, a mill, a ford, a street, the green line of the drain, and a plum tree where her mother's house stood, drawn by some hand a long time dead with a single curl of the pen. She had loved this leaf at seventeen. She had traced it herself two years ago, sitting where she sat now, and the tracing was in the file. She took it out and laid it over the leaf. The street matched. The mill matched. The drain was in the wrong place, as it should have been; the leaf had been corrected. And down in the lower margin, where the dividers had pivoted when the first hand drew it, the pinhole did not match.

The Guild's dividers always went in five millimetres from the gutter. It was the oldest habit in the Atlas, a rule so old that no one remembered making it. The old tracing had its hole at five. The leaf in front of her had its hole at four.

She measured it twice with her steel rule. She did not need to. She knew it was four as she knew the date of her own birth.

A new hole was a new drawing. You did not redo a leaf and keep the old hole; you set the dividers down again and they pierced the vellum again, a little off, because no hand sets down twice in the same place. Somebody had redrawn Sora's marsh. Somebody had redrawn it with dividers that sat down a millimetre wrong.

In the lower right corner, where the new ink had gone on, the black was glossy, as if it had not dried. The rest of the leaf had faded to brown over three hundred years. This corner shone like a beetle. Behind her, Tõnis breathed through his mouth and was, by the sound of it, asleep on his feet.

Reet slid the point of her pen-knife under the edge of the new ink and lifted a sliver the size of a grain of rice. It came away clean. She put it into the thumb of her glove and drew the cotton over it, and it was the first thing she had done in four years that was not in the Rules, and it took no time at all.

She made two tracings. One went in the file, as the Rules required. The second she made on onion-skin so thin it floated, and folded it small and put it inside her shirt. She did not need it. She could have drawn the leaf blind in a cellar. But a tracing was for everyone else; her own head was the one place in the Guild that nobody could be asked to trust.

The assay hall was on the ground floor, in a long vaulted gallery with green glass lamps, where the shelves rose to the ceiling and the vials stood in their thousands in racks, each corked and sealed and labelled with a place, a date and a name. It looked like a church built by a pharmacist. She had meant only to leave the Kuld vial in its row. Thirty-one, third rack. But on her way down the aisle her eye went along the numbers as it always did, and stopped.

Row 88 had held ninety-four vials in August. She knew; she had walked it. Now it held six at the far end, and where the rest had been the oak showed pale in rings, a long shelf of clean circles with a hand's width of dust between them. She went on to 163. Palu's valley had been a hundred and twelve vials, from every ford and well and bridge-foot, the careful work of eleven years of riders. The shelf was bare. It was as clean as a plate.

"Mistress Piret," she said.

The assay-keeper looked up from a ledger and an ear-trumpet. "Mm?"

"Where is Palu's row?"

"Breakage."

"A hundred and twelve vials."

"Glass," said Piret, "is a terrible material." She went back to her ledger. Reet stood with the Kuld vial in her hand, and then put it in its slot, and it looked very small and very alone.

There were nineteen other rows with gaps in them. She counted. She did not need to.

It was the lamp-lighting hour when she reached the great hall. The clerk had just finished his evening round, and was leaning on his stick with his spectacles pushed up, blotting. The Correction Book lay open on its lectern with the day's last line still shining in the lamplight. She read it from three feet away, and then, because she had been taught to be sure, from one.

*Leaf 163, Palu valley, re-inked.*

Her stomach turned over, slowly, like a fish under ice. Palu had nine hundred people in it and forty rope bridges. She had inked its Sheet a dozen times. She knew the gorge and the church and the shape of the east ridge above it, bare as an egg. She stood there with the clerk coughing behind her, and the ordinary feet of the Guild going by, and she lifted her hands.

She counted on her fingers: the left bare now, the cotton gone, ticking off one finger at a time against the ink-dyed knuckles of the right. Tonight was none. Tomorrow was one. She went on down the days, through the ones that were Toomas Veskimae's and Mihkel's and the Keeper's, and out the other end of her fingers, until the last one she had was a night with a number on it.

She stopped on the tenth.
