# The Road That Ended

The road ended at breakfast, which Toomas thought rude of it.

He had woken with the knee. It did not wait for the Shift to be over to complain of it; it began at the first grey, a hot bar laid across the joint, and he lay under the wagon for some while doing his sums. A man of sixty-two who has slept on ground that moved in the night should be grateful to be lying in one piece. He tried gratitude. The knee did not accept it. So he crawled out into a morning the colour of skimmed milk, got the chain-staff under him and levered himself up, and found the whole camp standing at the edge of the world with its porridge going cold.

The wagons had come to rest in a ring, as they were meant to. That was the odd mercy of the Seam: it set you down in your own arrangement. But forty yards beyond the northmost wagon the heath stopped. It did not slope. It simply left off, in a clean raw lip of turf with the roots hanging out, and past the lip was air, and in the air a long way down was a river the width of a bootlace, lying in a gorge with a mist on it.

"Yesterday," said Hendrik Loo, the Company's master, "there was a road."

"There was," said Aino.

"With a ditch on the left and a milestone."

"Eleven miles to Kesk's well. I know. I looked at the milestone myself." She held a bowl of porridge in one hand and her salt-hook in the other, and appeared to have forgotten which was which. "It's gone, Hendrik. It's down there somewhere, with the ditch."

Master Loo did not remove his hat, because he never did, but he turned it a quarter-turn on his head, which in a man of his habits amounted to a scream. Behind him the wool-train's mistress had come across from her own ring, a thin woman in a man's coat called Mägi, as her train was, and she stood with her hands in her sleeves and said that the Seam had done the same to her grandmother at Tallow Moor and that grandmother had gone round.

"Round which way?" said Loo.

"She never said. She died before she'd got there."

They argued for the better part of an hour. West, said Loo, because the Company's charter named west in bad ground. East, said Mistress Mägi, because wool did not like to be wet and the western lip had a look of drizzle. Someone proposed lowering the wagons by rope, and was asked by whom, and into what. Toomas leaned on his staff and let it wash past him; he had sat through forty years of the Assembly, and he knew the sound of men arguing in order not to be the one who decided. It was the sound of a kettle that has been told it may not boil.

In the end the oxen decided. A beast called Auditor, who had stood at the edge looking down with the long patience of his office, turned his head, swung it west along the rim, and began to walk. The Guildmaster fell in behind him, and Treasurer behind that, and the whole line of them went off at the plodding pace of animals who know they are being watched, and the masters, seeing their cattle depart, found that they had reached an agreement.

"West," said Loo, with dignity.

"I always said west," said Mistress Mägi.

* * *

Toomas rode ahead on a pony called Clerk, which Aino had lent him on the grounds that Clerk reads nothing and carries everything. It was a mealy, short-legged animal with an expression of settled disappointment. Reet rode beside him on the Company's second pony, sitting it as she did everything, upright and exactly, as if the saddle had been surveyed.

They went at a walk along the rim, a quarter-mile in front of the oxen. This was the work he had been trained for, once, and it surprised him how easily it came back, like a dance learned in youth. The ground in the Seam was a language that kept changing its nouns. You could not trust the nouns. You could trust the grammar.

"The rim runs west by north," he said. "And the heath tilts toward it. Do you know why?"

"It's been pulled," said Reet. "The turf on this side is stretched. Look at the heather, it's been combed toward the edge. Something heavy went over, and took the ground with it." She did not turn her head. Her grey eyes were running along the lip as a finger runs along a column. "Four hundred and ten paces back we crossed a hawthorn that has its roots on the far side of a wall. It's moved about seven yards from its wall. The wall hasn't."

"And so?"

"And so the slope is new, and it's pitched about one in nine, and the lip isn't going to give. I'd trust it to a pony. I wouldn't trust it to a wagon."

"Mm. Say that shorter."

"The edge will hold you. It won't hold the Company."

"Better." He looked down the slope with his good eye, and then with the bad one, which saw a glare and a smear and had the advantage of not being impressed. "The contour grammar would say we are on a convex slope, falling away, with the ticks pointing down and the lines crowding. A ledge, then, where the lines spread out. Do you see where the lines spread?"

"No."

"Nor do I. That is why we are riding."

It took them forty minutes. The ledge, when they found it, was a shelf of limestone twenty feet wide that ran out from the rim like a tongue from a mouth, and at the end of it, bright against the grey, was something that did not belong to the morning at all. It was red. It was a post of new red larch, as thick as a man's thigh, driven into a crack in the rock and bound with tarred cord, and from the post a bridge went out over nothing.

Reet counted it. He watched her do it, her lips moving without sound. Ninety-two feet of rope and plank, hand-rails of twisted hemp, the planks laid crosswise and wired, and on the far side a second red post, small as a matchstick. It swayed in the wind of the gorge. Under it there was a thousand feet of morning.

"Someone built this last night," she said.

"Mm. Someone who was caught on the same side of the same cliff as we were, with a quantity of rope and no sense of occasion." He pointed at the near post, where a tin tag swung on a nail. She dismounted and read it aloud.

"'Kask and Daughters, salt, southbound. Left for the next. Mind the ninth plank.'" She looked up. "It's the custom?"

"It is the custom. A caravan that is lucky leaves what it built. A caravan that is unlucky does not need anything." He tapped the red wood. "I have always found that the road has better manners than the people who govern it."

* * *

The Salt Company reached the ledge by noon and stood in a long and uneasy line along it, looking at the bridge as if it had said something about their mothers.

Aino did not say a word. That was how Toomas knew. She was a woman who narrated; she had named thirty-six oxen after men she disliked and addressed each of them daily, she had heckled a wool train across a hundred yards of dark. Now she stood at the near post with her scarf wound to the chin and looked at the planks, and all the colour went out of her except the chipped front tooth. She said nothing for a minute and a half. Then she said, in a voice that carried to the back of the line, "Well, it's only *rope*."

"Aino," said Reet.

"It's perfectly good rope. Look at it. Tarred. Somebody's *mother* made that rope." Her voice was climbing. "Hendrik, who's first? Somebody's first. Is it the oxen? It should be the oxen, they've no imagination. I'm only saying, I'm the guard, the guard goes *last*, it's in the charter, somebody has to watch the rear—"

"Aino," said Toomas.

"—and there's no rear, there's a very large *hole*—"

"Aino. Is the charter in the habit of saying that?"

She stopped. She looked at him, and for a moment she was very young and very tall and seemed to be standing on something that was already moving. "No," she said, in her ordinary voice. "No, it isn't. I made that up."

"Ah." He nodded. "Good. We have established it."

Nobody said anything else about it, then or afterwards. Reet crossed first, on foot, leading Clerk, counting the planks aloud in a level voice that carried back to the ledge: "Seven, eight, nine, mind it, ten." The bridge swung as the pony stepped, a long slow swing that went out and came back like a pendulum, and the wind took a plank and set it dancing, and Toomas, standing at the near post with his staff, thought that if he lived he would never again complain about a staircase. The first ox went over after her. The second. The line began to pour.

Taavi crossed with his eyes shut and one hand locked on the hemp. He got to the middle, where the bridge hung lowest and sang in the wind, and stopped, and leaned out over the side with great dignity and was sick into the gorge. "I'm so sorry," he called, to nobody, to the whole sky. "Technically that's below us, it should be fine, it should be miles before—"

"It is a thousand feet," said Reet from the far end. "Four seconds. Keep walking."

"Four seconds to *where*?"

"Keep walking."

The trouble came at the eleventh ox. Registrar was a big, slate-coloured, aggrieved animal, the oldest in the Company, and he walked up to the first plank, and put one hoof on it, and took it off again. He looked at the gorge. He looked at the bridge. He sat down. It was a considerable sitting, and it took the whole line behind him by surprise; the oxen piled up on the ledge, and a wagon-man swore, and Registrar closed his eyes with the serenity of a creature that has refused petitions all his life and will not be hurried by a petition from rope.

"He's got principles," said Aino, who had come forward, grey in the face. "He's been refusing everything since he was a calf. I named him well."

It was then that Toomas saw the far post move.

It was not much. A hand's breadth. The weight on the span had grown, and the red larch on the far side, set in a crack of rotten shale, leaned toward the gorge with a small dry crackle, and the whole bridge gave a lurch that sent two oxen into the rail. He heard Reet call something. He was already moving, as fast as the knee would let him, which was not fast, across the swaying planks with the chain-staff in his fist.

"What are you doing?" Aino shouted. "Veskimae!"

"Crossing, I believe!"

He reached the far end at a limp. The post was leaning, the guy-cord was singing, and the crack it stood in was widening at the lip in a thin trail of grit. He looked about him for ground that had stood a long time. Behind the post the rock was sound, grey, old, a ridge of it, running back into the heath. He took the chain-staff in both hands, set its iron shoe into the nearest seam, and leaned on it with the whole of his weight, which was considerable, and wedged it. Then he took the guy-cord, passed it twice about the shaft, and tied it off in a knot he had not used since he was twenty-three.

"There," he said. "Do you know what the Keeper before me said about moustaches?"

"What?" said Reet, who was holding the post.

"He said that a man's moustache is the only part of him that sees the future, because it arrives first. He had a very large one. There was a morning in the Assembly when a sparrow was found to have nested in it, and he would not allow it to be disturbed until the fledglings had flown, and so for nine days the Guild's accounts were audited by a man who could not nod." He gave the knot a final pull. "On the tenth day, the sparrows left. And the Keeper, being a man of regular habits, then took a pair of scissors to the whole thing, and has been clean-shaven ever since. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Because he said that he had learned, from the sparrows, that a thing is only worth keeping if something can live in it." He looked up. "I have never been able to decide whether he meant the moustache."

On the bridge behind them, and this was what he had been aiming for, the sound had changed. Aino was laughing. It was a cracked, furious, terrified laugh that went on and on, and between the gusts of it she was saying "a *sparrow*, oh, Veskimae, you old liar, a sparrow," and the planks under her were creaking with each step she took toward them. She did not look down. She came off the last plank on her hands and knees, in the shale, with the laugh still going out of her like air from a bellows, and Reet took her by the elbow.

"Not a word," said Aino.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to. Not a word. Not one." She stood, and wiped her face on the red scarf, and pointed with the salt-hook back along the line. "Somebody get that ox up."

Registrar, as it turned out, crossed with Reet in front of him, walking backwards, counting aloud. He took one plank at a time, with the air of an animal conferring a favour.

* * *

By the middle of the afternoon the Company was over, wagon by wagon, hauled across with the oxen unhitched and the wheels roped, and the last of the wool train had begun to follow. The wind had dropped. Toomas sat on a boulder on the south side with the knee stretched in front of him and a tin mug of tea that Taavi had made him, and for the first time that day nobody wanted anything from him.

He looked at the land.

It was very beautiful in the daylight. That was always the cruelty of it. The gorge below ran away in a long blue haze, and the cliffs on the far side were striped in rose and cream, and a hawk went round and round in the clear air with its wings quite still. He knew this country. He had walked it at thirty, with a chain and a plumb-bob, under a Keeper who had made him measure every bend of a river to the link. There had been a ridge here, a modest one, with a stand of birch. There had been a ford. He could see it still, the way a man sees the face of someone he has quarrelled with, laid over the true one: the ford was there, in his head, as clear as ink.

And it was fifty feet to the left. All of it. The gorge, the rim, the ridge, the whole bearing of the country had slid fifty feet from where he had known it, and not a living soul had said a word. It had happened in the night, the way a house gets older.

He drank the tea. It was too sweet. Taavi, who was frightened of him, always put in too much.

A river, he thought. A long thin stroke of blue-black, drawn with a steady hand under a lamp, from the high ground down to the plain, and how quietly the nib had gone, and how it had hardly seemed—

He put the memory down. He put it down as one puts down a pan that has no handle, quickly and without looking at it, and shook the fingers that had held it. It lay where it fell. It would be there tonight. It was always there at night.

"Toomas?" said Reet. She had come up beside him, with her left hand ink-black to the wrist, and was watching him with the exact look she gave the ground. "You've gone white."

"It is the tea," he said. "Taavi has put in a quantity of sugar sufficient to embalm a registrar."

"It's not the tea."

"Mm. Say that shorter."

"It's not the tea," said Reet.

Neither of them said anything more. Behind them a wagon-man began to sing, badly. Down on the ledge across the gorge, the last two wool carts waited their turn, small as beetles.

Then Reet's head came up.

He saw it a moment after she did: on the north rim, where they had come from, far across the gorge, a smear of tawny dust was rising into the clear air. It rose in a feather. Under the feather, as it thickened, he saw dark specks that were horses, and the glint of a bridle, and the specks were coming along the rim at a trot, in a bunch, and then in a line. Nobody on the ledge had noticed. Aino was shouting at an ox.

"How many?" said Reet quietly.

He counted them. He had counted a great many things in his life, and he did it as he always did, twice, to be certain. "Twelve," he said. "Twelve riders."

"Can you tell who?"

The Seam had given him a morning of bright, hard, cold light, and a white eye that saw nothing and a brown one that saw very well at a distance. At the head of the line, a good way in front of the rest, there was a rider on a heavy bay, round as a barrel in the saddle, and on his chest, plain across half a mile of air, was a small hard patch of colour like a button of mustard.

"Mm," said Toomas, and set the tea down on the rock. "That would be Org."
