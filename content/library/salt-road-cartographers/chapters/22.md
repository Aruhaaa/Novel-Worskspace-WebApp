# The Ninth Night

Palu sang the ferry song the way it did everything, which was loudly, in four keys, and with the goats joining in.

It had begun at dusk, when the last stake was driven and the boiler taken off its fire, as something to do with the hands and the mouth, and the children started it, jumping on *stays*. By full dark it had gone through the whole ridge. Nine hundred people sat on the turf in their coats with their knees drawn up, round a few small fires of birch, and sang what they had only ever skipped over: *the river rose up in the dead of the night*. They did it badly. They did it with their whole chests. Toomas, who had first heard it sung by a one-eared ferryman in a hut with a goat on his boot, found that he minded the tuneless version less than he would have thought. It had been made for poling and wading, not for choirs. It was a song for people with something to do.

He sat with his back against a stake. It was Ruus's gate, and he had asked Aino to put him by it. Beside him on the turf, her head on his rolled coat and a heap of sheepskins over her, lay Reet, with her bare left hand outside the fleece, the fingers a little curled and the ink dried black into the creases. She had sat down where she stood at the foot of the Charter, politely, as a child sits when told to, and Taavi had caught her under the arms. She had not woken. He had laid the back of his hand once against her forehead, like a mother, and it had been cold.

On her other side, on an upturned bucket, with a lantern between his shoes, sat Mihkel Org.

"Rule Forty," said Mihkel, in a low voice, to nobody, as another man might have read the time. "'No Surveyor sleeps unwatched in a shifting place.'" He looked at the sleeper. "She is not unwatched."

"No," said Toomas.

"I have not left her for six hours."

"No."

"I find," said Mihkel, "that it is not a bad Rule."

Far down the ridge, at the edge of the firelight, the Keeper still sat on her milking stool. She had not gone. Toward evening she had said a few words to her riders, and they had ridden off up the road to the upland to tell the carts to stand down, and since then she had been alone, with her hands in her lap and her back as straight as a plumb-line. Someone had put a sheepskin round her shoulders. She had let it stay.

Below them, in the valley, Palu burned. It was Liisu's doing. Every house had been left with a lamp lit in its window, two hundred of them, and the forty bridges had their lanterns hung as on a feast night, so that the town lay at the bottom of the dark in a great scatter of small yellow lights, like a hearth seen from above, like a handful of coals. "Houses should be able to see where they're going," Liisu had said, and nobody had argued. It was a thing that should have been absurd. It was beautiful, and it hurt to look at.

He did not look at it for long. He looked at the child.

She had come to sit at his other side an hour ago, with her knees up, in a sheepskin that reached her feet, and she had not said anything. Now she said, flatly, "I've got the last verse."

"Mm?"

"The names one. Reet had it in her book. She said I could copy it." She was looking at the lights. "Nobody's sung it. Do you want me to?"

He found that he could not answer. He had left a hut once because of that verse; he had gone out into a yard and put his back to a wall and heard it through the planking, name by name, and known them all. He looked at his great-niece, who had Helju's chin and the whole of Helju's unreasonable courage, and who was eleven. He saw that she was not offering him a kindness. She was asking whether the thing could be done, in the way that she asked whether a bridge would bear.

"It is not a thing you owe them," said Toomas.

"I know. I want to." She considered. "They stood too."

She sang it. It was not a good voice, a thin flat clear one like a reed whistle, and she sang it into the dark without once looking at him, to the same tune as the other, the old rocking tune, up and down like a boat. *Marten the baker and Ilse his wife, and Mikk who was six, who was six, who was six.* She had the whole of it from Reet's page. She sang the cooper and the twins who never were; she sang Rein at the mill and Anni at the loom and Jakob and Helmi and Oskar. A few voices near her took it up, those who knew the tune, and then those who did not, humming, and the hum went outward through the dark like a ripple in a pool until the whole ridge had it, without words, nine hundred people holding a note under the stars for eleven strangers.

Toomas stayed where he was. He kept his back to the stake, with his hands on the chain-staff, and he did not leave.

A big shape sat down heavily on the turf at his other side. It did not speak. It smelled of tallow and bell-rope. They listened to the end together, he and Jaan, with a yard of cold air between them and a sleeping girl on his other hand, and when the last note had gone out of the world Jaan said, to the dark, "She'd have hit you with a spoon."

"She would," said Toomas.

"After."

"After," Toomas agreed. His voice had gone. He cleared it. "Yes. I think after."

* * *

It came a little before midnight, as Reet had said it would, and it began with the quiet.

The wind dropped. The singing, which had run down by then into something more like muttering, stopped in the middle of a line, as a clock stops, and no one started it again. Every sound on the Rise began to come back half a breath late. A goat bleated and was answered by its own bleat out of the air. And from the south, from a long way off, there came a very low noise, such as a loaded wagon makes on a distant bridge, and it came toward them at the pace of a man walking.

He had felt it from inside, in the Seam, on a road, in a camp ring, on the edge of a pass that was not there. He had never in his life watched one from outside. In twelve years he had thought of it in his bed, in the dark, the thing he had done: a river led off the leaf, a wet noise, a flood. He had never once seen it land. He had gone home from the vault and been sick in a basin, and the water had gone to Soo with nobody watching.

The Rise did not stir. The turf under his hands was as still as a table. It was down in the valley that the world began to move.

The lights lifted.

They lifted all together, every lamp in every window of every house, two hundred and some, and the lanterns on the bridges with them, as a chest lifts on a held breath. For the length of that breath Palu hung a hand's breadth clear of the earth, a whole town of yellow windows with the black gorge underneath, and he heard on the ridge around him nine hundred people not breathing. Then it slid.

It came toward them across the dark like a tray carried by an invisible hand. The sound had swelled to a grinding roar, and under it, small and clear, the bridges' ribbons rattling in their thousands like rain on slates; and the lights tilted as they came, heeling over, and he thought *no*, and thought *the ridge is held*, and saw the first of the houses strike the long slope of the Rise a hundred yards below and ride up on it, and go over.

They arrived in a heap. There was no other word for it. They came up the slope in a long tumbling slide, roofs on chimneys, walls on gates, a hay-loft with its door open sitting on a pump, like crockery in a cart going over a rut, and for a space that he afterward found he could not measure there was only noise, and lamps rolling, and a woman on the ridge screaming a name over and over. He had his hand across Reet's body. He did not know when he had put it there.

And then, from the bottom of the heap, one by one, the houses began to work themselves loose.

They did it as hens do on a nest. A wall shifted, turned, a shoulder shrugged itself free of the next shoulder. A roof rose up on one side and sat back, true. Gables swung, chimney-stacks settled; two houses rubbed up against each other and, with the tired air of neighbours, parted and moved apart by exactly the width of a lane. Each one went a little way across the slope and stopped. He saw a door come to rest on an iron pin: a hand's breadth from it, and then, with a faint shuffle, on it. Lamps still burned in the windows. Not one of them had gone out.

"Ruus's gate," somebody said, near him, in a small stunned voice. "That's Ruus's gate."

A split-oak post with an iron ring in it stood in the lamplight beside the stake at his elbow. It had come up the slope with a hedge, and a ditch, and a long thorny field falling away east, and a stile of two flat stones.

Then the bell-tower came.

It was the last of them. It was an old tall square thing, windowed south, and it rode up the slope above the heap and came to a stand with its stones grinding, a little crooked, before the bell had decided that it had arrived. Then the bell spoke. It struck once, from the jolt, a single note, cracked as a pot, and Jaan Pold, beside him on the turf, put his two hands over his face.

"It rang itself," he said, through his fingers.

"It would."

"It's never rung without me."

"Then you have taught it well."

After the tower there was a long moment in which nothing moved. Then, from the lower edge of the new town, came a sound like a child's laugh: a single high peal of astonishment, which was a goat. It was standing on the ridge-pole of a shed, an ordinary larch shed, which had arrived whole and upright on a lawn of turf, and it was looking down at the township from above with the offended air of a creature who has been moved without consultation. It was Ivo Tamm's goat. It had slipped its rope at sunset, in the matter of a principle, and gone home.

"My goat!" bellowed Ivo Tamm, from a hundred feet away. "My *goat*! Marit, it's on the *roof*!"

The widow Saar's voice, high and exalted, floated up out of the dark: "Then it's on *yours*, you fool!" And nine hundred people began to laugh. It was an awful, ragged, helpless sound; half of it was weeping. They laughed on the dark ridge in their coats as the new houses steamed gently in the cold. Somebody was already calling out the roll.

"Registrar!" That was Aino, enormous, on a heap of stone at the lane end. She had her hook in the air. "Auditor! No, you thieves, not the oxen, I mean *you*: Lauris! Mänds! Tamms! Hands up!" And she counted them in, a household at a time, with the names of every Guild office she could call to mind, and he sat with the chain-staff across his knees and listened to the roll. They were all there. Nine hundred and three, said Aino. And then, in a different voice, "Nine hundred and four. Hold on. Who's that?"

Nobody could account for it. But in the second house from the bell-tower a woman was seen at the window with a bundle that had not been there at dusk, and a small fierce cry rose from it into the cold and was loudly answered by a goat.

In the valley, behind them, the sound had changed.

He turned his head. Where Palu had been there was a darkness without a light in it, a wide black bowl; and out of the head of the gorge, out of the throat of the thin river that was Toomas Veskimae's work and no one else's, there came the long hush of water. It came down into the bowl in a tongue, pale in the starlight, pushing out on either side, filling. The rope bridges had gone with their town. Nothing was left of them on the water but the ribbons, the feast-ribbons, thousands of them, red and blue and yellow, riding on the dark in a thin bright scatter like petals on a pond.

* * *

He found her at a little after one, at the edge of the water.

She was standing where the cart-road ran down into the lake, a small figure with a rope of silver down her back, ankle-deep in the shallows, quite still. The new lake lay at her feet to the far shore, with a faint light on it, and the water came up the slope and stopped at her boots as if she had told it to. He came down the hill slowly, with the chain-staff, on the knee. The turf became shingle. He stood beside her at a respectful distance of three feet.

"Mm," said Toomas. "Is it as you drew it?"

"The shore is four yards east of where I put it." She did not turn her head. Her gloves were still on. "That is the Rise. The leaf has it hatched as a cliff, from a Dawn Sheet of the year ninety-one. Some clerk who had not stood on it wrote *cliff*, and I drew what was written." Her voice was perfectly level. "I have always drawn what was written. I did not know there was a Rise."

"Nobody had told you."

"No." The water moved against her boots. "I did not ask."

He waited. He had been waiting for this woman, in one form or another, for twelve years; in the dark he had composed a number of things to say to her, some unpardonable, and found he had none of them. He stood in the cold and looked at the lake.

"How long will she have?" said Kaie. "The Surveyor. After tonight."

"I think all of it."

"Naturally." It was said without edge. It was the lightest thing he had ever heard her say. "I lost a road, the first time. It was the road to Tallow Moor. I thought: I can bear a road." She was silent a moment. "I cannot tell you where Sora Crossing lies, Toomas. I drew it. I could give you every contour of Leaf Eighty-eight, and every ditch. I could not find it if you set me down in its marsh."

He looked down at her. The small, composed, upturned face was a stranger's, and was not. It was the face of a girl of thirty-six who had held a lamp for him, in the vault, while he did a terrible thing, and who had known exactly what it was.

"She will remember her mother's face," said Toomas.

"Faces stay?"

"I believe so. It seems to be the arrangement."

"That is something." Kaie drew her hands together and held them. "Go back to her. Someone should be there. I find that I would rather it were not myself."

He went back up the slope. At the top he turned, and she was still there, ankle-deep in her own lake, watching the ribbons go out on it in the dark.

* * *

Reet woke at dawn.

He had been awake all night. He had sat with his back to Ruus's stake and watched the sky go the colour of a pearl, and at some point in the long grey wait Mihkel Org had fallen asleep on his bucket, upright, with the lantern gone out in his hands. And so he saw it: how she woke slowly, as someone does who has been a long way down. First she looked at the sky. Then she looked at him.

"Toomas," said Reet. "You've got tea in your moustache."

"I have not had tea for a day and a half."

"It's still there." She sat up. Her cropped hair stood on end and her left hand was black to the wrist. She looked about her at the new street, at the smoke going up from a hundred chimneys, at the goat on the roof, and he watched her look at it with the face of a stranger at a window. "Whose town is that?"

"Palu's. Yours, I should say."

"Palu." She turned it over. "I know the name. I know we came here. I know I was afraid of something." She frowned. "There was a road. We came along a road. I know there was one, because I know your knee hurt. I can't find it." She pressed the heel of her hand to her forehead, as if to push a door. "I can't find any of them. It's not like forgetting. Forgetting has an edge. This is only snow."

He had nothing wise to say. He had never had anything wise to say.

"The cairn," said Reet slowly. "Dry Cairn. I went there every morning for three years. Pille's mule is called Biscuit. I can see Pille." She said it with a sort of wonder. "Broad. Burnt nose. A plait. I can see her face. I can't see where she stood."

"That is how it works, I believe."

"Then it's all right." She said it quite steadily. "I know all of you. I know what you said. I know Aino took me by the scarf and the hair. I've lost the places and kept the people. Toomas, that's the right way round. Isn't it?"

He had to look at the sky. "I should say so."

Taavi arrived at a run with a heel of bread and a great many apologies, and Liisu with a notebook, and Aino with her hook, and in the noise that followed he slipped away, which he was good at. He went down the new street between the houses. It was a long, steep, crooked street with a gutter down the middle that had not been there the day before, already running with a spill of water from the seep; and here and there, at a doorway, a man or a woman stood in the grey dawn with an expression of complete bewilderment, laying a hand on a lintel as one lays it on a horse.

The churchyard had come. It stood behind the bell-tower in its rows, thirty-one stones of the Pold family and some hundred more, the moss still on the north face of each. The white stone stood among them. It had not so much as a chip out of it.

He took off his hat, which he was not wearing, and put his hand up to the place on his head where it would have been.

"Helju," said Toomas. "I am told I speak in questions. This is not one." He cleared his throat. There was nobody near but a robin on the wall. "I am going to Soo. There are forty wells there, I am given to understand, and I am going to map every one of them to the foot, for nothing, for as long as I have knees. I shall draw only what I see. I swear it by whatever one swears by."

It bound nothing on earth but himself. He had no ink, and no vellum, and no ground to put it on; and that, he saw, was precisely its use.

"You would tell me to say it shorter," he said. She had said it to him across a kitchen table for forty years, with her elbows in the flour: *Say that shorter, Toomas, I'm not paid by the word.* He had carried it into the vault and the Guild and a hundred cold rooms, and given it to a girl of nineteen as if it were his own, and had never once said where he had got it.

"Wells," said Toomas, to the white stone. "I'll map the wells."

* * *

When he came up the slope again, the sun was clear of the east. Reet was standing alone at the end of the ridge, on the edge of the new town, with the light on the side of her face. She had the brass plumb-bob in her hand. She was looking out over the lake, which lay under the early sun as flat and bright as a plate with the last of the ribbons on it, to the farther shore, where beyond a belt of birch the ground rose up long and green and round-shouldered against the sky.

She heard the chain-staff and did not turn.

"What's there?" said Reet.

She pointed. He followed her finger. He looked a good while, because she had asked, and a thing asked deserves to be looked at.

"Mm," said Toomas. "Say that shorter. A hill."
