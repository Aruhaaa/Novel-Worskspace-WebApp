# What Ene Ots Asked

The crock was blue, and it stood on the third shelf to the left of the stove, where it had stood in the other kitchen, in the other house, a quarter of a mile downhill and eleven feet under water.

Toomas noticed it first. He noticed it before the old woman, before the table scrubbed white and the two kitchen chairs and the smell that came off everything, a hard clean smell of lye soap and fried onions. It was a salt-glaze crock of the old pattern, a hand high and wide in the belly, with a chip in the rim shaped like a bite. He had seen it thirty years ago. He had come into that other kitchen as a young surveyor with a chain-boy and a head full of Soo's contours, and a woman had given him cold barley beer from a jug and kept her flour in that crock, and he had written the house on his sheet as the fifth along the lane. He had written down the colour of the door. It had been green.

"Come in or go out," said Ene Ots. "You're letting the heat out."

She sat at the end of the table, in the one chair with arms. She was eighty, and had been small to begin with, and age had folded her up like a carpenter's rule, so that she seemed to be built entirely from joints. A black shawl lay over her shoulders. Two ash sticks, polished dark at the top by her palms, were propped against the table beside her, and her hands were folded on its edge, and they did not shake. Her eyes were the colour of tin. They had not left his face since he came through the door.

She had not asked him to sit. She had not offered tea. On the stove behind her a kettle sat on the hob, cold, with the lid off.

A boy of nine or ten had fetched them from the rise: an egg-woman's grandson, with a runny nose, who had come tearing along the track and halted a good ten feet off to deliver his message at the top of his voice. *Gran Ots says if the Guild's man is on the Slope he's to come to her. She says now. She says bring the girl if he won't come alone.* He had said it in one breath. Then he had run back up the hill without turning round. Toomas had got up off the grass. It had taken longer than he would have liked. Taavi had offered an arm and he had not taken it, and Reet had said nothing and had fallen in a pace behind him as one falls in behind a coffin.

She was in the doorway now. He did not turn round. He knew where she stood, as he knew the knee, by a pressure on the back of his shoulders: a small, level, intelligent attention, taking in the room.

"You came to my door once before," said Ene Ots.

"I did, madam. Thirty years ago. You gave me beer."

"I gave a great many people beer. I don't recall you." She looked at his boots, and up. "I recall the other time. The third day. You came down the hill alone, in a Guild coat. You went into the water to your knees, in your good boots, and you stood in front of Rein's mill with a nail. I watched you from the wash-house roof. I'd gone up to see if the cow had floated." The tin eyes were quite steady. "I thought: there goes a man who knows exactly what he's done."

He said, "I came to look at it."

"We didn't need looking at."

"No."

"Do you know what I've wanted for twelve years?" said Ene Ots. "I'll tell you. Not an apology. I've had an apology: a clerk brought it in a folder, with a seal. Not money. I've had money. I've wanted one thing, and I've wanted it so long that I'd stopped believing I'd see the man to ask." She lifted her hand from the table by an inch. "Name them."

* * *

It was very quiet. Out in the lane a hen complained, and was answered. The kettle ticked.

He had thought a good deal about this moment. He had thought about it in the workshop, with the awl in his hand, at the hour past midnight when the second cup stood mouth-down on its saucer; and in the Hollow, in his old chambers, before they took the keys; and on the road tonight and last night and the night before. He had written speeches. He had been a Keeper of the Atlas, and had a gift for the structure of a sentence. He had composed whole paragraphs, in the long watches, about circumstance and intention and the weight of an office, and he found now that every one of them had gone out of his head like water from a sieve. There was only the ordinary thing. She had asked him a question. He was a man who answered questions.

"Marten Kõiv," said Toomas. "Thirty-four. Baker."

He did not look up at the shelf. He looked at the table, at the white scrubbed grain of it.

"Ilse Kõiv, his wife. Twenty-nine. She kept the shop and the books. Mikk Kõiv, their son. Six years." His voice was a plain voice, slow and low, and it did not alter. "Ode Raidma. Sixty-seven. Cooper's widow."

"Cooper."

"Madam?"

"Cooper," said Ene Ots. "Thirty years she was the cooper. Her husband never lifted an adze in his life. He was the widow in that house, if you like. She'd have spat in your eye for that."

He was silent for the space of a breath. "Ode Raidma," he said. "Sixty-seven. Cooper." It cost him something to say it. He had said it wrong, every night for twelve years, in the dark. "Toivo Pärn, twenty-three, ferryman. Tarmo Pärn, his brother, twenty-three, ferryman. They were twins, and the elder by a quarter of an hour. Rein Aavik, fifty-three, miller." He heard behind him the faint small sound of Reet's breath. "Anni Kuusk, forty-one, weaver. She had a daughter at Kesk, who was sent for. Jakob Mets, seventy, shepherd, whose dog came through. Helmi Vaher, nineteen, dairymaid. She was due to be married at midsummer." He paused. "Oskar Lepik, fifty-eight. Wheelwright."

He stopped. He had counted them as he went, with the fingers of his mind, as he did every night: ten, eleven. It was the entire list. It had taken him less than two minutes.

"Is there any more?" said Ene Ots.

"No, madam."

"There's one you've missed."

His heart went over, once, like a stone turning in a stream. He ran through them. Marten, Ilse, Mikk. Ode. The twins. Rein, Anni, Jakob, Helmi, Oskar. He had them. He had them as he had his own name.

"Eleven," he said.

"Eleven," she agreed. "I only wished to see if you'd count." She shut her eyes briefly. When she opened them, something in her face had altered, a very small thing, a loosening, as if a string pulled through her for twelve years had been given a quarter-inch of slack. "You've said them before."

"Every night."

"Have you."

"I do not sleep before I have said them."

"Good," said Ene Ots, without cruelty. "I'd have thought less of you if you hadn't."

* * *

She talked then. She talked in the same voice, a flat dry level voice, looking not at him but at the stove; and he stood where he was, on the bad leg, and listened.

The flood had come in the dark, in the third week of October. Two of them had been found by morning, and five in the week. The last had been found at the thaw. By the middle of November the ground on the slope was iron, the frost so deep that a spade rang on it like a bell, and the village had gathered on the rise with its dead in the snow, wrapped in sheets, and found that there was nobody to dig. They quarrelled about it. It was a bitter little quarrel and she recalled all of it. The men had said the rise belonged to Hanno Tamm, the farmer above, who wanted a rent. The women had said the dead belonged to nobody but themselves. A clerk had ridden out from the Hollow with a form to say that the new ground was not on any sheet and was therefore, strictly speaking, unsurveyed, and no one had authority to grant it. "Unsurveyed," said Ene Ots, to the stove. "We stood in it. We were standing in it, in the snow."

In the end they had lit fires. They had burned stacked birch on the rise for two days, and thawed a strip of earth three feet deep, and dug with the pick and the poker and the flat of a coal-shovel; and Ilse Kõiv had gone into the ground on the second night, with her boy beside her, in a hole that was not deep enough. They had to go back in the spring and dig it over.

"She was mine," said Ene. "My son's daughter. I took her at nine, when her mother died, and she learned the loaf from that boy at sixteen. She told me that bread was nothing but patience, and I said that was no way to talk to a man." Her mouth moved. "Mikk had a whistle. He used to blow it from the bakery door at five in the morning, and nobody ever had the heart to make him stop. It was in the water. It was in the mud. A girl found it in March, in the shallows, on its string."

"I saw," said Toomas. "On the stone."

"Did you?" said Ene Ots. "Then you've been up there."

"Not until this morning."

"No. You wouldn't have." She was quiet. Her fingers moved on the table, a quarter-turn, like a woman turning a coin. "Twelve years. A clerk from the Hollow once a year, with a folder, to ask if there was any further claim, and the word *lost* on every page. A lost leaf. As if it were a glove." And then, quite gently, she said, "Where were you?"

"At my bench. In Tinta Row. I was binding."

"Binding what?"

"Ledgers. Fish merchants'. A psalter once."

"And in the evenings?"

He said, "I said them."

* * *

He did not defend himself. He had known he would not, and still it was a surprise: there had been a hundred small defences standing by, ready to be called, and not one of them presented itself. He did not say that it was not what he had intended. He did not say that he had been a young man in love with a sister, in the grip of an office he had been taught to consider sacred, or that the water, in his reckoning, had been given somewhere else to go, a place of no account, a pasture. He did not say any of it. He stood with both hands on the chain-staff and the sweat standing on his lip, and the knee beginning, under him, a long slow whining note, and he said nothing, because the woman at the table had not asked him.

He did not weep. He was not a man who did. He knew, with a distant surprise, that he had perhaps hoped to, that it might have been a kind of payment, and that it would have been a theft.

At some point he became aware of the doorway. Reet had not moved. She stood with her back against the jamb and her satchel held to her chest in both arms, and he did not turn his head, but out of the corner of the brown eye he could see the notch in her eyebrow and the cropped dark head, and her face. It was perfectly white, and perfectly attentive. It was the face she turned on a hard piece of ground. It had been a long time since anyone had looked at him with such complete and impartial regard, and he found that he could bear it, and that this was a worse thing than if he could not.

His knee began to buckle. He felt it go, a small treacherous give, and he locked it with his whole will, and the staff creaked under his hands. The old woman's tin eyes flicked down to the leg and up again, and she said nothing, and she left him there. It was another minute before she lifted one hand.

"Sit," said Ene Ots.

He sat. He sat on the second chair, rather too suddenly, and the chair complained. He put the staff across his knees. She did not offer tea.

* * *

Outside, in the lane, Aino Rebane was being told no.

She had come up from the wagons with a sack of salt over each shoulder, a cut lip from an argument with Hendrik Loo, and a great deal of business in her head. The Company turned west at Soo. It always did: its charter stopped at the Slope market, and Loo was not a man who would carry a pilgrim one yard beyond the printed line. So she had bargained two oxen out of him, the slate-grey Registrar and the dun beast she had named for the Master-Surveyor, in lieu of three months' wages, and now she needed a cart and a pony to go behind them. She had found a man in the lane with both. He was thin and bald, with an egg-woman's jaw, and he leaned on his gatepost with his arms crossed and looked at her sacks as a man looks at a fire in someone else's house.

"I've heard whose party you are," he said.

"I'm the guard. I don't have a party."

"You came up the road with Veskimae."

"I came up the road with a hundred and four of the Company's customers, and one of them has a bad leg." She put down the sacks. "You've a cart. I'd call it a good cart. It's got all its spokes. I want it for a month, and I want the pony."

"Not for him."

"I didn't say for him."

"Not for his sake. Not a cart, not a pony, not a drink of water. I'll not do a kindness to that man. I'll not give him a crust." The thin man's voice shook, unexpectedly, with something that had been shut in a long time. "My wife's sister is on that hill."

"Ah," said Aino. She stood for a moment with her hands hanging. She was a woman who always had a joke, and she looked for one, and it did not come. "I'm sorry for it. Truly. I am."

"Aye. Well."

"I'll not ask you for his sake," said Aino. She bent and took up one of the sacks, and set it, with a small clean thump, on the top of the gatepost between his arms. It was Flats salt, grey-white and fine, from the pans at Orav, the best in three provinces. "I'll ask you for the salt. There's two of those. Two sacks for a month's lend, and I'll bring the cart back with all its spokes. If you'd rather shut your gate on it you may, and I'll think no worse of you."

The thin man looked at the sack. He looked, for rather a long time, up the hill toward the rise and the lime trees.

"It's good salt?" he said.

"It's the best you'll get this side of the Flats."

"My wife's been out for a month." He cleared his throat. "I'm not selling for his sake. You understand."

"Nobody asked you to."

"It's for the salt."

"It's for the salt," said Aino. "I'll write it in the book."

* * *

The old woman was looking at her hands.

"I won't forgive you," said Ene Ots.

"No, madam."

"I want that understood. I've no intention of it. If I were to forgive you, I'd be forgiving something that happened to those eleven, and it's not mine to give." She raised her eyes. "But you've something in your mouth. I can see it. You've had it there since you came through that door, like a man with a stone under his tongue, and you've not said it, and I don't think you've ever said it. Have you?"

He opened his mouth. He found, to his great disgust, that his voice had gone.

"Not aloud," said Toomas.

"Then I'll hear the rest." She looked beyond him, at the doorway, at the white-faced girl with the black hands, and her voice dropped, a little, to something very nearly human. "But not from me. I've had enough of you for one day. Go and say it to the girl."

He rose. It took both hands on the staff. He stood for a moment in the clean-smelling kitchen with the blue crock on its shelf and the cold kettle on the stove, and he bowed to the old woman, as he had bowed to the Assembly, from the neck, and she did not return it. He went past Reet. She stood aside to let him through. He did not look at her, and she did not speak.

The yard behind the cottage was small and cold, with a wash-house, and a well, and a washing-line strung between two posts with a single grey sheet on it. A hard wind had come up off the water. He crossed to the middle and stopped, and stood with his back to the house and the staff planted, and heard her come out behind him and halt at a distance of about six feet. He did not turn. It was easier, speaking to the pond.

The words were there, as they had been for twelve years, at the back of his tongue. He had never spoken them. He took a breath that went all the way down.

"Twelve years ago," said Toomas, "I redrew a river."
