# The Keeper Comes to Dinner

She had not aged. That was the first thing, and it was a stupid thing to think, and Toomas thought it anyway, at the reeve's window, with his hand flat on the cold glass.

The twelve riders had left their horses at the carters' stables on the south lip, where the rope bridges began, and Kaie Tammaru came across the Bell Bridge on foot. She walked it as she walked the corridors of the Guild House, at a small unhurried pace with her head up, and the bridge bore her weight as though she were an idea. She was under five feet. The braid hung to her waist, and it had been the colour of a wet fox when she held the lamp for him twelve years ago and was silver now, a rope of it, catching the lantern-light at every step. Her gloves were white. They had pearl buttons at the wrist. A rider walked on either side of her, and a pace behind, mopping his scalp with a handkerchief and carrying his tall black hat as a man carries a sick bird, came Mihkel Org.

Palu had stopped. The drummers on the barrel had run out of rhythm in the middle of a bar. Children who had been racing along the rails of the upper bridges had come down off them and stood in a row with their ribbons trailing, and at the end of the span the bell-rope hung in Jaan Pold's two hands without moving, as though he had forgotten what it was for.

"Mm," said Toomas, to the glass. "She has brought her own weather."

"Is that the Keeper?" said Liisu, at his elbow. She had come to stand beside him on the box she used for looking out of high windows. "She's small."

"She is."

"I thought she'd be bigger. For all the notices."

"So did I," said Toomas, "for a good many years."

* * *

They met on the step. The reeve's house stood at the north end of the bridge on a platform of black timbers over the gorge, a long low house of seven rooms with a floor that hummed when the bridges swayed, and its door opened onto a landing that was also the end of the span. Jaan had set down the bell-rope and come to it. Toomas, in the doorway behind him, with Reet at his shoulder, heard the Keeper's voice before he saw her face. It was exactly as he remembered it: quiet, complete, with every consonant set down as carefully as a dish.

"Reeve Pold. I am Kaie Tammaru, Keeper of the Atlas. I have ridden eleven hours, and my escort with me. I ask the hospitality of your house." There was the smallest pause, a pause he knew, in which she looked at a thing and chose whether to say it. "And supper, naturally, if there is any to be had."

Palu had a custom about guests. It was older than the Guild, and older than the bridges; it was the sort of law that does not need a book, because it lives in the body, in the hand that offers bread. Toomas saw it take hold of his nephew. He saw the heavy shoulders try to lift in refusal and be held down by something heavier. A man cannot turn from his door a stranger who has asked for salt. Not on the Name-Day. Not with the whole of his town looking on.

"Sit," said Jaan Pold. "Eat."

"You are very kind."

"It's stew."

"Naturally," said Kaie.

She came in. She passed Toomas without turning her head; her eyes went over him as over a doorpost, and came to rest, with the faintest flicker, on Reet. "Surveyor Sallik. You have been missed at the Day-book." Then, to Mihkel, who had halted on the threshold with his hat against his chest and the expression of a man who would give a year's pay to be in the wrong building: "Master Org. You will see to the horses and to the men, and then you will join us."

"Keeper," said Mihkel. He looked at Toomas. "Veskimae."

"Org. I understand you have mislaid a mountain."

"I have a letter from the Guild's insurers," said Mihkel, with bitterness, "on the subject of one mountain." He went out.

* * *

The reeve's table was a long one of scrubbed pine, with a bench down either side, and there were seven at it: Jaan at the head, the Keeper at his right hand, then Toomas, then Reet; across from them Liisu and two of the riders, who sat very straight and ate as if they were being timed. The stew was grayling, with dill and cream and small yellow potatoes, and it came in wooden bowls. Beyond the window the gorge went blue with evening. Under the floor the bridges moved, a long slow breath, in and out.

Kaie ate in her gloves.

Toomas watched it with a feeling of unreality. She broke bread with white kidskin fingers, and a pearl button at her wrist dipped once in the cream, and she wiped it, unhurried, with the corner of her napkin. At the far end of the table Liisu had stopped eating altogether.

"Why are you wearing gloves at supper?" said Liisu.

"Liisu," said Jaan.

"It is a reasonable question, Reeve." The Keeper turned her pale brown eyes on the child with perfect courtesy. "It is a habit of the vault, Miss Pold. Rule Nineteen requires two gloves on vellum, and in twenty years I have found that I forget to take them off."

"There isn't any vellum."

"Naturally not."

"Then you can take them off."

"I could," agreed Kaie, "and I shall, when I have finished." She smiled at her. It was a good smile; it was the thing that had made the Assembly vote for her. "That is a very fine map of yours, I think, upon the bridge. I saw it as I crossed. The mulberry is exactly where it should be."

Liisu looked at her, and then at her bowl. It was the first time Toomas had ever seen her unable to say a thing.

"Reeve," said Kaie, when the bowls were empty. "May I put some figures before you?"

"Say them."

She drew a folded sheet from the breast of her coat and opened it on the table among the crumbs. It was ruled in columns, in a clerk's neat hand, and as she laid it flat he saw Reet's whole body go still beside him, the stillness of a hound that has seen the hare.

"The Seam widens one mile in the year. I say it as one says that the river runs downhill, because it is the same kind of fact." The gloved finger moved down the paper. "The Hollow's rim lies thirty-one miles from the Seam's northern edge on the line of the Cold Stream. That is thirty-one years. In the year three hundred and three, when I was given the keys, it was eleven. If I had done nothing, the edge would be at the Guild stair in three." She did not raise her voice. "Forty thousand people live in the Hollow, Reeve. They cannot be moved in a season, or in ten. They can be moved in forty years, with the Assembly's help, if the Assembly can be made to believe in the necessity. The Assembly voted against moving your own town, twelve years ago, on a question of cost. You may remember."

"I remember," said Jaan.

"I do not intend to ask them again until I have gained the time to be refused." She turned the sheet a little. "I have gained twenty-eight years, in eight places, on land that was thinly peopled. I moved every household first. I paid for it from the Keeper's own purse, as grain-relief. Not one soul has been harmed. Palu is the ninth place I have had to ask."

The word *ask* sat on the table among the bowls. Toomas saw his nephew look at it.

"On the night of the tenth," said Kaie, "this valley will be a lake. I will not discuss the means. I ask you to believe the date. It is tomorrow night, Reeve. The weight of the Seam settles here, on the throat of the Haldja, where it is thickest, and the water will stand to the lip of the upland, and Palu will be forty feet under it. I do not want your people under it. I have brought riders and forty ropes and a plan of march. I have Orav, with its empty pans and three hundred roofs, which the Hollow has held since the sickness. You would have timber for your bridges. You would take your goats."

"And the dead?"

It was said very quietly. For the first time something moved in the Keeper's face. "That," said Kaie, "I am sorry for. I have not found a way."

"My mother's behind the tower."

"Yes."

"Thirty-one of my family are behind the tower." Jaan's two hands lay flat on the table on either side of his bowl, heavy and still, the bell-rope callus gleaming yellow. "I rang a bell over every one."

"Surveyor Sallik," said Kaie gently, as though he had not spoken. "You have the best head for figures in the Guild. Tell the reeve whether I am wrong."

Reet had not spoken since the door. She had her hands under the table, and her chin up, and Toomas, who sat beside her, felt her draw in a breath and thought: she cannot lie to this woman. She will have to answer it. "The figures are right," said Reet.

"Thank you."

"The sum is wrong."

"Which line?"

"I don't know yet," said Reet.

It was true, and she said it without a hitch, and he saw the Keeper hear that it was true. Something went across the small, pointed, lovely face and was put away.

"No," said Jaan Pold.

"Reeve—"

"No. Not tomorrow. Not Orav. Not forty ropes, not a plan of march, not any of it. Nine hundred of us have stood here since before there was a Guild. You'll not walk us out of it on a paper." He stood. The bench scraped. "Eat what you want. There's a bed. You'll be gone in the morning."

"Naturally you may refuse," said Kaie. She had not moved. She folded the sheet along its creases and put it in her breast. "I will ask again."

* * *

She asked him, later, in the yard.

It was Toomas she asked. She said, "Master Veskimae, will you walk with me?" as if inviting him to cross a courtyard, and he got up, and the reeve looked at the two of them, and said nothing.

The yard was a ledge of bare rock behind the house with a rail along its edge, and below the rail the gorge went down in the dusk to the bootlace of the river. There was a woodpile, and a rain barrel, and half a dozen buckets turned upside down to dry, and from the other side of the chasm the sound of a drum, picking up again, rather uncertainly, as though someone had gone to find it and brought it back. The lantern on its nail made a small gold room in the dark.

"You have grown a moustache," said Kaie.

"I had one when you last saw me."

"It was smaller." She stood at the rail with her gloved hands folded, looking not down but across at the far wall of the gorge. "I did not expect you to come. I expected Sallik, in time. I believed I had given her enough to think about."

"You gave her tea."

"Naturally."

"How many, Kaie?"

She did not pretend. It was one of the things he had admired, once, in the thin brown clerk who kept the inventory and held the lamp: she did not pretend. "Eight places, on fourteen leaves. Palu is the ninth. The first was Tallow Moor, in the autumn of three-oh-three, a heath with a farm on it, four souls, who are in Orav now, with a cow. Two hundred and eleven in all. Nobody has died."

"Nobody has died," said Toomas, tasting it. "Mm. And I?"

"Eleven." She said it at once. "I know the claim file by heart. I have read it more often than the Rules."

"Where did you find the notebook?"

"In the false drawer, behind the lamp-oil receipts. Two years after you were dismissed. I was thirty-eight." She spoke to the far wall of the gorge. "I read it on the vault stair, with a candle, in one night. In the morning I went down with a vial and a leaf and proved you right on a heath near Tallow Moor. I held the lamp, you will remember, the night you drew the river. I watched your hand. It did not shake. I thought: that is a man who knows precisely what he is doing." She turned her head. "I did not learn until I read the notebook that you had no idea."

"No."

"The notebook began it. I would not take that from you."

"You did not ask me."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because you would have said no," said Kaie.

"Yes," said Toomas. "I would."

"And the Hollow would have gone on dying politely, in the interests of consultation." She said it without heat, as she said everything. "I am forty-eight, Master Veskimae. I have been careful. I move the people first. I draw nothing on a leaf that I have not read twice, and I turn the page." She let that rest. "You did not. That is the difference between us."

"Is it?" He looked down at the river. "It costs you, I suppose. The drawing."

"Less than it cost you. I draw from the leaf and not the ground. But it is not nothing." The pale eyes did not leave the cliff. "I could not tell you tonight where Sora Crossing lies, though I have its population to the head. Tallow Moor I know as one knows the moon."

"Then you cannot see them."

"I do not need to see them. I need to count them."

He was silent for some time. The drum across the gorge had found its pattern again. "And the water?" said Toomas at last. "It is borrowed. I used a vial of Palu's, myself, and never asked whose. Every vial on the shelf was filled by a rider who stood in the place and said so. They did not know they were lending."

"Naturally they would have objected."

"Mm. As would I."

There was the small sound of a footstep on rock. Toomas turned his head.

Mihkel Org stood at the corner of the house with a lantern in one hand and his hat in the other. He had come, plainly, to report on the horses; he had, in the tradition of his office, a sheet of paper in his coat and a Rule on his tongue. He had been standing there for some time. In the lamplight his face had gone the colour of tallow, and the walnut fringe stood up at the back like a cock's comb. "Keeper," he said. "The riders—"

He stopped.

"Master Org," said Kaie. "You were not meant to hear it like this."

"The ninth," said Mihkel.

"Mihkel."

"I audited the Correction Book. I audited it every quarter for nineteen years. Re-inking, I said. It is a matter of maintenance." His voice had gone up, like that of a man trying to speak while walking on a plank. "The notebook. Veskimae's notebook. You said, a moment ago, the notebook began it. And Soo. You said eleven."

"How was it you wished to hear it?" said Kaie.

"I should have wished to hear it in some other way. I should have wished to be *told*."

"Gently," said Kaie. "In the Hollow. In due course."

Mihkel Org opened his mouth. Toomas saw it, and was to remember it for the rest of his life: the round pink face, the lantern shaking, the whole pillar of the man drawing itself up for the one resource he had never lacked. He opened his mouth for a Rule. And for the first time in thirty years there was not one.

He sat down. He sat on an upturned bucket, in the exact middle of the yard, with the lantern on his knee, and the bucket gave a small, damp, fishy sound, as of old herring-heads settling. He did not seem to notice.

"It was very tidy," said Mihkel, to nobody. "The Book. It was a very tidy Book."

* * *

They went in. Kaie asked the reeve for pen and paper, and a candle, and was given them; she sat down at the head of the pine table and drew the sheet toward her, and wrote at the top of it in a hand as small and clean as a row of seeds. Toomas stood by the window. Reet stood at the foot of the table with her back straight and her hands at her sides. The reeve had not sat down again. Liisu was in the corner on the box, perfectly motionless, with her thumbnail between her teeth.

"Master Org," said Kaie, without lifting her head. "You will take Surveyor Sallik and Master Veskimae into custody. The Ilves boy also, and the guard Rebane. They will be held in the grain store, and watched, and treated with every courtesy, until the night of the tenth is over."

Mihkel stood in the doorway. He had put on his hat, as a man puts on a coat in a house where he has not been asked to stay. "Under what Rule, Keeper?"

"Rule Forty."

"Rule Forty," said Mihkel. His voice was very flat. "No Surveyor sleeps unwatched in a shifting place."

"For their protection. Naturally."

"Naturally," said Mihkel. A long breath went out of him. "Rule Sixty-two requires that an order of custody be signed in the hand of the issuing officer. Bare."

It was the habit of thirty years. It rose in him without his leave, like a hiccup. He heard himself say it, and his round face did something terrible, and Toomas, who knew what it was to be caught by one's own reflexes at an hour of disaster, felt for him something so close to love that he had to look away.

"Bare," said Kaie.

"I beg your pardon. It is in the Rules."

"I know that it is." She laid the pen down. For a moment she did nothing. She looked at her own hands on the paper, the white gloves folded over each other, and Toomas saw her decide. She took hold of the pearl button at the wrist of the left glove, and undid it. She drew the glove off, finger by finger, slowly, as one draws a sheet off a body. She did the same with the right.

Nobody in the room breathed.

The hands beneath were small and fine-boned and stained to the wrist. It was not the pale grey-blue of Ilves Black, which comes off in a week's soap. It was the dark one, the true one, blue-black with a gloss on it like a beetle's wing, soaked into the skin to the depth of the pores and stopped at the wrist-bone in a line as sharp as a cuff. It had been laid down in layers over years. It was a colour Toomas knew from the inside. His own hands had been that colour once, before twelve years of Tinta Row and a great deal of lye.

In nineteen years Mihkel Org had never seen the Keeper's hands. Rule Nineteen. He had thought it was modesty.

Kaie took up the pen and wrote her name.

From the yard, as she signed, came a crash, a bellow, the unmistakable clang of a salt-hook meeting a Guild shield, a stream of oaths in a woman's voice, and the heavy rattle of ox-chain being run through a wheel. Nobody looked round. Reet's eyes had not left the Keeper's hands. Toomas saw her take it in, all of it, in the way she took in a hillside, and set it where she kept such things.

"You've been drawing for years," said Reet.

Kaie Tammaru blew gently on the wet ink, and set down the pen.

"Naturally," she said.
