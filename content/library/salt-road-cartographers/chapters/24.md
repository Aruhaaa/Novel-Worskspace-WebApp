# Ask the Ground

> PRIVY: OUT THE DOOR, LEFT, TEN STEPS. NOT THE GOAT SHED.
>
> YOU ARE REET. YOU ARE IN THE SCHOOL. YOU ARE ALL RIGHT.
>
> BREAKFAST IS WHERE THE SMELL IS. — A.

The note was pinned to the wall above the washstand, in blocky capitals, with a tent-peg for a pin, and Reet read it every morning. She had stopped being embarrassed in the second week. It was a good note. It was better than the one before, which had said only *WELCOME BACK, YOU STUPID GIRL* and had required her, on three consecutive mornings, to be told what she was welcome back to.

She lay a little longer under the quilt, looking at the roof. It was larch, new, with the resin still sweating from the rafters in the sun, and the whole room smelled of it. Beyond the window a wedge of morning was coming over a lake. She knew it was a lake because she had been told. She did not know why it was so bright.

She got up. She found the privy by counting ten steps, which she could still do. It was only the steps she could not keep.

* * *

"That's wrong," said Liisu. "The bend's bigger."

"Is it?" said the baker.

They stood in a doorway in the lane under the school, in a smell of rye and woodsmoke and hot stone, and the baker, whose name was Hindrek Salu and who was built like one of his own loaves, looked down at the page Reet held out to him, at the pencil line that was to be the lane's first yard. He took the pencil in a floury fist. He had already drawn it once, in flour, on his counter, with his finger, to show her. Now he made it again, a little rounder.

"There. Left at the oven and it swings out as you go up, like a hip. Twenty steps to the cooperage. You'll smell the oak."

"Thank you," said Reet. She wrote it down: *Salu, left at oven, swings out like a hip*.

She had a new notebook, which Toomas had bound for her before he left, in grey cloth, with a tea-ring on the cover that he swore was a maker's mark. On the flyleaf, in his slow upright hand, was a question and nothing else: *What is here?* She had read it a good many times. She had thought it was a rebuke, and then a kindness, and she had settled, this week, on the opinion that it was an instruction.

She did not remember yesterday's lane. She could remember the baker's face, because it was a face; she could remember that he had told her yesterday, and the day before, that it was left at the oven. She could not remember the bend. The ground went out of her every night like water out of a basin and left her the people. So she did what she had worked out in the first week, in a state of fury and some tears, with Liisu watching her with a flat, interested stare. She asked.

The cooper was next, in her yard, among the staves. Epp Mänd drew the slope of the lane with her compass, in three swift curves, the way she drew the belly of a barrel. "Down to the well, then up to the school. Mind the root of the pear." And the woman who was hanging out washing by the pear tree, Mistress Lauri, with her mouth full of pegs, took the pencil from Reet's hand and drew a line from her gatepost to the pear tree, level, and under it another, slanting. "Level's the washing," she said, round the pegs. "Slope's the lane. Eight inches of fall, gatepost to pear. The washing doesn't lie."

Reet looked at the page. The lane lay on it in four hands, the baker's rounded and the cooper's curved, and the washing-woman's as plain as a line of sheets, and it was a very good lane. It would never have fitted in one head. She was beginning to think that was the point of it.

"It's right," said Liisu, behind her. She had her own map under her arm, rolled and tied with a ribbon, which she took everywhere like a sword. "I checked. It's all right."

"Thank you," said Reet.

"You don't have to say thank you to *me*."

"I'd like to."

Liisu looked at her feet. "All right," she said.

* * *

In the lower square, under the mulberry, Taavi Ilves had a stall.

It was a trestle and a striped awning with a sign over it in Liisu's lettering, which leaned to the right: ILVES & SON. HONEST INK. IT SAYS ON THE LABEL WHAT IT DOES AND WHAT IT COSTS. The mulberry had arrived with the town, last, a little crooked, with a sparrow's nest in the fork and the sparrow in it, complaining; it stood over the new well, which had come with it, and which Liisu had pronounced to be a good well, and the Lauris and the Mänds were already at law over the fruit.

"It's technically two pennies," Taavi was saying, to a farmer's wife in a headscarf. He had a vial in each hand. He was holding them out like a man offering a pair of shoes that he is certain will not fit. "For the black. That's Ilves Black, it's day-ink, it only keeps a yard for a night, you'll want to know that. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry in advance. The other one's the Founding, and I'd honestly rather you didn't buy it if you haven't stood anywhere first, because it, well, it takes. I mean it costs. It says on the label. It costs what you draw. I'm so sorry."

"What do I draw?" said the farmer's wife.

"Whatever you stood in. And it'll take it off you." He swallowed. "That's the, that's the honest part."

The farmer's wife bought the two-penny vial and said she'd think about the other. She went off with it down the lane to the well, saying over her shoulder that he was a very nice boy and ought to be less sorry. Taavi stood and watched her go. He turned, then, and saw Reet, and the whole face went off like a lamp.

"Reet! Look at this." He pulled a piece of paper from under the trestle. "A standing order. From the Hollow. Forty quarts, monthly, paid on delivery, from the new school of the Oath. Signed K. Tammaru. In her own hand." He held it up with both thumbs. "I've framed it. Technically I shouldn't have. She's paying in full."

"Naturally," said Reet.

"Oh, don't." He was laughing. His ears had gone red. He folded the paper very carefully and put it back, as if it were a letter from a lover, and said in the voice of a person coming to the point after a long detour, "I've been meaning to say. I've stopped apologising to my mother. I wrote and said I wasn't sorry I'd taken the funnel. She wrote back and said I was a thief, and enclosed forty pounds of clove. I think it's love."

"I think so too," said Reet.

* * *

At noon Aino took the first Palu caravan out on the south road.

There were six wagons, loaded with plums in brine and bridge-rope and cheeses the size of cartwheels and, in a locked chest on the second wagon, a hundred vials of honest ink for the villages that had asked for them. The oxen were in their yokes in a long line down the lane, steaming. In the second wagon, sitting very straight with his hands clasped round the reins as round a hot coal, was a thin man in a new coat who had promised his sister he would not touch cards and was keeping the promise with the expression of someone carrying water uphill in a sieve. She had given him a whip. He held it like a candle.

"Rasmus," said Aino, from the lead, in passing. "Eyes front. If I see a card I'll feed you to the Treasurer."

The lead ox was a huge dun creature with a pale belly and an air of grievance. A new name had been burned into the yoke with a hot iron: BAKER. Reet read it and looked at Aino.

"You said you would never name an ox for a person you respected."

"I swore that to a pigeon." Aino did not look round. She was fixing the strap of her salt-hook to her back. "It isn't binding. He's named for Hindrek Salu. He stands in doorways and sulks and has opinions about the weather. I respect him *extremely*." She reached down from the box and pulled Reet's cropped hair, once, hard, as she had on a night in the Seam, and which now meant something else. "And you, shortarse, you eat. Don't let the child run you off your legs. I've left a note."

"I know," said Reet. "It's on the wall."

"There's a new one in the pantry. It says where the cheese is."

"Thank you."

"Don't." The big woman's voice thickened. She cracked the whip over the dun ox's ear with great violence. "Go on, you great sulking *Baker*! Hup!"

The caravan went off down the lane in a long clanking rumble, and over the plank bridge at the foot of the Rise, which was forty feet long and four feet off the water, and the first of the twelve. Aino went over it with her eyes shut, singing the names of oxen at the top of her voice. This time nobody had to count.

* * *

The letter came in the afternoon, by the carrier from Soo. She read it on the school step in the sun, with the lake in front of her and Liisu on the step below, pretending to look at a beetle.

> Soo-on-the-Slope, the second week of the thaw.
>
> Surveyor,
>
> I have mapped sixty wells. I had been told there were forty. Soo, which I took for a small place, turns out to have been dug by people who disagreed with one another, and every disagreement has a well in it. Do you know that Ene Ots and her neighbour have not spoken since the year 288, and have between them three wells within nine paces of each other? I measured them. I was not thanked.
>
> The moustache is well. A child of four mistook it on Tuesday for a goat, and was most put out to be corrected.
>
> Ene Ots has begun to correct my spelling. She does it in red thread, laid across the page, which she says is cheaper than ink and makes the point. She has found three errors in the word *Soo*. I find that I look forward to the next.
>
> You will not remember the pond. I should like you to know that it is still there, and so are they. I go and stand by it in the evenings. The knee does not like the slope. I do it regardless.
>
> You will want me to say it shorter. Very well. Sixty wells; red thread; I am not forgiven, and I am fed. Come at midsummer.
>
> T. V.

She read it twice, and then a third time, aloud, for Liisu, who did not look up from the beetle but whose shoulders moved.

She did not remember Soo. She had been there; she had read the eleventh name aloud and lost a pond for it; she had a notion of a kitchen smelling of lye. She had not the first idea what the pond looked like. It was a strange thing to be told, by a man who loved her, that a place she had lost was still where it had been. It was like being given a coat that one had worn once and could not remember, and finding that it fitted.

She folded the letter and put it in the notebook, at the flyleaf, against the question.

* * *

The girl came up the cart-road a little after four.

She was fifteen or so, thin and brown, in a boy's coat with the sleeves rolled, carrying a flour sack and a rolled map done in charcoal on the back of a sugar-bag, and she walked as if the road were rather hot. She stopped at the foot of the school steps. She looked at Reet. Reet looked at her. She was a good deal smaller than the girl had expected, plainly; the girl hid it quite well.

"I'm looking for the one who draws towns," said the girl.

"There's no such one," said Reet. "There's a lot of us."

"They said in the Hollow. They said she was at Palu. They said she'd do it, if I asked. My name's Kadri Aav. I'm from Kask Ford. We lost our ford, the year three hundred and five. It moved a quarter-mile and nobody ever said why, and our well went with it." She swallowed. She had the air of someone who has rehearsed a good deal on the road and is now forced to speak the abridged edition. "The Speaker says we can keep our own atlas now. Under the oath. We've no one who knows how."

"I don't know how," said Reet.

It came out before she could stop it. She had a dreadful moment, in which she heard how it sounded, and Kadri Aav's face did not fall, but stood quite still, like a pot on a shelf. Reet found that her hands were cold. She was nineteen. She had a notebook with a tea-ring on it and a map made of other people's yards, and she had never taught anyone anything, except a lamp-boy once how to count in links.

"I mean," she said. "I know one thing. I don't know if it's a lesson."

"What?"

"You go and stand somewhere. And you ask somebody who lives there what's there. And you write down what they tell you. And you say that you stood, and you only drew what you saw, and it's true, so it takes. And you find out what it costs." She took a breath. She had no idea if this was teaching. "That's all I've got. It isn't much. You can come and ask with us, if you want. We've a room. It smells of larch."

Kadri Aav thought about it with her lips pressed together.

"I thought you'd be taller," she said.

"Everybody does."

"It's all right. Neither am I." And, unexpectedly, she smiled; and Reet understood that she had been terrified, too, the whole of the road.

* * *

It was Liisu who took them out at dusk, the two of them, along the lane and past the cooperage and the washing and the school, to the edge of the new town, where the last stake stood. Beyond it the land fell away east in the evening light. A field lay below them, long and sloping, going silver at the edges as the sun went, and at the far end of it a boy was sitting on a wall, eating an apple.

The air was very still. Somewhere below them water was running. Kadri Aav stood a little way behind with her pencil out, being careful not to look as if she were learning.

Liisu stopped at the stake. She stood with her hands at her sides and her chin up and her map under her arm, in the grey smock with its ribbons, in the same stance in which she had once corrected a Dawn-Surveyor on the subject of a mulberry. She did not look at Reet. She pointed across the hedge at the long field, with a plum-coloured thumbnail.

"What's here?" said Liisu.

Reet looked. It was a field. She had the idea that it was a good one: she knew that she ought to feel something about it, a kind of tug, as a tongue goes to a place where a tooth has been. She could not have said what she would have felt. There was a hedge. There was a post. She stood for a time with her hands in her pockets, in the cold, and thought of a girl who had counted bells since she was four, and who would have known it to the yard.

She had said *I don't know* so many times that month that she had grown fond of it. It had stopped being a thing that happened to her. It had become, she thought, a sort of door.

"Tell me," said Reet.

And Liisu began. "There's a thorn hedge on the north side," she said, in her flat clear voice, as if she were reading it off the air, "and a ditch behind it, with water in. The gatepost's split oak, with an iron ring that Ruus's father hung. And there's a stile of two flat stones."
