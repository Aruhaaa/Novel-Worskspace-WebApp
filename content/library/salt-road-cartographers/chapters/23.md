# The Hearing in the Hollow

The Hollow had shrunk.

Toomas noticed it on the second landing of the Guild stair, with his hand on the arm of a lamp-boy and his knee filing a formal complaint. He had been out of this city for a month and out of its heart for twelve years, and in all that time he had carried it about with him as a thing the size of a cathedral: forty thousand people in a limestone bowl, tier on tier, lit from below like a lantern. He found that it was only a town. It was a town in a hole, with frost on its steps and its lamps still burning at ten in the morning because the sinkhole held the dark like a cup. A woman was shaking a rug from a window. A cat was asleep on a coil of rope. The oak-gall smell, which he had thought a smell of power, was simply the smell of ink.

"Four hundred and nineteen," said the lamp-boy. "You're on two hundred and twelve. Don't look down, sir, it only makes it longer."

The lamp-boy was about eleven, with a brass lamp-hook over his shoulder and a quick pointed face, and Toomas had had his name from the porter at the foot. It was Ants. He had been told about Ants by a girl who kept her friends in a list, in the white quiet of the Blank; she had put him first, ahead of her mother and Mihkel's pastry crusts.

"Is she coming?" said Ants. "Reet?"

"This morning. With the ink-boy."

"Does she know me?"

"She knows your face," said Toomas. "She cannot tell you which stair you stood on."

Ants thought about this with the seriousness of the very young. "Good," he said at last. "I'm memorable. I've drawn her arrows." And he showed Toomas the chalk on the wall at the turn of the stair: a small fat yellow arrow, pointing up, and above it another, and above that another, one on every landing from the Wharf Gate to the Assembly's door.

* * *

The Assembly Hall had been cut out of the living limestone at the foot of the Guild House, in a horseshoe, with a dais for the Speaker at the open end and wooden benches rising in tiers from the floor. Behind the dais a short passage ran back into the rock to a door of black iron with three locks in it. The gallery above the benches had been given up that morning to those who had been wronged.

Toomas looked along it from the floor and saw them. He saw Palu: Jaan Pold in a coat that had been made for a smaller man, with a bell-rope's callus on his hands; Ruus, who had brought his hat; Liisu, scrubbed, in the grey smock with its ribbons, sitting with her back as straight as a rule and a large map under her arm. He saw Soo, a dozen of them in stone-grey wool, with Ene Ots in the middle on two sticks like a small ship at anchor, and she did not look at him. He saw the Ford: Veikko Pent, in a coat that had once been brown, on the end of a bench, humming, and tethered to the leg of the bench by a length of twine a goat called Rule, which was eating the Assembly's bunting with deliberation. Behind them, in their eight different kinds of coat, sat the eight places whose ground a woman in this room had taken apart and put together again: Tallow Moor, Kask Ford, Sora Crossing, and five more. Toomas saw a woman from Sora Crossing with Reet's chin. He saw Salme Ilves, in the end seat, with a thin cigar between her fingers, unlit, and a face like a closed ledger.

The Speaker was an old woman, Urve Kann, with a face like a walnut and a gavel she did not appear to know the use of. She sat in the middle of the dais with her hands on the arms of her chair. In front of her, on a green cloth, lay three keys.

"The first is the Master-Surveyor's," said Urve Kann. "The second is the Assembly's own, which has not left the strong-room in my time. The third is the Keeper's, which she surrendered on her return. It is the wish of the Assembly that the vault be opened before this Hall, by all three together, and that the Correction Book be read aloud." She looked down. "Master Org."

Mihkel Org rose from the front bench. He had put on the mustard waistcoat. He had combed the walnut fringe flat and brought his tall black hat, and he held it before him like a man carrying a lit candle through wind. His hands were not shaking. Toomas, who had watched them shake for three weeks, thought that it was the most alarming thing he had ever seen.

He went down the passage with the three keys. The Hall followed, in a body, the Speaker on a stick, the gallery in single file, so that the corridor behind the dais became a procession in the lamplight. At the iron door he stopped. There was a row of brass pegs beside it where for three hundred years the Surveyors had hung their cotton gloves, according to Rule Nineteen, and they hung there still, a hundred grey pairs, like a row of hands waiting to be asked. He turned two keys, and then the third, and left all three standing in their locks.

The door swung. A smell came out of it that Toomas knew in his teeth: oak gall and wet stone and old vellum, the smell of a room that has been kept. Beyond the door the shelves went back into the dark, and he saw, small and exact in the lamplight on the middle shelf, a creamy foxed leaf with a square on it and a dot. Leaf Two-oh-six. He found that he could look at it without his hands shaking.

They brought the Correction Book out on a trolley. It was a long ledger bound in black, and Mihkel set it on the Speaker's lectern in the Hall, opened it at the page he had marked with a strip of pastry paper, and read.

"'Autumn, the year three hundred and three. Leaf Fifty-six, Tallow Moor. Re-inked. Tidy.'"

He read it in a steady voice. It was the voice he used for the Rules, a dry clerkly voice with every letter set down, and it did not change from the first line to the last. He read the second. "'Leaf Seventy-one, Kask Ford. Re-inked. Tidy.'" From the gallery a man stood up, an old man in a farmer's coat, and stayed standing. "'Leaf Eighty-eight, Sora Crossing. Re-inked.'" The woman with Reet's chin got up. It went on. Mihkel read for an hour and ten minutes. He read fourteen leaves in eight places, and the people of each place stood in turn as their name was said and did not sit down, until the gallery was a row of the standing, silent, all in different coats. And the word at the end of the line was always the same, and Mihkel said it each time with exactly the same level voice. He said *tidy* nine times. He said it to the end.

"I initialled this," he said, when he had finished. "Every one. For nineteen years. It was a very tidy Book."

He closed it.

* * *

The Keeper walked in alone by the side door.

She had been confined to her rooms since her return, on the Speaker's order, and no one had seen her. She wore a plain grey dress and carried nothing. She walked down the length of the Hall in the silence with her head up and her silver braid down her back, a woman under five feet, and stopped at the lectern. She stood a moment looking at the Speaker. Then she took off her gloves.

She did it one finger at a time. She laid them on the green cloth where the keys had been, a small white pair with pearl buttons. And the Hall saw her hands for the first time: blue-black to the wrist, glossy, years deep, as if she had dipped them in a well.

"I shall give the figures," said Kaie.

She gave them. She spoke without a note, in her quiet complete voice, and Toomas heard in it every word he had once taught her. The Seam widened one mile in the year. In the year three hundred and three, the rim of this city had stood eleven years from it. It stood thirty-one now, by her count, and that was eight places, which were in the Book that had been read. She had moved two hundred and eleven people first, at her own expense. None had died. She had done it in her own hand, from the leaf, in an ink she had bought from a woman who understood what it was for. The ninth place had been Palu.

"I do not ask the Assembly to find the figures wrong," said Kaie. "They are right. I ask it to find that I was wrong, which is not the same, and which I did not know was possible until I sat for six hours on a milking stool and watched nine hundred people tell a hillside what they meant. Naturally I should like to say that I have regrets. I find I do not have the right word. I have the figures. That is all I have brought."

There was no sound in the Hall. The Speaker, who had not touched her gavel, looked down from the dais over her spectacles. "Does the Assembly have questions?"

Nobody had. A child from Palu, said the Speaker, had asked leave.

Liisu Pold stood on the gallery bench with her map. She did not appear to be in awe. "Why didn't you ask us?"

Toomas, on the floor, felt the Hall tilt toward the answer. It was a question he had put to himself, in various forms, for twelve years.

"Because I knew what you would say," said Kaie, "and I did not wish to hear it. I told myself that this was kindness. It was something a good deal smaller."

"That's not a good reason."

"No. It is not."

Liisu considered this. "If we'd said no," she said, "would you have done it anyway?"

A pause. In it Toomas saw a small hand, bare, closing on the edge of the lectern.

"Yes," said Kaie.

A sound went through the gallery. It was not loud. "I should have asked again," she said, "and again, until you were tired. And then I should have drawn it."

"That's not asking."

"No," said Kaie. "I have only lately learned the difference."

"Okay," said Liisu. She sat down.

He had been ready for tears, or a lie, or a long speech. He had not been ready for *yes*. It was the answer that he would have given himself, at her age, in that vault, with a lamp held for him, to the same question about the same river. He found that his throat had closed. He looked at the stone floor of the Hall until it went away.

* * *

They did not take long, after that.

The motion was put in plain words by the Speaker, from a paper in her own hand. That any town, village or hamlet of Orrin whose people had stood upon their ground might keep its own atlas under the Oath of Standing. That the leaves of the Hollow be opened to all comers, and the Correction Book kept in public, and read each year at midwinter. That nothing in the Founding of Hilja Orm, who had carved her oath on a stone in the middle of the Blank for anyone to read, set up any Guild whatsoever. "The monopoly is a hundred and ninety years old," said Urve Kann. "It was invented by her heirs. I cannot find that she ever asked for it."

She called for hands. Toomas did not count them. They were not close.

"One more thing," said the Speaker. "The Hollow has forty thousand people and a rim thirty-one years off. I am told that ground which is held does not move. The Assembly will therefore hold it, ward by ward and tier by tier, beginning with this stair, and it will take us the winter and a great deal of ink." Her eyes went to the small figure at the lectern. "Kaie Tammaru. The Assembly does not imprison you. The Assembly has found that it has a use for you. You will teach the oath. You will teach it from a table in the open air, to anyone who comes, with your hands bare, so that those who learn may see what it costs. The salary is twelve marks a year and a room over the tannery."

Nobody laughed. It was not a joke. It was, he thought, much the hardest sentence he had ever heard handed down, and also the kindest, and the Speaker had known precisely what she was doing.

"Naturally," said Kaie. She did not look at her hands.

"There is a further matter," said the Speaker. "Toomas Veskimae."

He stood. The knee had something to say about it. "The Assembly has no standing in a flood twelve years old," said Urve Kann, not unkindly, "and does not mean to assume any. But Soo has asked to be heard."

Ene Ots got to her feet. It took her some time, on the two sticks, with a young woman at her elbow whom she brushed off; and when she was up she was a very small, very upright old woman in grey wool, with a face like a winter apple, and her voice carried to the roof.

"Soo has met," she said. "I'm to say what Soo says. We don't forgive him. That isn't ours to do. It's for eleven people who aren't here." She looked at him then, directly, for the first time since the kitchen. "We've decided what he's for. He'll come and live in the hut at the mill, the one with the tick scratched on the lintel, so he can look at it. He'll map our wells, every well, to the foot, and when he's done he'll begin again, because wells move. He'll say the eleven on the day in the yard, standing, and say them right. He'll write it in a fair hand. I can't read a scrawl. And he'll eat at my table once a year, and I won't talk to him."

"I accept," said Toomas.

"I wasn't finished." The old woman drew herself up. "When he's dead he's to be buried below the stones, on the slope, not in the churchyard. So that they can see him."

"I accept," said Toomas, again.

"Good. Sit down," said Ene Ots. "Your knee's shaking."

* * *

It was Taavi who found the Hall for her.

She had not been able to, on her own. She had walked up the Guild stair behind a yellow chalk arrow on every landing, and had still turned left at the fountain, and he had gone after her with a fruit-seller's basket in his arms, apologising. She came in a quarter-hour late with the ink-boy's hand under her elbow, her cropped hair on end and her left hand black. The Hall turned. She stopped in the doorway, small and grey-eyed, with the notch through her eyebrow, and looked about her at the benches, the faces and the lamps, with polite interest, as at a play she had been told about.

"The Assembly would be glad of a word from the Surveyor who drew Palu," said Urve Kann. "Surveyor Sallik, one question, for the record. Do you remember the vault?"

Reet considered it, in earnest, with her head on one side.

"I don't know," she said.

The Hall laughed.

It was a good laugh. It rose from the benches and the gallery and the tiers, enormous, warm, a laugh that had been waiting three hours to be let out, and it went up to the roof and came down again as something much easier. Veikko Pent slapped his knee. The goat Rule looked up from the bunting. And Reet, who had not intended to make anyone laugh, looked startled, and then smiled slowly, as at a gift.

"I mean it," she said. "I've been told I was in it. I'm told I measured a pinhole. I'd have to go and see if it recognises me."

"It has your initials in the pinhole log," said Mihkel Org, from the front bench, gravely.

The Hall roared.

When it had subsided, the Master-Surveyor got to his feet again. He walked across to the great tiled stove in the corner and, from the pocket of the mustard waistcoat, took out a paper folded in four.

"A note of hand," he said, "for three hundred and forty silver marks, drawn upon one Rasmus Rebane, and purchased by the Surveyor's Office from a house on Lamp Street, on the nineteenth of last month, under Rule Ninety-one: Contingent Expenses. The Office has reviewed it. The Office has no further interest in the debt." He opened the stove door. He put the paper in, and watched it a moment, and shut the door again, with a click.

In the gallery something large had gone very still. Aino Rebane sat with her salt-hook across her knees and a thin man in a borrowed coat beside her, whose hand she was holding as one holds a rope. She did not speak. She was crying, silently, with her chipped tooth showing. Then she lifted her face to the Hall, and said in a ringing voice that reached the back wall:

"I'll have to rename the ox."

"Madam?" said Mihkel.

"There's an ox called Mihkel." She wiped her face on the red scarf. "I can't have an ox called Mihkel now. It isn't respectful. I'll call him something very rude about a Treasurer."

The Hall rose again like a flock off a field.

Toomas did not laugh. He stood with his hand on the back of the bench and looked at the front of the Hall, where the green cloth lay under the lamps with one pair of small white gloves on it. The Speaker had risen, and the Hall was emptying toward the passage and the open vault, in a long, chattering, unsteady line; and no one was looking at the cloth.

He went to it. His knee made a remark and was overruled. He picked up the gloves, and they were very light, lighter than he had expected, and they smelled of kidskin and salt tea and the inside of a cupboard. He turned them over. Then he carried them down the passage, against the current, to the iron door and the row of brass pegs beside it, where the hundred grey cotton pairs hung as they had always hung.

He hung them up at the end, small and white, with their pearl buttons done. They swung a little, and were still.

"For whoever needs them next," said Toomas, to nobody.
