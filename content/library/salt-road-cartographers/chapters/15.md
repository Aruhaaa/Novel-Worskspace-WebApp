# Palu Under Its Bridges

They heard the drums a mile before they saw the bridges, which Toomas took for a kindness, since it gave a man time to arrange his face.

They had left the Blank at first light and found the Seam waiting on its southern side with every hill in a new place, as if it had been rearranged for their arrival and then thought better of the effort. It had been a long day of detours. Aino had driven it with her jaw set, and the two oxen had gone through the hours in a state of injured silence, and toward dusk the track had climbed a long scoured shoulder of limestone, and the drums had begun, very small, like a heartbeat in a wall. Then the cart came over the top, and the ground stopped, and there it was.

The gorge opened in the plateau like a wound that had been left to heal itself. It was perhaps two hundred feet deep and three hundred across, and the town clung to both walls of it, on ledges, on stilts, on shelves of timber driven into the rock, house above house in a stair of steep slate roofs lit from within. Between the walls, from every level, ran the bridges. He had known there were forty. He had not remembered what forty looked like. They spanned the gap at every height and angle, rope and plank, some no more than a cable and a handrail, some broad enough for a cart; and from every cable hung ribbons, red and yellow and green and a blue like the sky over water, so many that the whole town seemed to be draped in a web of coloured breath. Lanterns swung from the spans. Under them the evening was a deep violet, and far below, thin and pale, ran a river.

It was a bootlace. He thought of the river at the foot of the road that ended, and then he put the thought away. This river ran in a bed far too wide for it, with a bar of dry stones on either side, and he knew why. Painted on the east wall of the gorge, twenty feet above the water and bright as a fresh cut, was a red line, and it was dated. *299.* It was where the Haldja had stood on the ninth day of the rain. It had never come near it since.

He looked upstream.

He had told Reet of this himself, in the yard at Soo. Still it was a strange thing to do: to turn the head toward a place you know to be there, and to feel the eye slide off it like rain off a waxed coat. There was a gorge. There was surely a gorge. Men had told him there was. But where it bent, and where it fell, and the colour of the rock where the river came white out of the dark, there was nothing in him, not a darkness even, only a smooth cool absence like the place a tooth has been. He stood with his hand on the cart-board and looked at it until Reet came up beside him.

She did not touch his sleeve. She looked where he was looking. "It turns east-north-east at three hundred yards," said Reet, in her counting voice. "There's a fall at four hundred and fifty. White. About thirty feet. The rock's rose-coloured on the left bank and grey on the right, and there's a birch growing out of the cliff just above the lip, sideways. It's been there a long time."

He listened to her. He did not say anything. After a time he heard the fall, a very far, thin hiss that had been under the drums all along, and what he felt was not the return of anything. It was something like being lent a coat.

"Mm," said Toomas. "Say that shorter."

"A fall. White. It's loud."

"Thank you."

"Don't," said Reet, and went to see to the ox.

* * *

The child was sitting on the top rail of the carters' gate with her legs wound round the post, as though the post might be taken from her. She was eleven or so, in a grey smock with ribbons pinned all down the front of it, and she had a gap in her front teeth and a thumbnail the colour of a ripe plum. Across her knees lay a roll of coarse brown paper.

"You're from the Guild," she said.

"I'm a Surveyor," said Reet. "I'm not here for the Guild."

"That's the same."

"It isn't. Not now."

The girl considered that. She looked at Reet's ink-stained hands, and at the plumb-bob on its cord, and unrolled the paper over the top of the gatepost with a flick, so that it hung down the far side like a flag. "This is Palu," she said. "Every well. Every goat. Nobody asked me."

Toomas, who had been prepared for a good many things, had not been prepared for the map. It was drawn in pencil and walnut ink, with a bad pen, by a hand that had never been shown how and had worked it out regardless. It was crowded and crooked and wholly accurate. There were forty bridges, each with a number in its middle, and under each number a tiny drawing of whatever was most important about it: a bakery, a washing-line, a goat in a hat. The bell-tower stood at the square, with the little cross-hatched window turned south. The east ridge was a bare brown hump with a scribble of birches on its flank. The contour ticks, he saw, all pointed uphill.

Reet was reading it with her whole face. She read it as she read everything, rapidly and exactly, and he watched her come to the well in the lower square and put her finger on it.

"Here," she said. "East of the fish-stairs. That's where the Dawn Sheet—"

"That's wrong. The well is by the mulberry."

"The Dawn Sheet has—"

"The Dawn Sheet's old. They moved the well when the Lauris' cow fell in. The mulberry's seven years old. It's here." The plum-coloured thumbnail came down on a small lopsided tree in the corner of the square. "The Lauris' cow was called Marta. She was all right."

Reet was silent. Then she said, in a voice Toomas had never heard from her, and which cost her visibly something to produce, "Thank you."

"For what?"

"The correction."

The child looked at her with deep suspicion. "It's just where the well is," she said. "Are you Uncle Toomas?"

He had not expected it. He saw that he had been standing very straight, with both hands on the staff, like a man waiting to be sentenced, and that he had been looking at the map for some time without seeing it. "I suppose I may be," he said.

"Gran said you'd be taller."

"Your Gran," said Toomas, "was generous. And where is she?"

"Behind the bell-tower. Under the white stone." She said it as one says *in the kitchen*. She was already rolling up the map. "I'm Liisu. Dad's ringing. You'd better come, he doesn't like it when people are late for the toast."

The cart-yard was very quiet for a moment. Reet had turned her head and was looking at him with the exact attention she gave a hard piece of ground. Aino was looking at the sky. Toomas stood where he was, on the bad leg, and found that his hands had stopped doing anything at all, and he looked at the gap-toothed child on the gate, who had her grandmother's way of lifting one eyebrow, and thought: she is eleven. I did not know that she existed.

"Come along," said Liisu, in the voice of one who has said it once already. "He's not going to wait."

* * *

The bell-tower stood at the south end of the great central bridge, at the edge of a small square of beaten earth hung with lanterns. The bridge was the longest in Palu, two hundred and ten feet of rope and oak, broad enough for two carts, and from every handrail and every cable trailed ribbons by the thousand, stirring in the draught of the gorge. The square was full. Somebody had set trestles end to end, and on the trestles were plum dumplings, smoked trout, bread hung with ribbons, a roast goat, bowls of the dark blue plums the valley was famous for, and everywhere there were jugs of the brandy that came from them. Three drummers sat on a barrel and beat out a rhythm that made the planks of the bridge shiver. Children ran along the rails of the bridges above, shrieking, at a height from which a fall would have taken eleven seconds.

Aino stopped at the edge of the square and made a small, well-bred sound in her throat.

"They've never fallen," said Liisu, passing her. "Hardly ever."

The man at the bell-rope was built like a door. He had shoulders on which one might have stood a table, grey at the temples, and a heavy jaw; and when he let go of the rope and flexed his hand, the palm of it was hard and yellow as a horn spoon. He had just rung the toast. The echo of it was still going in the gorge. Nine hundred voices were answering it: *And we on it!* He looked at the newcomers one at a time, without any hospitality, and finally at the old man with the staff.

"Veskimae," said Jaan Pold.

"Jaan." Toomas looked at the jaw. "You have your mother's chin."

"She said you'd come."

"When did she say so?"

"Every winter. Last time, the week before Midwinter. She'd been three days in bed." The reeve's voice did not rise. It was a voice used to the bell, and it did not need to. "She said you'd come when you'd got up your courage and not before, and I wasn't to write, because writing would only make you late. So I didn't write."

"I did not know."

"No."

"When—"

"The eleventh night of the dark month. The ground was iron that week. We burned a pit for her." He took up the rope again, and looked at it, and put it down. "She's behind the tower. You'll see the stone. There's a feast, so it'll wait until morning."

It was said without cruelty, as a man gives the time. Toomas stood in the lantern-light with a roast goat steaming at his elbow and thought that of all the sentences he had composed in twelve years of the second cup, not one had been *I am too late*, because it had never occurred to him that a thing might simply be over without a hearing. He bowed to his nephew from the neck, as he had bowed to the Assembly. Jaan Pold did not return it.

Reet spoke. "Reeve Pold. You had a notice from the Guild."

"On the fifth."

"A precautionary gathering on the upland. Tomorrow at noon."

"I had one last autumn." Jaan did not look at her. "A survey drill. I sent nine hundred people up the Birch Hanger with blankets, and it rained, and the Guild never came, and we lost two goats and a child's tooth. The year before it was a landslip at the south stair. It wasn't. Guild notices cry wolf, Surveyor. I've a feast to run."

"The ground in this valley is going to be moved," said Reet. "On the night of the day after tomorrow. By a drawing in the vault, a week old. The valley is going to be a lake."

She did not stammer. She said it flatly, as a fact, and it was a fact, and he saw the reeve hear that it was one. He saw something go across the broad face and settle, hardening, as a man will settle his weight when a floor begins to give.

"Then you'll tell me which clerk," said Jaan, "and I'll go and have a word."

"It's not a clerk."

"I'm reeve of Palu." He said it gently. It was worse than shouting. "I'll not cancel the Name-Day on a Guild paper. I'll not send nine hundred people up a hill because a girl in a Surveyor's coat says the ground has been talked to. If it comes, it comes to me, in the road, with the bell in my hand." His eyes went to Toomas, once, and away. "I'm not the reeve who ran. You can tell her that. Whoever she is."

He went off through the crowd toward the plums, with his big shoulders going before him like a plough.

* * *

"He's like that with the bell too," said Liisu. She had appeared at Reet's elbow with a flour sack slung on her back, and she looked up with the air of someone who has seen the grown-ups through a difficult scene and can now get on. "Come behind the tower. I borrowed something."

Behind the tower, in the shadow of the stone, a lantern hung on a nail. Beneath the lantern, with his boots in a bucket of plum stones, a man in a grey Guild coat lay snoring under a trestle. His mouth was open. A brandy-cup rose and fell on his chest like a small boat.

"He came on the fifth with the notice," said Liisu, "and he hasn't been sober since. He says it's the only way to bear the Name-Day." She took from the flour sack an oblong board of pale lime-wood, the size of a tea-tray, with a sheet of onion-skin pinned to it by four brass pins. "I'm going to put it back before he wakes up."

Reet took the board in both hands and held it to the lantern.

Toomas looked over her shoulder. It was a clerk's tracing of a leaf: he knew the hand, the clean impersonal copperplate of the Hollow's copy-room, and the cross-ruled margins, and the fine pinned register-marks at each corner. It was a leaf of the Atlas, in the new colours. In the middle of it, over the place where he knew the town stood, a wash of clerk's blue lay across the valley from the gorge to the ridge. A lake basin, with a neat ruled legend. No bridges. No tower. No fields. No street. The east ridge had been shaded to a cliff-line, hatched and clean, and the upland above it left bare.

"Where's us?" said Liisu. "Where's the Bell Bridge? I looked all over. It's not on it."

"No," said Reet.

"Is that what the Guild thinks? That we're not there?"

"I think the Guild has drawn it that way. Yes."

"That's wrong," said Liisu. It was her flat voice, the voice she had used for the mulberry, and it carried exactly the same degree of outrage, and no more. "We're *here*."

Reet had not answered. She had tilted the board a little, and Toomas saw what she was looking at. At the left margin of the tracing, where the vellum folds into the gutter of the book, a clerk had pricked a tiny hole through the onion-skin to register the original's pinhole: a pin-point of light in the paper. It was very close to the fold. Closer, he thought, than he would have set it. She put the nail of her little finger against it and did not measure.

"I'll look at it by day," she said. Her face was perfectly still. "I have to see it in daylight."

* * *

They crossed the Bell Bridge in the dark, toward the reeve's house, behind Liisu and her lantern. The span was a hundred and sixty feet above the river and it moved a little at each step, with the long slow give of a thing that is used to being walked on, and the drums beat below, and the ribbons whipped at their sleeves. Midway Aino stopped. She put one hand on the cable. She had been walking with her eyes shut and her lips moving, and Toomas had assumed that she was counting.

"Reet," she said. It was a whisper, and for Aino a whisper was a feat. "They're the worst thing I've ever seen."

"I know."

"Forty. I counted them from the gate. And I'm going to cross every one." She opened her eyes. "I've decided. Don't tell the oxen."

"I won't."

"I'll do it on my hands and knees if I have to."

"I'll count them for you."

"Would you?" said Aino, with real gratitude. She moved on.

Toomas had stopped at the centre. He did not mean to. His knee had made a remark, and he leaned on the cable to answer it, and the bridge lifted and fell gently beneath him like the chest of an animal asleep. From here he could see the whole of the town in its web of lights. He could see the lanterns on the other spans, and the ribbons streaming out of the dark toward him, red and yellow and blue, and on the cable beside his hand, among a hundred coloured knots, one white ribbon tied with a child's bad knot.

And next to it, pinned by a loop of string to the rope, a sheet of brown paper. It had been left to swing in the wind of the gorge, with a pebble sewn into each corner for ballast. It was a map. He saw the bell-tower, with its window turned south. He saw the forty bridges, numbered, each with its small drawing, and the mulberry in its corner, and in the margin in a clumsy careful hand: *PALU. Liisu Pold. Her map.* A smaller one, an earlier one, had hung here last year, no doubt, and the year before. She tied them to the bridge so that the bridge would know where it was.

He stood and looked at it for a long time. It was the town he had saved with one stroke of a pen, and the town his saving had very nearly put at the bottom of a lake, drawn on a sack-paper by his great-niece, whom he had met an hour ago, and hung out over the dark on a string. The white ribbon stirred. The map swung out on its string and back, and he saw that the Bell Bridge, which had been drawn with such pains, was drawn exactly right.
