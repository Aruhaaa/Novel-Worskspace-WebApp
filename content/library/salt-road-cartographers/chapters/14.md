# What the Pigeon Carried

Taavi had been gone eleven minutes after kindling, and Reet, who could not help timing things, had begun to wonder what a boy could find to do for eleven minutes on a plain with nothing on it.

She sat with her back to the black stone and her field book on her knee, drawing the carved hands for the fourth time. Each drawing she held at arm's length against the original, and each time the lines agreed, and she did not trust the agreement. The sun was low and red on her right. The groove had emptied at one o'clock, with a small sound like a cup being set down in the next room, and the afternoon since had gone the way afternoons go after a revelation, slowly, with everyone busy at a distance. Toomas slept upright at the stone's far end with the chain-staff across his knees and his moustache lifting on each breath. Aino had been with the oxen since noon. The cart stood tilted on its pole against a wind that did not exist, out of habit, and the shadows lay long and blue and perfectly still, because on the Blank there was nothing to move them.

Then Taavi came across the crust with the basket in both arms.

He walked the way a man carries a bowl filled to the brim. His freckles stood out on a face the colour of tallow, and his bandolier made no sound at all, because he was pinning it against his ribs with his elbows. He stopped six feet from her and stood there. In the dead air his voice came out flat as a slate.

"I wasn't looking in Aino's things."

"No."

"Technically I was looking in the kindling box, under the seat, which is where kindling is, and it has a false back, and I put my hand through, and I'm so sorry, Reet, I'm so sorry, they were *warm*."

She closed the book.

It was a narrow travelling cage of peeled willow with a leather hood, and it had six stalls. Five of them held a grey bird apiece, pressed onto its perch like a clerk into a pew. Their eyes were open. They made no sound. The sixth stall was empty and had a ring of red sealing wax on its floor. Tucked into the lid-strap was a roll of tissue slips no thicker than a cigar, and a stiff card of waxed linen ruled in a grid of letters, and across the foot of the card, in a looping hand that put long tails on every g and y, were the words *Keep dry. Burn if taken.*

She knew the hand. She had been reading it in red pencil for six years.

She had filed this in the ferns at the Gullet, with the clatter like shaken cards and the lantern carried north, and she had told herself that she was waiting for the right time to take it out and look at it. She saw now that she had been in no hurry at all. She liked Aino. It was an inconvenient thing to find out about a person you were about to be right about.

Inside the tissue roll was a slip that had been written and not sent, creased flat, the copy a careful agent keeps until the receipt comes back. It was cipher: a square of paired letters, the row giving the first of each pair and the column the second. She worked it in her head. It took her under a minute.

*SALLIK, VESKIMAE, ILVES BOY. PILGRIMS TO PALU BRIDGE. V LAME RIGHT KNEE. S DOES NOT SLEEP. EATS WHAT SHE IS GIVEN. SHE COUNTS. R.*

She read the last three words twice. They were true. They were the kind of true that you are when you have been watched by someone who was not unkind about it.

"Put it down on the stone, Taavi. Gently. They're alive."

"I know they're alive, that's what's—"

"Gently."

* * *

Aino was at the picket line with a bucket, watering Registrar, who had risen at last and stood with his great head down, drinking like a bellows. She had her back to them. Reet came up on her left side and set the basket on the crust between them, and stood.

"They're quiet," said Reet. "For hens."

Aino looked at the basket. She did not look at Reet. She put the bucket down with care, as though it were still full. "You train them to it. You can't have a pigeon cooing on a salt road. People ask."

"Master Org asked. At the Gullet. He said your name."

"I wondered if you'd caught that."

"I caught it. I filed it, with the clatter on the first night and the way you carried it north at dusk." She heard her own voice, flat and level, the voice she used for reading chain-figures aloud. "I've known since the Gullet, Aino. I didn't say. I'd like you to know that I knew and didn't say."

"Why not?"

"You gave me an egg. I didn't want to find out what it had cost."

Aino laughed. It was not a good sound. Behind Reet, at a careful distance, Taavi had begun to hiccup.

"Three hundred and forty," said Aino, to the ox. "Silver marks. My brother Rasmus. He plays cards, and he's clever, and the two of them together are the worst thing that ever happened to a family. There's a house on Lamp Street that deals them. They hold his note, and his person, and they know where he sleeps." One enormous shoulder lifted and dropped. "A grey coat from the Surveyor's office came to the Company yard the afternoon you booked your passage. He paid a hundred down, to keep Rasmus's hands in one piece, and said the other two hundred and forty would follow the day I did a small service for the Master. It was nothing, he said. A girl called Sallik, and where she went."

"And Rasmus is in the Hollow."

"In a room over a baker's, with two gentlemen who are extremely nice about it."

"I see."

"Do you?" It came up out of Aino all at once, as the cry had come at noon. "Do you? With your head full of every road in Orrin, and a Keeper who sends for you and pours you tea? I sent one. One bird, the first night. What you ate. What you said. How many times you looked at the sky. It's a girl's breakfast, it's *nothing*, and it's the whole of my brother's neck. And I haven't sent another. Three dusks I've carried that basket out into the dark and stood there with it, and three times I've carried it back. You want to know what that's like? You want to know what it is to stand in the dark holding the thing that will—"

She had taken the salt-hook off her back. Reet had not seen her do it. She flung it down, not at Reet but near her, and it struck the crust a yard from Reet's boot with a flat dead clank, like a spoon dropped on a rug.

There was no echo. That was the strangeness of it. A quarrel on the Blank had no audience, and so no way of becoming a performance; it had to be exactly as loud as it was, and no louder, and the words did not go anywhere after they were said. They lay on the ground between the two of them, where they could be seen.

Reet did not step back. She was five feet tall and Aino was six feet two and the hook lay glinting between them, and she tilted her head up and looked, as she would have looked at a cliff, without opinion. "Pick it up or leave it there," she said. "I'm not going to move, so you may as well decide."

Aino's chest was going like Registrar's. The tears had started again and she did nothing to stop them. "I've never said it," she said, much more quietly. "Do you know that? Not to a soul. That I can't afford to be honest. I tell jokes. I name the oxen. Do you know why I name them for officials? Every one of them is a man with a say over somebody like me, and I can stand in front of a Treasurer who's an ox and shout at him till I'm hoarse." She dragged her wrist over her face. "And then you walked into the yard with a satchel and a way of looking at the ground, and I thought, that one will find me out. And you did. You've known for days, and you said nothing."

"I lied to the Keeper," said Reet.

Aino's mouth stopped.

"She asked if I'd spoken to Toomas. I said no. I stammered, three syllables, and she heard it, and so did Toomas, from the next street." Her hands had closed. She opened them. "I can't do it, Aino. I never could. So when I say I know something of what it costs, it isn't kindness. It's information. I know what it is to be caught by your own mouth. You've been caught by your pocket. You've a better mouth than I have, that's all the difference."

For a while the only sound was Registrar, drinking.

"Who've you got, then?" said Aino at last, roughly. "Back home?"

"Ants. He's eleven. He lights the lamps."

"That's the list?"

"My mother. In Sora. She writes about drains."

"That's the *list*?"

Reet considered. "Master Org," she said, "used to leave me the crusts of his pastries."

Aino sat down. She did it all at once, on the crust, like a tent coming down, and put her face in her hands, and Reet could not tell whether the noise she made was laughter or the other thing, and thought on the whole that Aino could not either. "Poor girl," said Aino, into her palms.

"I'm not."

"You are a bit."

"I am a bit," Reet agreed. She crouched, and picked up the salt-hook, which was heavier than it looked, and held it out by the haft. After some time Aino took it.

* * *

"Mm."

He had come across the crust without a sound, which on the Blank any man could, and he had plainly been standing there for some while, leaning on the staff with his bad leg out. His moustache was crumpled on one side where he had slept against the stone. He looked at the basket, and at the card of waxed linen in Taavi's fingers, and at Aino, and at the hook, in that order, and made no remark about any of them.

"I wondered about the hens," said Toomas. "They ate grain and they never laid. Aino. How does the Guild pay you for this?"

"By the bird. A bird at dusk, a day's wage. Where we are, what we do."

"Where we are and what we do." He seemed to taste it. "I observe that it does not specify a standard of accuracy."

"It's a Guild paper. There's a clause for everything."

"Is there a clause about truth?"

Aino's head came up. She thought. Reet watched her think, and watched a number of long habits of fear rearrange themselves in her face. "No," she said slowly. "No, I don't think there is."

"Then I will say to you what I would have said to any journeyman of mine. You are paid by the day, Aino, not by the sentence."

There was a pause.

"Technically," said Taavi, hiccuping, "that's—"

"Taavi."

"—brilliant. Sorry."

"I can't," said Reet. "Not aloud. And the Master knows Aino's hand."

"Mm. Reet cannot lie. Aino can. It is the better part of her trade. She sold three people passage as pilgrims to a bridge shrine, and kept a perfectly straight face, and I watched her do it." Toomas turned to the tall woman on the ground. "You need only write a bird that Mihkel Org will believe, and you are not asked to believe it yourself."

* * *

They did it by lantern at the foot of the black stone, as the last of the day went out of the plain. The white crust turned the colour of the inside of a pearl, then of ash, and the stars came on all at once, with nothing between them and the ground. Aino sat on the cart-tail with a slip across her knee and the pencil stub in her fist. She wrote the plain draft first, on the back of the card, in block capitals, and her tongue came out of the corner of her mouth, and twice she stopped and looked at the sky for help.

"Read it back," she said at last. "I want to hear how it sounds. I can't tell by looking."

Reet took the card. She held it to the lantern.

"'Caravan lost in the Seam, night of the sixth,'" she read. "'Hill came down on camp. Salt lake rose from the ground. All drowned. Veskimae last seen holding moustache above water. Sallik went down counting.'" She paused. "'Ilves boy went down apologising.'"

"I thought that was a nice touch," said Aino.

"'Registrar drowned first, with dignity.'"

A sound came from Taavi, a small high squeak.

"'Auditor sank shouting. Guildmaster tried to bargain with the water. Treasurer kept the receipts above the waterline to the last.'" She went on in the same level voice, since it was only reading. There was no stammer in it anywhere, and she noticed that with a small cold interest, as one notices that a pin does not hurt when it is held against the skin and not pushed. A lie she was reading aloud was not a lie she was telling. "'The ox called Mihkel fought the water to the end and was heard to quote. Writer escaped on a barrel. Bird only survivor. Will not report further. Debt to be closed.'"

Taavi had gone over onto his side. He lay on the crust with both hands on his stomach, laughing, in great soundless heaves that the Blank swallowed, and each one ended in a hiccup that was louder than the laugh. "Registrar," he gasped. "Is *lying* there. He's *chewing*."

At the sound of his name Registrar lifted his head, regarded them with enormous contempt, and went on chewing.

"Strike the last line," said Toomas.

"The debt?"

"A dead woman who writes to ask after her wages is not credible. Let them come to you with it. They will be most gracious about it, I think, and it will be more convincing."

Aino struck the line. She enciphered the rest herself, with her lips moving, pair by pair, off the linen square, and Reet checked it letter by letter and did not find a mistake. It took half an hour, and in that half hour nobody said anything of importance, and Reet found that she had stopped being afraid of the thing in the cage and had begun to be afraid of the lightness she felt, which was surely not warranted by any facts.

* * *

Aino chose the grey one with the white fleck at its throat. She slid the quill capsule on its leg and sealed it with a smear of wax from the stub of the candle, and she stood in the last grey light on the north side of the Hearth-Stone with the bird on her open palm and did not throw it. It stood there. It turned its small round head, one way and then the other. It looked at the plain, and there was nothing in it: no tower, no hill, no hedge, no line of smoke, no river, not a thing for its small stubborn mind to take a bearing from.

"Veikko said there's no bird on the Blank," said Reet.

"There's one," said Taavi, "for about a minute."

Aino lifted her hand. The pigeon went up in a clatter of wings that was the loudest thing the Blank had heard in three hundred years, and the sound came back to them as nothing. It rose. It circled the stone twice with its head cocked, looking for something to be told, and found nothing, and hung there in the green light over their faces; and then, as though it had decided to take the world on trust, it turned and flew south.

"It's going the wrong way," said Taavi.

"He'll know when he's out of the quiet," said Aino. "He's got a head like Treasurer's. It takes a while."

Reet counted the wingbeats until she lost it, at ninety-one.

Aino had picked up the empty basket and was holding it against her chest, as though there might be some part of it left to keep. "I'll tell you something," she said. "Mihkel. The ox. I put in that he fought to the end. I thought: it's the nicest thing I've ever said of him." Her mouth did something complicated. "I'm not naming another one for anyone I respect. Not ever. It's a liberty. You can't put a person on an animal like that."

"Who do you respect?" said Taavi.

"That's the trouble," said Aino. "I've started."

Overhead the sky was perfectly empty, and a long way south, over ground that moved, a bird was finding its way home by a road no one had drawn. Reet stood very still and let the number sit in the place she kept numbers. Two hundred and forty. It did not get any smaller.
