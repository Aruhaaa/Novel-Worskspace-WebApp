# Sugar for the Gentleman

The hospital's wheelchair had a number stenciled on its back, HC-0447, and a strip of tape reading PROPERTY OF MERROW GENERAL, and Mr Grieve had already asked twice whether he was permitted to take it out of the county. It rolled over the oval of carpet worn to canvas in front of the window in 6A on Monday evening and stopped where his own chair had stopped for thirty-six years. He looked down at 31st Street, at the bus shelter and the wet black road, and said "Quite so," as if the street had given a satisfactory answer.

They had sent him home with an oxygen concentrator that hummed like a refrigerator, a hospital bed that Rafa and Owen Fenske had carried up in two pieces and put together in the front room under the window, and a pamphlet. Priscilla Obi had come down from 5E in her scrubs to look at the dials. I had seen her twice in fourteen months, in the elevator, with her hood up. She read the pamphlet standing, and put it in the bin, and showed Chioma and Anjali how to turn a man in a bed without hurting the shoulder. "I'm on nights," she said to the room, which was the longest speech I had heard from her. "Knock on the floor."

"I would like to go on Tuesday," said Mr Grieve. "At four."

"Mr Grieve—"

"Strictly speaking, Mr Mahajan, I have asked, and you have said you would ask, and I should like the answer." He had the cannula in, and the tweed waistcoat on over the pajama top, and the brass duck across his knees. "Not in the abstract. At four."

I had gone in alone at five to four that afternoon, nine in and nine out, and found Mr Ambler in his shirtsleeves by the radio with the ball game on and a pencil behind one ear. I said there was a gentleman in the building who sent him tea, and that the gentleman was unwell, and would like very much to shake his hand. Mr Ambler put the pencil down. He said, "The fellow with the tea," and then, "Well, I'll be." He said he would ring down for a tray. He said Tuesday would suit him, since his afternoon calls were all in, and that I should tell the fellow not to trouble about a hat.

* * *

On Tuesday we carried him, because the threshold of the door is a hand's height and a wheelchair is not a thing that the room will let in. Chioma and I made a seat of our crossed hands and took him down from the corridor chair like a man being carried out of a river, and he weighed what a coat weighs on a hanger. Rafa folded the chair. He stood with it under his arm in the corridor with the key in the lock, as he had stood on the twenty-third of October, and gave me the face of a man at a funeral, and a nod.

"Count in," I said.

"Nine," said Mr Grieve, who had been counting since the corridor. Chioma carried the oxygen in a canvas bag over her shoulder, like a sack of mail. It was thirty-one paces to the door, and nine from the door to the window, and on the ninth I heard a ball game, small and cheerful, and a man's voice say, "Well, there he is."

It was the same room I had been in on the twenty-seventh of October, the striped paper and the chenille bed and the sample case, and the window with the green HARWICK awning and the tan Pontiac below it. There was a hotel tray on the table by the radio with two cups, a pot under a cozy, a bowl of cubes with tongs, and a plate of soda crackers. I did not see who had brought it. I have stopped asking the room how.

Mr Ambler had put on his jacket. He had pulled the bow tie off the hook and tied it, a little crooked. He came around the bed with his hand out, round-faced and brisk, the wet side parting combed across, and then he saw the cannula and slowed, and said, "Sir, are you ill?"

"Strictly speaking," said Mr Grieve, "I am concluding." Chioma had set him in the hotel armchair. He took the hand that was held out, in both of his. "Ptolemy Grieve. The P is silent."

"Tol-uh-mee. Thaddeus Ambler, Rosewell Dental Supply, Dayton, Ohio—"

"Of Dayton, Ohio. A salesman. I know. I have had the pleasure of knowing it for a long time."

"Good man," said Mr Ambler. "Good man. You're the one with the tea. I've been wanting to say thank you for I don't know how long." He glanced at me. "Mr Mahajan. The floss gentleman."

"Technically I'm the buyer."

"He is a very good buyer, Mr Mahajan," said Mr Ambler. "He paid thirty-five cents and didn't ask for change."

Mr Grieve took the pot. His hands shook, and Chioma took one step and he looked at her and she stepped back. He lifted the cozy and poured, one cup and then the other, and put down the pot and took up the tongs. "I have been sending you this cup for thirty-six years, Mr Ambler, and have never once seen it drunk. Permit me." He lowered a cube into the saucer's edge and held the tongs up. "Two lumps. You take two lumps, I think."

"Two, thank you," said Mr Ambler.

I want it on the record that nobody had told him. I do not know if he was right, or if the room was being kind, or if there is any difference in a room like that, and I have decided it is not a question with an answer. He put in the second cube. His hand did not shake at all.

They talked for ten minutes. I have written down some of it on the pad, and the rest I will not need to. The home nine were two up in the sixth. "I follow the local nine, Mr Ambler," said Mr Grieve, "which is a form of mourning." Mr Ambler laughed so hard he had to put down his cup. He offered to sell Mr Grieve a gross of floss, and Mr Grieve said that he had his own teeth, and had had them since 1948, and Mr Ambler said that a man with his own teeth at his age was a credit to the profession. And then, because the plate of soda crackers had been going round, Mr Ambler said, "I'll tell you the hotel has been doing a decent biscuit lately. A sort of plain sweet one. It used to be nothing but soda crackers."

"A Rich Tea," said Mr Grieve.

"That's it."

"I have sent you a Rich Tea at four o'clock every afternoon since 1989."

Mr Ambler looked at the plate, and at him. "Those were yours? I took it for the hotel improving." He put his hand over his mouth, and laughed, and then did not. "Well. Well, that's the best thing that's happened to me in some time."

It is the best thing I have ever watched two people do. I have watched people sign leases and I have watched Brent from Tenantry say super at a camera. I have never seen anything better than two lumps.

* * *

"There is a matter," said Mr Grieve, "that I should like to place on the record."

The ball game had gone to a commercial. Mr Ambler turned down the radio, with a look at the black telephone on the night table that I recognized from my first visit and had not understood. "In 1994," said Mr Grieve, "I wrote to Dayton. To the register of deaths for the county. I had a name and a date of arrival. I should have asked first, and I did not." The cough took him, and let him go. "Lorna Ambler died in the spring of 1979. I am so sorry. I have known for thirty-one years and I did not know how to say it."

Mr Ambler looked at the telephone. He picked up the cup and looked into it. "I thought as much," he said quietly. "I couldn't tell you when I began to. The cables stopped being answered. A woman who answers a telephone in Dayton doesn't let it ring twelve times."

Nobody said anything. In the pipes behind the wall someone tapped once, a long way off, and stopped.

"I'm waiting anyway," said Mr Ambler. "I hope you'll understand. If I step out of this room, the call's gone. It rings and there's no one."

"Then I shall take your call," said Mr Grieve. "On Sundays, at a quarter past four. As long as I am able."

Mr Ambler pulled the hotel pad over, and wrote, and tore off the sheet and held it out. HARWICK 6A, in a salesman's slanting hand. Mr Grieve read it with his lips and folded it into his waistcoat. "Quite so," said Mr Ambler.

I did not ask how a telephone from 1961 would find a number from this year. I had stopped asking the room how.

At the door, with Chioma's arms under Mr Grieve and mine under his knees, I turned around. I had thought about what I would say for three days, and I said it plainly, as one says the name of a guest at the head of a table. "Thaddeus Ambler." He looked at me over the cup. I should say that the door was not finished. I did not say it as an order and I did not say it as an offer. "If there were a door that went out onto 31st Street, in daylight, a proper one. Would you come?"

He thought about it properly, which was why I asked him. "I'm obliged to you, Mr Mahajan. I truly am." He set the cup in its saucer. "But I think not. I'd rather be in the room it rings in."

That was the first answer anyone in that room had given me, and I wrote it down on the pad with a crossed seven in the date.

* * *

Mrs Han, Zofia and Chioma took shifts at the bed in 6A that evening, with Priscilla Obi on the chair by the door from eleven, and Anjali beside her with her hands loose in her lap. Rafa made coffee in the kitchen with the toothpick in his mouth. At half past one Mr Grieve asked for the steno pad.

He dictated slowly, with his eyes on the ceiling, as he must have heard it in a thousand court rooms, and I wrote it in longhand. When I had finished he made me read it back, and corrected a comma.

> Let the record show that I, Ptolemy Grieve, of 6A Harwick Terrace, being of sound mind and indifferent lungs, was attended. I leave to Devendra Mahajan the steno pads, ninety-one of them unused, the dictation recorder, and the tenancy of this apartment, at the pleasure of the management, who is requested not to be difficult. The cane to Mrs Han, who will need something to point with. The watch to Miss Eze, who has the better use for time. The clocks are to be left as they are.

"Is that correct?" said Mr Grieve.

"Yes."

"I did not ask you," he said. "I asked the record." He moved his hand on the blanket, and found the saucer on the bed table, with its cold cup, and let his fingers rest on the rim.

At ten to four he asked what time it was, and Zofia said, "Is nearly," and measured his wrist with the tape, without a word, and wrote it in her book. At four Mrs Han said, "Good morning, Mr Grieve," in the voice she uses for the chat, and he said "Quite so." I looked down at the pad to write the time. When I looked up, at ten past four by the gold watch that I had in my hand, the saucer had been turned. The handle pointed left. His fingers were on the rim and he had stopped breathing.

Priscilla Obi looked at her own watch and wrote it down, and said the time aloud, for the record, and then none of us said anything. Chioma took his hand. The concentrator went on humming in the next room, a long time, until Rafa went in and switched it off.

* * *

At four that afternoon Mrs Han went up to the fourth floor with the plate. She carried it in both hands, the cup and the saucer with the rose and one Rich Tea, as she carries the thermos, in front of her chest. She had put on the church blouse again. I stood on the stair landing below the ledge with Chioma and Zofia. She set it down, and turned the handle of the saucer to the left, as she had been shown.

"Four o'clock, Mr Ambler," she said. "Mr Grieve sends his regards."

She went down. I did not go with her. At ten past, I climbed the half-flight and looked.

The cup was warm and empty. The Rich Tea was gone. The saucer had been turned, and the handle pointed left: yes.
