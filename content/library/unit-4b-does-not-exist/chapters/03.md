# Posted by Mahajan

Joyce Lim was holding a five-dollar bill up to the window when I came in on Wednesday, turning it by one corner as if it might yet show her something it had been saving. It was 7:40. Rain had run down the front glass overnight and dried in tracks, and the light she held the note to came through the backward letters of EMBRACE THE CHANGE. The bill had a red seal and a red serial number, and across the top it said UNITED STATES NOTE, which I had not known they ever printed on a five. SERIES 1963, said the small type beside the portrait.

"Good morning, Joyce."

"Noted," said Joyce, to the bill.

I had not slept in the way that counts. At four I had decided that a man with a problem goes in early, and at 7:15 I had gone in early, and now I sat down at my desk with my coat on and my bag still on my shoulder and clicked the blue link I had not clicked the night before. Show full history.

Tenantry lays out an audit trail the way a bank statement lays out a life: newest on top, no adjectives. I counted fifteen lines.

> HT-4B | Audit | 10/06 23:58 | ACKNOWLEDGED | Amount: none | Source of Funds: PREPARER | By: DMAHAJAN

> HT-4B | Audit | 08/19 23:58 | RENT POSTED | Method: CASH | Amount: $1,840.00 | Source of Funds: PREPARER | By: DMAHAJAN

Between those two stood thirteen more, and they were the same line in different months. RENT POSTED, CASH, $1,840.00, on the nineteenth of every month at 23:58, from August of last year to the nineteenth of September: one for each month I had held the job, to the day. The odd one out was the fifteenth, at the top. It was Monday's, and it had no amount. I am a man who reads verbs. RENT POSTED is something that happens to money. ACKNOWLEDGED is what you say to someone who has knocked twice.

The nineteenth of August was my first day. I know where I was at 23:58 on my first day, because it is the kind of day you remember: on top of the covers in my work shirt, worn out in a way I had mistaken for contentment. I did not need to check the other thirteen. I am asleep by eleven, and have been since college.

The Source of Funds column said PREPARER on every line, and that was the part I could not leave alone. Brent had been clear that the field was new. RentWise's posting screen had four boxes, date and amount and method and memo, and I had used it for thirteen months; nowhere on it could a person in August have typed a word about a source. So the migration had stamped each old line with the default, which is the sort of thing a migration does, and I wrote that in the margin of a printout. Under it I wrote the other explanation, which was that the lines had said PREPARER all along. I put a question mark beside it and drew a box around the question mark.

Before the nineteenth of August the trail fell away to a single gray line reading MIGRATED, with no name next to it. Tenantry, I learned from its help page, carries across the history only of users who still hold an account, which is a rule a vendor makes by accident and a clerk lives under on purpose. Whoever had posted 4B's rent ahead of me no longer had an account. I wrote that down too.

I opened the banking app on my phone, since that was the other place a source of funds might be hiding. Fourteen months, and not one withdrawal of eighteen hundred dollars or any piece of it. My money is a modest, well-behaved thing. It goes to Tolliver Court, the phone company and Halloran's.

That left the possibility that someone had my password. I changed it (nineteen characters, no words, a code that goes to my phone) and sat looking at the confirmation. Next came the Corvane help desk, which has a form for Unauthorized Access, with a drop-down for Severity and a field for Affected Record. I could see the Affected Record arriving on Randall Pugh's dashboard before lunch, in red, with a note. Randall's remedy for HT-4B was already built into the software. It was a small gray button, top right.

* * *

At ten past eight I carried the printout to Joyce's cubicle. The five was in a clear sleeve in her cash tray now, and she was working through money orders with her cardigan sleeves pushed to the elbow.

"The cash from 4B," I said. "What's in the envelope?"

"Noted." She squared a stack against the blotter. "Twenties, mostly. Ninety of them. Then it changes. Tens, ones, a five. Some months there's something old."

"Old."

"Like that." She tipped her head at the sleeve. "September's. The bank's machine spat it out. The teller looked it up and said a collector would give twelve dollars for it, and asked if I'd robbed a museum." One corner of her mouth moved. "I said I reconcile what I'm shown. I won't bank a five that's worth twelve, it isn't mine to undervalue. So it sits in the tray, and the tray counts it as five."

"Have there been others?"

"March. A silver certificate, nineteen fifty-seven, blue seal. One dollar. I banked that. A dollar is a dollar."

"Who leaves it?"

"It's in the drop when I open the safe on the twentieth. Plain envelope, 4B on the front in pencil." She looked up at me. "I don't see it come. I see it when it's there."

"Who did the deposits before me?"

"I do the books. Margie did the run to the bank."

"Margie who?"

"Dunleavy." She said it the way you read a name off a card. "Before you. She sent a Christmas card every year with a robin on it. I have it somewhere."

She opened her second drawer, which sticks, and went through a folder of cards, then the folder behind it, then the space under the shortbread tin. Joyce has a steady pair of hands, and I watched them go faster. She took everything out and laid it on the blotter in the order it had come out, and then put it all back in the same order, and shut the drawer.

"It was here," she said. "Noted." The second one was addressed to nobody. "I could tell you her handwriting. Round. I couldn't tell you what she looked like." She looked at me. "That's a thing to say about someone you stood next to at a safe."

"It's probably in a box at home."

"Noted," said Joyce, in the voice that means it is not.

"Is there a receipt when the cash comes in?"

"One carbon. It's in the tray when I get in on the twenty-first. I file it with the slip."

"Who signs it?"

"You do."

"I don't."

Joyce considered me for as long as it takes a calculator to print a total. "I assumed you did it late," she said. "Some people like to do the cash at night." And she turned back to her money orders and counted the same stack twice, which in fourteen months I had never seen her do.

* * *

The filing room is a windowless closet with a lock Noreen never uses, three gray cabinets and a stool that tips. The receipts were in the brown accordion folder marked CASH RECEIPTS, behind BANK REC, and Joyce had filed them the way she files everything, in order, with the staples all at one angle. There were fourteen, numbered HT-4B-0819 through HT-4B-0919, thin pink carbons from a book with a red serial number in the corner.

> Received of: OCCUPANT, Unit 4B | The sum of: One thousand eight hundred forty and 00/100 | For: Rent | Received by: D. Mahajan

I held the top sheet to the bulb. The D had the long back stroke I give it. The M had two peaks of unequal height. The pen had pressed harder at the end of Mahajan than at the start, because I am always hurrying to be finished with my own name, and the j had a loop in it that I would have said was not mine, except that I have made it exactly that way on birthday cards: a little more confident than the rest of me. On the July receipt the date was 7/19, and the seven wore a bar through its stem. My mother wrote hers like that, the way she had been taught in Pune, and she taught me at eight, and a teacher in Edison marked mine wrong for a year before she gave up. I cross them still. Whoever had crossed this one had learned from the same woman.

It was my handwriting and I had not been there, which is a sentence that does not belong in a filing room.

"You're in early, hon."

Noreen stood in the doorway with her tea in one hand and her lanyard bunched in the other, and I understood that the keys had been silent on purpose, carried the way you carry a sleeping child.

"I'm doing a reconciliation."

Her eyes went to the folder in my hands, to the tab, 4B in Joyce's block capitals. The gum stopped. "Good records," she said. "That's why I hired you." Then, brightly, the way you draw a curtain: "Did you talk to Randall?"

"I'm reviewing the remediation path."

"Is that a yes?"

"It's a path."

"Don't let it turn into a project, hon," said Noreen, and went off with her keys held still.

* * *

I took the contact-verification printout up to four at a quarter past nine. It was a real task: one page per unit, a box to tick beside Phone and another beside Emergency Contact, because Corvane wanted every number in Tenantry confirmed by the thirty-first and it had not occurred to anyone that a clerk might treat that as a reason to knock on a door. I started with 4A. That meant walking past the cabinet, and I did it at the speed of a man carrying a form.

Chioma Eze opened the door as far as the chain allowed, shut it, took the chain off and opened it again, which is the correct order for a woman who has been awake since yesterday afternoon. She was six feet tall in her socks. She wore a gray hospital hoodie with MERROW GENERAL RESPIRATORY across the chest and red-framed glasses, and her twists were tied up in a scarf, the ends dyed the gold of honey that has been left in the sun. The hand on the door had an old shiny burn on the inside of the wrist, about the size of a thumbprint.

"Mahajan," she said. It was less a greeting than a spot check.

"Good morning. Sorry. It's contact verification, for the new system. Phone and emergency contact. One minute."

"For the record, it's nine seventeen. I've been up since four p.m. yesterday. A minute from you is a long time from me." She held out a hand. "Give."

I gave her the page. She read down it with her lips pressed together, like a person reading a bill from a company she intends to leave. "Phone's right. Mother's right. That number hasn't changed since I was nine, she'll outlive the cell towers." She handed it back. "Tick it."

I ticked it. There was a pause the width of the doorway, and I used it. "Is there anything else on the unit? Maintenance, noise. There's a Notes field. Two hundred fifty-five characters."

"For the record, I have no complaints. The neighbors are quiet." She pushed her glasses up with a knuckle. "Quieter than 3B's television."

"Which neighbors?"

She looked at me for some time. Behind her, down a short hall, a bedroom door stood open, and on the wall beside the bed there was something written in pencil, small and in rows, like a page. Then she shifted her weight and it was only a wall.

"Mahajan, don't."

"I only meant, technically, that on your east side there's a fire-hose cabinet."

"I know what's on my east side." She said it at the volume you would use in a library. "The radiator asks questions."

She said it so evenly that for a moment I took it for a clause in the lease.

"What does it—"

"I work nights. Forget I said that." The door was already moving. "Thank you for verifying me."

The chain went on. The green clogs on the mat stayed where they were, toes to the door.

In the corridor I put my heel against the sill of 4A and walked to 4C. Fourteen. I turned and walked back and got fourteen again, and that was worse. On Monday the two directions had disagreed, and a disagreement is at least a kind of measurement. Now they had settled on a longer corridor between them, without consulting me.

The smell of toast came and went at the cabinet, like an oven door opened and closed in someone else's kitchen. I did not knock. I did not say anything to it either, though I wanted to apologize for something, and could not have told you what.

Randall had written at 9:48.

> From: Randall Pugh | To: Devendra Mahajan | Cc: Noreen Whitmore | Date: Wed 8 Oct, 9:48 AM | Subject: RE: PHANTOM UNITS: ACTION REQUIRED BY 10/31

> Dev, HT-4B is still live on my dashboard. Going forward I need a date I can put against it. Can you confirm by EOD what the remediation path is and who owns it? Appreciate the bandwidth. Randall

I do my best work in the genre of the reply that says nothing. I once held off a regional vice president for eleven days over a tenant's ferret. I wrote:

> From: Devendra Mahajan | To: Randall Pugh | Cc: Noreen Whitmore | Date: Wed 8 Oct, 10:31 AM | Subject: RE: RE: PHANTOM UNITS: ACTION REQUIRED BY 10/31

> Hi Randall, thank you for flagging. I am reviewing the remediation path for HT-4B and will revert with a timeline once I have confirmed the data lineage. Best, Dev

Data lineage means nothing, which is its charm. Randall answered in four minutes: Great. Let's circle back Friday. I had bought two days. I have a method for buying days and, that morning, no idea what to spend them on.

* * *

At some point in the afternoon the office went quiet the way it does around three, when Espie is out showing 2B and Joyce's calculator is the only thing awake, and I sat back and my knee went into the drawer, where it always goes. I said sorry to it. Then, for the first time in fourteen months, I opened it on purpose.

It stuck, and gave, with a sound like a cough. The lemon-drop tin was flat and old, with a lemon on the lid worn to a yellow smudge by other people's thumbs. Inside were two lemon drops, fused at one edge by their own sugar. Beside the tin lay the horn button, the color of oatmeal, with four holes and a quarter inch of oatmeal thread still through one of them. It had been pulled off something, not cut.

I had assumed, the way you assume things about a chair, that whoever sat here before me had left these behind by accident. I now thought they had been left the way you leave a pot on a low flame for someone arriving late. Joyce had a name for her. Margie, who sent a robin every Christmas and did the run to the bank, had held this job, and the job had come with a chair, a drawer, a tin and, I now suspected, a standing appointment at two minutes to midnight on the nineteenth. I had believed I was hired to keep forty-one rows. I had been hired to keep one of them, and I had kept it for fourteen months without once being awake. I had been working for 4B since the morning I first sat down.

On the screen the payment history for HT-4B had a column I had never read, between Method and Balance, headed Posted by. I scrolled it from the top. D. Mahajan. D. Mahajan. D. Mahajan. Below the fourteenth the column said MIGRATED, all the way down to 2003. I have signed things I did not read: a lease, a gym contract, a consent form in a hospital corridor. Everyone has. I had never signed for something I was not there for. I put the tip of one finger against the glass at the first of the lines, where the monitor had warmed it, and it read, in Tenantry's calm gray type, Posted by D. Mahajan, which is the sort of sentence you cannot un-sign.
