# Walk-Through

A red dot slid along the beige wall of the fourth-floor corridor at a quarter to four on Wednesday the twelfth, stopping at each door frame the way a finger stops on a line of print.

Behind it, holding the instrument at the height of his sternum, was Woo-jin Cho of Larchmont Residential Partners, who had been in the building since nine. He was six foot three and carried the stoop of a man who has been hitting his head on doorframes since junior high. Round glasses, a neat side part, a Casio on a steel band, and a badge on a retractable reel that he pulled out and let go, pulled out and let go, like an anxious tape measure. A pocket notebook in one hand and a clipboard under the other arm. "Woo," he had said to Noreen at the front door. "The badge says Woo-jin. Nobody has used that since college."

He had done the three lower floors in the order of the checklist. On two, Owen Fenske had handed him a list of eleven complaints, and Woo had listened to all eleven, written them down and said "Out of scope" to each, which Owen told me afterward was the happiest he had ever been.

Randall Pugh was with us in the form of an iPad in a leather folio, which Noreen carried against her chest like a hymnal and held up at each door. Behind him were a lemon tree and a very blue sea. He had the tan of the sixth of October. "Going forward," he said at the door of 4D, to nobody's bathroom.

Espie had made an event of the corridor. There was a rolling garment rack, a folding table, a hand-lettered sign saying FOLD LOUNGE (POP-UP), and eight tenants in a row with baskets of laundry. Mrs Han held one corner of a fitted sheet that was beating her. Zofia made hospital corners and measured each with the tape for show. Owen had dark T-shirts. Rafa stood in the stairwell door with a radiator key, bleeding a radiator that did not need it. They stood very still, in the manner of people who have been told to look natural.

"The dryers are in the basement," said Woo.

"Ok so that's the old model," said Espie. "We're activating the corridor as an amenity."

"I love an activation," said the iPad.

I stood at the 4A mat in the raincoat with my name in the collar. Anjali had been left upstairs in 5C with a bowl of chestnuts and instructions.

"Per the checklist, common-area dimensions are verified against the plan." Woo planted his shoes on the mat and aimed the laser across the maroon diamonds at the frame of 4C. It beeped. "Twenty-two feet, zero inches. Per plan."

"It's thirty-one," I said.

"Thirty-one what?"

"Paces. Mine."

He looked at me over the round glasses with the polite horror of a man who has been handed a receipt in a foreign currency. Then he walked it, with a long even stride, like a heron going through shallows, and said "Eight" at the far door. He walked back. "Eight."

"It was twelve on the way back," I said. "On the sixth."

"A count is not a measurement."

"Technically it's a measurement of me."

He wrote that down. I could not tell if it was a joke he was recording or a finding.

At the middle of the wall he stopped, because the checklist stopped him. "Fire hose cabinet. Recessed, glass front, riser R-4, per plan." He bent to the nozzle and found with visible joy that the paper inspection tag had expired in March. He wrote it under Life Safety. He went a little pink.

"Mr Cho," I said. "Would you put your hand on the wall? Just there. Left of the cabinet, about here." I showed him, chest height, where I had always heard it.

"There's no hand-on-wall step on the checklist."

"No."

"Then why?"

"Because it's the only evidence I can give you that you can carry."

He considered that, over the glasses, the way he must have considered a good many requests that were not on the form. Then he let the reel snap back, and put his palm flat on the plaster.

Two knocks came out of the wall under his hand. Unhurried, like someone confirming a delivery.

He took the hand away as if it were hot. "That's the riser."

"That's two knocks."

"Pipes knock."

"In pairs," I said. "With a pause. And then they wait. Please. Knock back."

On the iPad, Pugh was explaining to a ceiling tile that what we were really solving for was optics. In the row of tenants no one folded anything. Woo looked at the plaster, and at me, and made a fist, and knocked on the wall twice with the second knuckle.

"Good afternoon, young man," said the wall.

It was dry and flat and clear, and a little muffled, and entirely courteous. "Would you mind terribly not deleting me?"

I have thought about that sentence more than any other in this note. In six years Margie had asked for nothing. She had said, to me, that she would have liked to see a spring but was not asking. And here she had put a request to a stranger with a clipboard, in the tone of a woman asking whether the window could be shut.

Pugh was still talking. He had not heard it. I will say that for the record, since it was the point: he did not look up, and the folio did not flinch, and Noreen, with her eyes shut, held it very straight.

Woo opened his mouth, and shut it, and opened it again. "Good afternoon," he said.

The laser was still in his other hand, switched on, the dot lying on the wall beside the cabinet. As I watched it, the dot ran across the plaster by itself, or so it seemed, and lit on brass: a 4 and a B, beaded like drops on a window, and under them a beige door with a knob, standing in the corridor where it had stood since the twenty-third of October for everyone who had ever paid it any attention. He took one pace back. I saw him count. Down the row, Zofia put the tape back around her neck and said, to the sheet in her hands, "Is door." Nobody else spoke.

"Woo?" said the iPad. "You've frozen on my end."

He wrote with his back to the iPad, quickly, in a small hand. He showed me the page afterward, and I have it here, because he gave me the leaf.

> HT-4B. Corridor 22.00 ft (laser) / 31 paces (Mahajan, D.). Cabinet: hose, tag expired 03. Audible: female voice, wall, courteous. Door: present (visible on laser dot; brass 4B; knob). Door of record: DISPUTED. Status: UNDER REVIEW. W.C.

"I'm not hearing an audible," said Pugh, who had been told. "That's not a metric. Woo, let's not boil the ocean here. Is the unit validated or not?"

"No. Per section 4.3, a unit that can't be documented can't be signed off." He had his badge out on its reel again. "I can log it as a discrepancy, under review."

"Great. Whatever gets us to the twenty-fourth. Going forward, I need bandwidth on the actual deliverables. Great walk, everybody." The iPad went dark and Noreen let out a breath through her nose and closed the folio on her own reflection.

Woo came over to the 4A mat and spoke without looking at me, in the voice of a man reading from a binder. "I'm going to need that in writing. All of it. What you know about that wall. Dated, signed."

"I've been keeping a record. In a steno pad."

"That will do." He wrote something. "Under review is a hold, not an approval. It gets you until the twenty-first, when the integration runs. I can't make it not run. I'm an analyst." He turned the reel in his fingers. "I have filed three deviations in nine years. A boiler, a lease, and a sub-basement that one party stated did not exist. In each case I was thanked and nothing happened. This is my fourth."

"I count too," I said.

"I noticed."

The stairwell door opened.

Mr Grieve had come up from 6A on the stairs, which he was forbidden to do, with the tray in both hands and the cane hooked on his forearm: the cup, the saucer, the one Rich Tea. It was five to four. "Mr Grieve, you shouldn't be—" said Noreen.

"Strictly speaking, Mrs Whitmore, I live here." He crossed the diamonds, past the Fold Lounge, past a tenant who had stopped breathing, and set the plate down on the ledge of the west landing with the handle turned left. "Four o'clock, Mr Ambler," he said, and the cough took him.

It was the one I knew, a drawer being opened, but the drawer did not stop. It came out of the runners. He had the handkerchief up, and when he took it down there was a thin bright red on the white linen, in a shape like the shadow of a leaf. He looked at it with interest. Then he sat down on the carpet with his back against the wall under the window, with the cane across his knees, as a man sits at a station.

"Strictly speaking," said Mr Grieve, "I am not dying. I am sitting."

Chioma was there before I had moved. She had come out of 4A in the hoodie at the first cough and she was on her knees with a pulse oximeter off her pocket onto his finger. "Mr Grieve. It's Chioma. Look at me. Eighty-four. Mahajan, call it in." Mrs Han got down on the carpet beside him, which I had not known she could do, and said, "Good afternoon, Mr Grieve," and he said, "Quite so." Woo was holding the cane. He had picked it up without noticing, in both big hands, with the brass duck against his chest. "Is there a defibrillator?" he said.

"There's a lobby," said Noreen, which I took for a no.

The paramedics came up in Right with a stair chair, two women, brisk, who called him sir and Mr Grieve and let him correct the pronunciation of the second. Chioma went down with them with her hand on the arm of the chair. Rafa put the cane in my hand. I took the plate off the ledge, because I could not leave it, and then put it back.

* * *

I walked to Merrow General. It is six blocks and twelve minutes, and by the fourth block the umbrella had lost a spoke. I got there at five.

Chioma was in the Emergency hallway in her own scrubs, the green ones with the hospital's name on the pocket, her badge reading RESP THERAPY on its clip. "I'm not on until seven," she said. "I came in early and I'm using my badge to be rude to a registrar. Curtain seven." She walked me to it. "The oncologist says weeks. It was months. It's in the fluid now, and we're going to be careful about how it goes."

"Chioma. He's going to ask me for something I'm not sure is safe."

"For the record, I'm not a doctor of whatever that is." She pushed the frames up. "But I've sat with a lot of people at the end, and they all want one thing, and it's almost never the thing in the chart."

He was propped on two pillows behind the curtain with a cannula in his nose. The brown tweed waistcoat was unbuttoned and folded on the chair, with the tie on top, and the gold hunter watch lay open on the sheet, ticking. The hearing aids whistled on the tray where a nurse had put them. Without them he was smaller, and I saw how much of him had been the formality.

He put out his hand and took my wrist, with fingers like pencils.

"Mr Mahajan. Put my name down."

"Where?"

"The roll. The register. Whatever it is you people keep." His breathing made a sound like a page being turned. "Let me go in. I have been talking to the gentleman for thirty-six years, and I should like, before the end, to see what sugar he takes."

"Mr Grieve, I don't know what it would cost you. I don't know what the room—"

"I am eighty-four years old with fluid in my chest," he said. "Cost has lost some of its purchase." One eyebrow went up. "I am not asking to be rescued. I am asking to be introduced."

I looked at the watch on the sheet. It said 5:10. It was the only clock I had ever trusted in that building, and it was correct.

"I'll ask him," I said. "I'll ask the gentleman."

"Do," said Mr Grieve. "Strictly speaking, it is the gentleman's to say." He let go of my wrist, and closed his eyes, and the pale hand lay on the sheet beside the watch.
