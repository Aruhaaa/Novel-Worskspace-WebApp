The box arrived at 8:52 on Wednesday morning, when I clicked Publish to Data Room on the rent roll, which I had done sixteen times the week before as a rehearsal, and which had worked. It had a red border. The OK button did nothing. The Learn more button opened a help page on required fields in general, which began, *A required field is a field that is required.*

Behind it the queue stood where it had stood since go-live. HT-4B was at the top, in red. Behind it, in amber, were the 211 data-quality flags the sweep had raised on the sixth: trailing spaces, transposed digits, a lease that ended on a Sunday, a phone number with eleven digits and a hopeful look. They had been grayed out, like tickets in a queue where the front window has closed. I could edit them one at a time and each edit would save. I knew now what a save cost. I did not click on any of them.

At 9:20 I opened the Support chat, a small blue bubble with a headset, because I wanted a record that I had asked.

> Tenantry Support | Brent K., Tier 1 | Dev: HT-4B is blocking publish. The three fields are not in the legacy export. What do I enter? | Brent K.: Super question! Have you tried entering the missing data?

I asked which data. He answered that it was the data that was missing. I thanked him, and the bubble closed, and the headset went on smiling after it had gone.

At 9:58 Randall wrote, in capitals.

At noon I went up and counted the corridor. Twenty-four. I had been at twenty-one on Monday, and I had not counted on Tuesday, being underground, and I stood by the bicycle at 4C with the notebook open and wrote 24 beside 21 and looked at the two numbers as a man looks at a blood pressure. I did not knock on anything. On the way down I met Chioma at the mailboxes, signing for a box, in the green clogs and the red frames, and I said, because I had been rehearsing it, "I may be in the building late tonight."

"Define late."

"Technically after midnight."

She looked at me over the frames and said nothing, and it was a long nothing, with a lot of work in it. Then she picked up her box. On Monday, in the lobby, over the package locker, she had put her number into my phone with the air of a woman installing a smoke detector. I took the way she did not ask as a sign that she was an excellent neighbor.

At ten to five Noreen came out of her office with her coat over her arm and her bag on her shoulder. She stopped in the doorway of my side of the room, a thing she never does, with the keys held silent in her left hand, and she looked at the banner, at the backward letters of EMBRACE THE CHANGE, and not at me.

"Put something in, Dev."

"Which field?"

"Put a one." The gum had stopped. "Just put one."

"I'm not sure I can enter data I haven't verified. DOOR_COUNT is a count. I'd want to know—"

"It's a one." She said it with an odd flatness, a woman reading a number off a card. "The system takes a one. Randall gets his tick. It won't hurt anything." She had said that last in the tone you use with a child about a needle, with the exact amount of conviction the situation requires and not a gram over. Then, because she is Noreen, she said "Let's circle back," and went out into the lobby, and the glass door sighed behind her.

I sat for some time looking at the box. I tried the field. I typed a 0 into DOOR_COUNT, to see, and the cell went pink. *Enter a whole number from 1 to 9.* A unit may have one door or nine and may not have none. It is a reasonable rule, the sort I would have written myself. It had never before sounded like a sentence.

I considered the others. I had the permit number, on a half sheet from the Annex in my wallet, and I did not type it. It was the permit for a window. I know the difference between a field and a fact, and a woman in a lavender cardigan had told me, for nothing, that a number is only as true as the work it is attached to. The floor plan I could not have done at all: there was no sheet, and nothing in the county's cabinets drew a B. But DOOR_COUNT I could do honestly. There was a door. I had seen it on a video, and RentWise said so in a one with Margie's name on it. I would not be inventing a value. I would be carrying one forward, which is all a migration is. Technically, it was data entry.

I wrote that on the handout from the Lunch & Learn, and then I looked at it for a while and wrote beneath it, *Technically so is anything.*

At 9:12 that evening, in the office, with the lights off except mine and the rain ticking in the gutter outside the front glass, my phone lit.

> Chioma Eze, 9:12 PM: For the record I'm on shift till seven. Don't do anything stupid.

> Dev Mahajan, 9:13 PM: It's data.

> Chioma Eze, 9:13 PM: That's what stupid people say.

At 11:30 the glass door opened, and Rafa came in. He was in boots and his canvas coat, with a steel thermos in one hand and the brass key in the other, its green tag hanging from his fist. His face was a face you see at the back of a funeral.

"You came."

"I didn't say I would come."

"No."

"I didn't say I wouldn't." He set the thermos on Joyce's blotter, where nothing is ever set, and it left a ring. "Is the kind of night you bring coffee to."

We did not talk much. He sat in Espie's chair and turned it a quarter turn, back and forth, with his foot. I watched the clock in the corner of the screen. At 11:57 I put my cursor in the field. At 11:58 I typed a 1 with my right forefinger, the split thumbnail of my left hand pressed against the edge of the desk to steady it, and saved. The screen thought about it, and the banner stayed red, and a small line changed in the corner.

> HT-4B | Validation | 2 of 3 required fields remain.

Nothing happened in the room. Behind me Rafa took the toothpick out of his mouth, looked at it, and put it back.

* * *

We did not wait in the office. Rafa said that he was not going to climb four floors like a man going to a hanging, and that if it wanted us it would come, and if it did not we would have drunk the coffee. At five to two by the lobby clock, which meant one minute past, we sat on the bench by the mailboxes under the brass H with the thermos between us. He had poured the cap full. It tasted of cinnamon and the cap.

I watched the clock. At 2:01 by it the pipes began, low, from the basement, a note climbing through the risers behind the mailboxes. At 2:04 on the lobby clock, which is 2:10, the second hand reached the fifty-four and stayed there. My phone said 2:10 and then said 2:10 again. The radiator under the mailboxes knocked twice, and the doors of Left slid back by themselves, with the ceiling light on and nobody inside it. The display above read 4B.

Rafa stood up and put a hand flat on the frame of the car, as you touch a doorframe in a house where someone is ill. Then he stepped in. I went in after him. Neither of us touched the buttons. The doors closed, and the car rose through the numbers in no hurry, and it opened on four.

The corridor was a corridor. For about four seconds that was the whole of my report: the maroon carpet, the lozenges every three feet, the green clogs on their mat at the far west end, the blue bicycle at the far east. I walked to the mat and stood on it. I turned and faced the cabinet, with the notebook open in one hand and the pencil in the other, and I counted, and I stopped counting, because it was happening.

The red glass of the cabinet clouded as a window does when it is breathed on. The paint around it lifted. It did not peel. It lifted in one long clean line, the way laminate lifts at the edge of a lunchroom counter that has been wiped with the wrong cleaner for years, and under it the plaster was not plaster. It was a pale grain, like a cabinet door seen from very close. Three brass pins rose from the wall one after another at the left-hand edge, each turning a little as it came, like staples being drawn by a very slow remover. A molding drew itself around the lifted line with a sound like a thumbnail running down a comb. Two brass numerals sweated up through the paint at the height of a face, beading first, like drops on a cold glass, and then setting. A 4. A B, a little out of true.

Rafa crossed himself, forehead and chest and both shoulders, with his thumb, quickly, as a man does who has done it for years in places where he did not think it showed. "Mira," he said. It was all he said, and he did not say the rest.

I would like to report a sound. There was very little of one: the comb, the small ticks of the pins, a faint dry pressure in the ears, as when a door is opened somewhere else in a house. It was quieter than an elevator. It was quieter than the office printer warming up. I have thought since that a building does not hurry what it has done a hundred times, and I stood in it with my notebook open and wrote nothing, as a man stands in a church who does not know when to sit.

The glass cleared. Where the hose had been, in the frame the cabinet had been, there was a door, beige, with a knob. As far as I could tell, to anyone who had not spent two weeks knocking on it, it still looked like a cabinet.

The corridor had changed too. I did not notice when. I noticed it in the way that the east end, where the bicycle should have been five paces off, was further, as though someone had taken the corridor by both ends and drawn it out, like a tape from its case, and the carpet had been drawn with it. The diamonds went on. I walked it, with my lips moving, from the mat. Fourteen paces to the door. Seventeen more to the bicycle. Thirty-one. I turned and walked it back and got thirty-one. On the sixth of October the two directions had disagreed, and a disagreement is at least a kind of measurement. These agreed. I would have given a great deal for a disagreement.

I put my hand to my belt loop for something to hold, and found the ring lighter than it should be. I do not mean it hung wrong. The ring carries ten keys, in an order I could draw in the dark: office, filing room, mailroom, night drop, basement, roof, and four more. It carried nine now, evenly spaced, as if the tenth had never been cut. I went round them twice by touch. The one that was gone was the key to 3F.

I had turned that key twice a day for four years, going out and coming in, and I could not have told you what color its head was, and now I could not, and that was the answer. It was not use the building counted. As far as I could tell, it was looking.

Rafa had his hand over his mouth. At the height of my waist, in the door, a rectangle appeared that had not been there a minute before, cut without a saw, with a brass flap on it and the word LETTERS in capitals.

The flap lifted. A folded square of yellow paper came out of the slot, through the gap, and stopped, quivering a little, like something being held out. I took it. It was a Post-it. I unfolded it under the corridor light, in the round hand of an OCCUPANT card in a mailbox and a note in a green cabinet.

> You're late. M.
