# The Quiet Ones

A measuring tape came around my shoulders before the door of 3B had closed behind me, cool through my shirt, and a voice somewhere near my collarbone said, "Don't breathe."

I didn't. It is the kind of instruction you take from a person with a pincushion strapped to her wrist.

Zofia Baran is four feet eleven. She wore a man's gray cardigan with the sleeves turned back twice and a second tape around her neck as a spare, and when she spoke I could hear that two of her back teeth were missing, because her s's came out with a small whistle in them. She read the tape where it crossed my back, took it away, ran it from my neck to my cuff, and let the end snap into its case.

"You lost two centimeter," she said.

"Lost from what?"

"From you. Shoulder." She wrote in a small black notebook with a rubber band around it. "August last year you come in lobby with bag. I look. Now I look again. Forty-two, forty. In fourteen month."

"You measured me in the lobby?"

"I measure everybody in lobby. Is not rude. Is like weather."

Her front room was her shop. A dress form stood in the window wearing half a gray suit, a black machine with gold lettering sat on a table beside a lamp, and along one wall hung a rail of garments in dry-cleaner plastic, each with a tag pinned to the collar bearing a name in pencil. A radio on low was reading the news to itself in Polish. I had come about the contact verification. I held the page out and she took it and slid it, unread, under a bolt of cloth.

"Yes, yes. Later. Come."

Her kitchen was yellow and very small. She took a broom from behind the door and pointed its handle at the ceiling, where a brown stain the shape of a boot had been painted over twice and come back.

"Is lady," she said. "Four step, turn, four step. Many years. Evening, mostly. When I cook, when I sew."

"What lady?"

"Don't know. Heel is soft. Man heel is flat." She lowered the broom. "I ask Mrs Han. I ask super. All say, 'Is building.'" She lifted a shoulder, and the cardigan lifted with it. "Is not normal. Is building."

"What's above you?"

"Four-B." She said it the way another person would say it was raining. "You are clerk. You tell me."

"Technically there isn't a 4B."

"Technically." She pointed the broom handle at the stain again. "Then is technically very loud."

I laughed, and then I stopped, because she hadn't. She opened a drawer under the toaster and took out a spike with a stack of carbons on it, and drew off the third from the top. "I don't make nothing without write. Look."

> Z. BARAN, ALTERATIONS & MADE-TO-MEASURE | Invoice 0412 | 3B, 114 East 31st Street | 14 Sept 2020 | 1 pair curtains, lined, oatmeal wool-linen, 38 x 62, for Four-B | $140.00 | PAID CASH: N. Whitmore

I read the line for Four-B three times and the name once. Whitmore is not a rare name. I had known two people who carried it, and one of them wore fourteen keys on a lanyard.

"Curtains," I said.

"Lined. Is good cloth. Window I never see. She give me paper with numbers, thirty-eight by sixty-two, I make to number." Zofia took the invoice back and squared it on the spike. "Is not my window."

"My manager paid for curtains for a unit that doesn't exist."

"Curtains exist. I made them." She looked at me with satisfaction, as at a hem that had come out even. "Come back six o'clock. I make soup, she walks. You hear."

Oatmeal is the color of everything that is bought in a hurry. I thought of a button in a drawer and decided it was a color and not a clue, and I went up to five with the contact page still on its way to nobody.

* * *

Mrs Han opened the door of 5C before I could knock, which I would learn is her practice, and looked me over from my shoes upward. She is a very small woman who stands as though somebody told her to be taller and she took it as an order. She wore a quilted vest the green of a pine needle, a steel thermos in the crook of her left arm, and white hair in a bun pinned with a jade stick.

"Mr Mahajan. You are the office."

"Technically I'm the clerk. Dev."

"I know who you are. You muted my chat in your first week."

"How could you—"

"I am the administrator. I see who reads and I see who does not. You read in the evenings. Seven-forty, I think. Standing." She stepped back from the door. "Shoes."

Her apartment was spare and warm and smelled of roasted barley. A low table, a shelf of books with cloth spines, a framed photograph of a man in a short-sleeved shirt squinting beside a rowboat, and in front of it a tea light no bigger than a thimble. She poured from the thermos into its own lid and handed it to me with both hands, and set down a plate of rice cakes dusted in roasted bean powder, and sat across from me with her back not touching the chair.

"Eat."

I ate one. It was soft and a little sweet, and it stuck to my teeth.

"Owen asked on Tuesday who the quiet ones are," I said. "I've been wondering since."

"Everyone wonders. Only Owen asks." She folded her hands. "Since 2013 I say good morning to this building. Six o'clock, every morning. I write the quiet ones because it covers."

"Covers what?"

"Whoever is there." She said it as if reading out a reference number. "Some are shy. Some are in bed. Some I do not know. Nobody in my building is not-said-good-morning to."

I decided not to ask what had happened in 2013. It had the look of a date with a door behind it.

From the pocket of the vest she drew a tablet in a cover the color of a tangerine and held it where I could see. It was the group chat, the whole thread, a long column of her capitals. She touched a corner and a list came up.

I had muted that chat for fourteen months and never opened the member list, and had told myself the building held thirty-eight residents and an office. I counted again. Thirty-eight, the office, and one more at the very top, where the numerals sort ahead of the letters: 4B, with a gray circle where a face should be.

"I did not add that one," said Mrs Han.

"Then who—"

"Someone who wished to be added. I did not remove it." She turned the tablet back toward herself and drew her thumb across the row, as you would smooth a sleeve. "It has never written. It reads everything. It is the first to read, every morning. Six o'clock and three seconds. I have not yet put down the telephone."

"Mrs Han, there is a 4B. Technically. On the roll. But there's no—"

"Good," she said. "Then it should be greeted."

* * *

At five to four I was standing on the half landing of the west stairs between three and four, one hand on the rail, in the ordinary posture of a man waiting for nothing. I had told myself I was checking the fire door. The landing at the west end of four, past 4A, is a small square of marble tile with a window onto the alley and a sill as deep as a bookshelf. In fourteen months I had walked past it perhaps a hundred times and had never noticed that the sill was bare, until it was not.

He came down from six, one flight at a time, with a rest between each. I saw the shoes first, black oxfords, resoled, and the hems of brown tweed trousers, and then the cane, with a brass duck's head for a handle, held across his body like a rifle at ease. I knew him from the roll and the mailboxes. Ptolemy Grieve, 6A, tenant since 1974, whom I had seen every weekday at ten past nine collecting his mail in a three-piece suit, and to whom I had never been introduced. He had a long gray face like a well-kept letter, a pencil moustache, and large ears with a hearing aid in each that whistled when he turned his head.

He carried a white saucer in his left hand at the height of a waiter. On it were a cup, full and steaming, and one Rich Tea biscuit squared to the saucer's edge. He set it on the sill with a small click. He turned the cup until its handle pointed left.

"Four o'clock, Mr Ambler," he said, at the volume of a man announcing a train.

Then he coughed. It was a long cough with a drawer in it, a drawer being drawn out as far as it would go, and he took a folded handkerchief from his waistcoat and held it to his mouth until it was done, and looked at the handkerchief, and put it away.

"Stenographers have ears, Mr Mahajan," he said, without turning around.

I came up two steps with my hands where he could see them. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to be on your—"

"Tol-uh-mee," said Mr Grieve, to the window. "You have been reading it with the P. Everyone does. Saturday at four, I think. Six-A. Bring the question."

"Which question?"

"If you have to ask, you have several. Bring the better one." He took up his cane and went off along the corridor toward the elevators, tapping on the carpet, past 4A, past the cabinet, past 4C. At the cabinet he inclined his head to it, a small bow, the one you give a neighbor on the stairs, and went on.

The tea steamed on the sill. The cup's handle pointed left, as he had set it. It was 4:03. I had a 4:15 about tenant notices, and I am a man who keeps his appointments with computers, and I went down.

* * *

At a quarter past five I made a spreadsheet, and I am aware of how that sounds.

It had four columns: Unit, Ritual, Since, Who knows. In the first row I put 3B, a broom handle against a ceiling, a pair of curtains made to number. Then 4A, pencil on a wall (I typed the question mark), a radiator that asks. Then 5C, good morning at six, since 2013. Then 6A, a plate at four. The first three columns went in without argument. The fourth I could not fill. The answer was everyone, and also no one, since not one of the four had said any of it to another; Mrs Han knew nothing about the curtains, Zofia knew nothing about the plate, and the tenant in 4A had told a man holding a form to forget it. Four people in one building had each decided, separately and without comment, to be polite to a wall. I typed *Everyone* in the cell and deleted it and typed *No one* and deleted that.

Tenantry's dashboard has a line for occupancy, which had been climbing since I started, and Randall was fond of the arrow. It has columns for turnover and days vacant and average time to repair. It has the Notes field, two hundred fifty-five characters, and I tried it, to see, and managed Mr Grieve's plate before the characters ran out. Nothing in the software could say that people stay in a building when somebody says good morning to it, or that some of them had been saying it to a room.

At six I went down. A pot of beet soup was going on Zofia's burner, the red of something freshly cut, and she had a finger to her lips before I was through the door. She pointed at the ceiling with the ladle, and we stood in her small yellow kitchen like two people waiting for an elevator.

"Always four," she said, so low I read it off her mouth. "Since first time I hear. Four step, turn, four step. Today—"

"Shh."

I shushed. I heard the radio in the front room, and a bus, and the soup. Then the steps came. They were soft and a little uneven, with a short drag at the end of each, like slippers on a worn carpet: one, two, three, four, five.

Zofia's eyes came around to me. "Five," she said. "Is first time five."

I did not tell her about the corridor. I had a corridor that had gone from nine paces to fourteen to fifteen in four days, and a ceiling that had gone from four to five, and I know that a person who writes numbers down will see a shape in them whether there is one or not. As far as I could tell, the one thing that had changed in that building between Monday and Thursday was that someone had started to look. I wrote the numbers in the margin of the Lunch & Learn handout, where I write everything: 9, 14, 15, and beside them 4, 5.

"What you did?" said Zofia.

"Nothing."

"Mm." She measured me with her eyes, shoulder to shoulder, and wrote nothing down. "Is something."

Above us the steps went on. Five of them, a pause the length of a held note, the scuff of a turn, and five back.
