# The Missed Calls

The phone came back to life on Rafa's workbench, between a coffee can of washers and a bench vise, and for the better part of two minutes it would not stop. Every notification arrived with its small flat tick and slid down the cracked corner of the screen, a crack I had been meaning to see to since August, to make room for the next. I stood with half a bread roll in my hand and watched my sister's name go by again and again in a column, like a rent roll.

"Eat," said Rafa. "You look like a man who has read his own obituary."

He had walked me down to the shop with a hand under my elbow, the way you walk a person out of a church, and had put me on the stool and the charger in the wall and a plate in front of me: a bolillo from the bag he keeps for lunch, a wedge of white cheese in a paper napkin, one pickled jalapeño, a knife with a taped handle. I hadn't known I was hungry until the cheese. Then I ate all of it, standing, in six minutes, and he watched me with his arms folded and the toothpick going and said nothing about it.

On the pegboard behind him the forty-one hooks hung in their rows, the forty labeled keys and, at the end of the fourth row, one hook with nothing on it, marked 4B in his square capitals. He had taken the brass key from me at the elevator, with two fingers, and hung it back where it lived, and stood looking at it like a man returning a library book that he fears may be overdue.

"Three days," he said.

"I was inside an hour. A little over."

"Thursday I sit until seven. Nothing. I go do the boilers. I come at noon, nothing. Friday I come at six, at noon, at six. Yesterday I eat my lunch on the chair." He took the toothpick out and looked at it. "This morning I bring the paper, and I think, today he comes. Then you come." He put it back. "I did not know whether to wake you or call a priest. So I slept."

The ticking stopped at forty-one. The screen went still, like a street after a parade, and presented the thread.

> Anju, Thursday 8:14 PM: Dev. Call when you get this. Nothing wrong. Just call.

> Anju, Friday 6:02 AM: Dev. Dev. Dev.

> Chioma Eze, Friday 7:31 PM: For the record you have not texted. Neither has the wall.

> Anju, Saturday 3:07 AM: Call me or I'm calling the police.

Under the texts were the calls, which the phone, being fastidious, summarized as a number. Anjali does not leave voicemails. She says a voicemail is a letter to a stranger. I took the phone out to the loading dock at the back of the basement, the one place under the building that gets a signal, and sat on an upturned milk crate under the awning with the alley running with rain, and pressed her name.

She answered before it could ring. I heard the connection and then a breath, held.

"Where are you."

"Anju, I'm sorry, I—"

"Where. Are. You."

"Harwick. The basement. The dock, it's raining. I'm fine."

"You're fine." Her voice was shaking, and I understood that it was temper, because my sister shakes with temper the way a bridge shakes in a wind. It means the structure is sound. "Forty-one times, Dev. I counted. You'd like that."

"My phone died."

"For three days."

"We had a thing with the data room. A freeze. I was in the system, I lost track, there's a, it's an enormous—"

"I called your office on Thursday. A woman answered and said 'Noted.' I said, is my brother there, and she said 'Noted.' I said please, and she said 'Noted' and put me through to a recording of a man selling software. Nine times, Dev. I thought she was a bot."

"That's Joyce. She's in accounts."

"Then on Friday I got your manager. Noreen. She said you were at your desk, very sure, like a woman reading it off a card. I said put him on, and she said you had just stepped out. I said I'd hold. She said, 'Let's circle back,' and hung up on me. Politely. I have been hung up on by experts, and that was a master class."

I looked at the rain. Somewhere above me, I thought, in a file in a system, a woman had written that I was sick on Thursday and sick on Friday. Two stories, one for the system and one for my family, and neither of them true, and both of them useful.

"Noreen's always like that."

"She sounded frightened. I wasn't going to say that, I was going to be furious for ten more minutes. She sounded like someone doing a bad impression of a person who isn't frightened." A silence, with Tia's voice in the far part of it, asking something about a sock. "Have you seen someone?"

I thought of a wall, a woman in a cardigan, a plank of mugs. "I see people all day. It's an office."

"Dev." It was the occupational therapist, the voice that waits. "I mean someone you can tell."

I understood her then, and it was worse than the temper, because I understood what she had been counting. A brother whose mother died in January and who cannot be reached in October is not a scheduling problem. "It's work, Anju. It's only work."

"It's never only work with you. You can quit a job, you idiot." She breathed. "I'm calling every day now. Every day, at six, not just Sundays. If you don't pick up I'll call that woman and say 'Noted' to her until she cries."

I laughed, and she did, a little, and it didn't go all the way down for either of us. "Tia wants to know if you checked the total," she said.

"Tell her not yet."

"Okay. Okay." Then, quietly: "Say it."

"Sunday at six."

"It's Sunday. It's twenty past eleven." But she said, "Good," and hung up, and I sat on the crate in the noise of the alley with the phone held against my chest.

* * *

Rafa drove me to Tolliver Court in a white pickup with a ladder rack and a rosary on the mirror. I had said I would take the bus. "You don't have a key," he said, and I hadn't told him that. He had watched me pat my pockets at the elevator for the third time. He parked outside Building F, and we went up the thirty-four stairs and along the fourteen steps of the breezeway, and at 3F he got down on one knee on the wet concrete with a leather roll of wire and did it in under a minute, with his tongue between his teeth and the missing finger held out of the way.

"Is not a good lock," he said. "You should tell someone."

"I'm the someone. I mean it's mine to be concerned about."

The apartment was mine. That was the first thing, and I stood in the door and let it be true. The bed was made with the corners tucked, the one chair stood at the table, the tiffin sat by the stove with the photograph leaning on it, and the radiator under the kitchen window ticked as it warmed. The second mug was gone from the drainer, which I had known, and there was a clean space on the counter where it had been, as in a mouth.

What was different took me a minute, and it was the hall. The hall runs from the kitchen to the bathroom, seven steps, and on the left there was always a length of off-white wall with a stain the shape of Ohio at the baseboard. The stain was there. Beside it, where nothing had ever been, stood a door.

It was beige. It was a hollow-core door of the color of a hotel corridor, with a brass knob and no number on it, and it fitted its frame as if it had been put up with the building. I had not typed anything about Tolliver Court. I looked down the hall at the bathroom, and back at the door, and Rafa, in the entrance behind me, said "Mira," very softly, which is what he says instead of a prayer.

I thought of Chioma's wall. I thought of the key that had gone from my ring, and of the clerk's own life, which the building's rules seemed to follow wherever it went, and I turned the knob, which was cold, and opened the door.

Brick. It was a wall of brick, painted white, close enough to touch, and cold the way the inside of a cellar is cold, and the smell that came off it was toast. There was no room behind it and no corridor. I would call it the place a sentence runs out.

Two knocks came from the other side of the brick, at chest height, unhurried, like someone confirming a delivery.

This time I didn't wait. I put my knuckle to the paint and knocked twice, and two came back.

I closed the door. I had a theory, and I give it with the caveats it deserves. As far as I could tell, the building's reach stops at its own walls with one exception, which is the clerk. I am the extension cord. Wherever I go, a little of Harwick goes with me, and where the cord is plugged in at Tolliver, the brick is what the record looks like at its margin: a surface where the writing stops. I did not know who knocked. I did not want to know what a margin sounded like when it answered.

Rafa had taken off his cap. It is the only time I have seen him do it indoors. "Is not my building," he said.

"It might be mine."

"Then paint it," said Rafa, and put the cap on, and went down the stairs to his truck.

* * *

I took the 14 back, and Chioma was sitting on the mat outside 4A when the elevator opened, in the hospital hoodie, her clogs off and her bare feet on the maroon carpet and her phone in both hands. The red frames came up. She looked at me for a long time, like a nurse reading a face on a gurney.

"For the record," she said, "I have been knocking on that wall since Thursday night. Three nights. Nothing. In six years it has never not answered, Mahajan. Not on Christmas. Not the night my mother went in to hospital. I thought you'd killed her."

"I was inside."

"Inside what?" But she said it quickly, and turned her head a little down the corridor, and I looked too. The door stood in the wall exactly as I'd left it, beige, with its brass 4 and the B that was a little out of true. Fourteen paces from where she sat. "I saw it Thursday morning," she said. "Seven twenty, coming off shift. A door with a B on it between my wall and the bicycle. I walked past it like a woman who has decided something isn't there." She held up a hand. "Before you say anything, I've been very good at that for six years."

"I typed a one."

"That's what stupid people say."

"I know."

"I told you."

"I know. Her name's Margie. Margaret Dunleavy. She was the clerk before me. She's in there, there are ten of them, and I was inside an hour. It's been three days."

Chioma went still. She holds still the way she must at a bedside, when a number on a monitor has changed and she has not decided yet what it means. "Is she all right?"

"She's making tea."

"Okay." She let a breath out through her nose. "Okay. I'm going to be angry for one minute. It will be very loud inside my head and quiet out here, because I'm a professional." She was silent for ten seconds, and I counted them. "Three days. You didn't text."

"The phone—"

"You didn't text." She turned the phone over in her hands. "New rule. Eight in the morning and eight at night, you text me. Say your name."

"I know my name."

"Say it, Mahajan."

"That's ridiculous."

"In an ICU, when somebody is going under, you say their name. Out loud, to their face. Mr Alvarez, it's Chioma, you're at Merrow General. Nobody tells you why. You just do. It works, on people the monitor has already given up on." She tapped the glass. "Something in this building has been keeping a count of you since August of last year. I would like somebody else to be keeping one."

"A text isn't saying it."

"I know it isn't." She said it with an anger that had no object left. "I don't trust a text to count. So I'll say it too. At night, when I do my note. Rain tonight, Margie. Dev Mahajan. Out loud, to the plaster, like an idiot."

I got out my phone, whose screen had a crack across one corner and four percent of its battery, and typed it. *Dev Mahajan.* Her phone buzzed in her hand. She read it, and nodded once, and did not say it was good. She said, "Tonight at eight."

At six my sister called. I answered on the first ring, on the stairwell landing, and we talked for nine minutes about a loose tooth of Tia's, and not once about me, which I understood was a gift and which I took.

At twenty to eight I rode up to four a last time, and looked at the corridor. The door was there. I had half expected it to go when Chioma left for her shift, the way a reflection goes when you look away, but it was where I'd left it, with a faint line of lamplight under it that I told myself I was imagining. Fourteen paces. Seventeen more to the bicycle. I did not knock, and I did not try the knob.

I sent it from the lobby bench by the mailboxes, under the brass H, at eight by my phone and six minutes to by the clock. *Dev Mahajan.* I rode the 14 home with the rain sliding down the window beside me.

The answer was waiting when I let myself into 3F, which Rafa had left on the latch with a strip of masking tape across the bolt, by the light of the stove. I will say here that I did not look at the hall.

> Chioma Eze, 8:01 PM: Good. Sleep.

Under the kitchen window the radiator ticked and ticked, and then knocked twice, spaced as a person knocks who knows you are in.

I knocked back.
