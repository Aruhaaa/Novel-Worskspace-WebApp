# Too Much Dal

The church hall had folding tables, a serving hatch with a roller shutter, an urn of coffee the size of a fire hydrant, and thirty-eight residents of Harwick Terrace in their coats. I knew the figure because Mrs Han had made me count them at the door, so she could mark them present. There were more than thirty-eight, in fact. There was Woo-jin Cho of Larchmont, at the back by the coat rack, in a black tie, standing.

Mr Grieve had died on Wednesday morning and was being buried on Thursday for two reasons. The first was that in 1994 he had prepared a binder. It lived on the shelf in 6A between the steno bundles for February and March, with tabs from A to K, and it was titled PTOLEMY GRIEVE: ARRANGEMENTS (REV. 1994; AMENDED 2009; AMENDED AGAIN IN OCTOBER). The second was that Mr Danziger of Danziger & Sons had had a cancellation, which he reported to Noreen without any visible shame. The binder specified the hymn, the hall, the sandwiches, and the form of service, and Chioma had the typed sheet.

> ORDER OF SERVICE: Hymn (one verse; I have made my views known). A reading of the morning greeting by Mrs Han. Remarks, not to exceed four minutes each, timed by Miss Eze with the watch. Tea. Strictly no flowers.

We sang one verse. Mrs Han climbed onto a step stool that she had brought in her bag, in the quilted vest and the jade pin, and read in her capitals, in a voice that could have taken the roof off, "GOOD MORNING TO ALL, INCLUDING THE QUIET ONES." Then she looked at the closed box at the front of the hall and said, in her ordinary voice, "Good morning, Mr Grieve."

Rafa spoke first, in the suit he had borrowed from a brother. "Mira," he said. "Mr Grieve was a man who knew what sugar a person took before he asked. I fixed his tap in 2011. He gave me tea and he said, strictly speaking, the leak was in the washer and not the tap, and he was right, and I have been thinking about that for fourteen years—"

"Time," said Chioma, holding the gold hunter watch open in her palm.

"I haven't said it yet."

"Four minutes."

"I was at two."

"You were at four, Rafa. He'd have stopped you at three."

Rafa sat. The hall laughed, the kind of laughter that has crying behind it like a second room.

The sandwiches came from Tab F: egg salad, in triangles, with the crusts off, crusts being, according to the binder, a form of aggression. Zofia ate three and measured the length of the buffet table with her eyes. Owen Fenske, in a tie I had never seen, sang the one verse a half-beat behind everybody and a third flat, with total conviction. Anjali sat beside me on a folding chair with her coat folded over her knee, and when Mrs Han said *Good morning, Mr Grieve* she put her hand on mine on the seat between us and left it there.

Afterward, at the urn, Noreen tapped a plastic stirrer on her cup. She had the lanyard around her neck and both hands on the cup. "I want you to hear this from me and not from a memo," she said. "If the Larchmont sale closes on Monday, I won't be staying on. I have thirty-one months to my pension. I've decided I would like to spend them somewhere else." Nobody said anything. Mrs Han inclined her head, the way a general does when told of the loss of a position. Noreen added, to the urn, "I'm sorry. I should have said a lot of things a lot sooner."

I went to the back. "You didn't have to come."

"The binder has a dress code." Woo had a hand at his badge reel. "Black or, failing black, brown. Anyone who has been in the building in an official capacity wears a black tie." He looked at the box. "I counted that as me. I read the footnotes. Footnote nine concerns the Larchmont person, whoever he may be, and states that he is to be given tea."

"Did you have some?"

"I am waiting to be asked." So I asked, and he had two cups, with sugar, and wrote nothing down.

* * *

At noon I went up to the roof of Harwick by the steel door that sticks. It was between the church hall and the cab, and I had forty minutes. The tar paper was wet, and a pigeon sat on a vent, and the water tank stood on its stilts. From the parapet you could see the beltway and the roof of Halloran's and the bus shelter on 31st Street, small and bright, and on the other side of the parapet the fourth floor, bay 2, the infill of lighter brick, from above.

I sat on an upturned bucket. The pigeon watched me out of one orange eye and did not leave. From the pocket of the raincoat I took Mr Grieve's dictation recorder. It was the size of a bar of soap, and he had left it to me with the steno pads, and it still had the cord wound round it. I unwound the cord. From the other pocket I took the microcassette with my block capitals on the label. I do not know how I knew it would fit. It fit. It went in with a small, definite click, like a key.

I pressed Play with my thumb, the split one.

There was a hiss, the sound of a small room somewhere with the door open. Then my mother.

> Dev, beta, it's Mummy. I made too much dal, so, if you're passing, no, never mind, it's the wrong day, Anjali says you're busy Thursdays. Call me when you can. No rush. Okay. Bye bye bye.

That is all of it. I have copied it here exactly, and I have not corrected her. It was twenty-three seconds. She was slow, with the long pauses of a woman on a great deal of morphine, and she was pleased, in the way she was pleased when she had made a pot of something for a boy who might be passing. There was no dal. I knew that, I think I knew it on the roof as a fact, my mother had not stood at a stove since Thanksgiving, and the kitchen had belonged to the aunties for weeks. She had made it in her mind. It was a Thursday in my childhood and I was a person who might drop in.

I had been afraid for twenty-two months of a sentence. It was not in the tape. There was nothing in it that I could have answered and failed to answer. There was a request that I call, when I could, with no rush.

I listened to it nine times. I counted, naturally. The fifth time I heard *Anjali says you're busy Thursdays*, and understood that my sister had told our mother where I would be, and had told her kindly, and had never mentioned it to me. On the ninth time I did not listen to the words at all. I listened to the last few seconds after *bye bye bye* and before the click, in which, as far as I could tell, she was still holding the phone.

* * *

The curb at Departures was wet and loud with taxis. Anjali's flight was the 6:15 and we were two hours early, because she cannot be late, and because Tia's pageant was on Friday morning. "She's a sweet potato," said my sister, getting the suitcase out of the cab. "She has one line. 'I am a sweet potato.' She's been practicing for two weeks and it's gone to a whisper and Ben says, Mommy has to be there to hear it."

She took the coat off the cab seat, the navy one, and did not put it on. She held it. She put the suitcase upright.

"Tell Chioma she's to take her glasses off when she sleeps. She wakes up with a mark on her nose, I saw it."

"I'll tell her."

"And tell Margie I'll be back for the radiator. Don't say it like a promise. Say it like a work order."

"Technically that's what a work order is."

She looked at me, and then she did what she has done at train stations since I was seven, and took my face in both hands, and pulled it down, because she has to reach.

"Sunday at six."

"Six."

"I mean it. If you don't pick up I'll get on a plane and sit in your lobby, and I'll bring Tia, and she'll tell everyone you're a spreadsheet."

"Technically—"

"Don't." From the tote on her shoulder she took the blue-lidded container, in its bath towel. "I made it Tuesday night in Chioma's pan. It's too much. I know it's too much. Don't microwave it on high. A splash of water, medium." She put it in my arms. "I'm not flying with it."

I stood with the dal against my chest. I opened my mouth. I had the sentence; it had three clauses and her name in the second one. She watched my face with her head a little on one side, as she watches a child about to say the thing it has been rehearsing.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"Where you start a sentence and end up in a drawer."

"I'm fine."

"Okay," said my sister. "Okay. Say it back."

"Sunday at six, Anju."

She kissed me on the cheek, hard, and went in. The doors opened for her and closed and opened for the next person and closed. I counted them, because I count. Eleven. A man in a high-vis vest came from the next lane with a whistle in his hand and asked whether I was all right, and I said that technically I was parked in a loading zone, and he said that wasn't what he'd asked.

* * *

Noreen had given me the key to 6A in an envelope after the hall. *6A. Temporary. Lease to follow. N.* I sat at Mr Grieve's desk at ten to nine under six clocks that said eight forty-four, eight forty-seven, and four others. The recorder was in front of me with the cord wound round it again, and the stenotype slept under its dust cloth. I had not turned on a lamp. At eight I had said my name aloud to the six clocks, as Chioma requires, and typed it as well, and she had sent back from the break room at Merrow General *Good. Sleep.*, which was not an instruction I intended to follow.

At 8:51 by my own phone, which was right, the screen lit on the blotter. ANJU. I looked at it.

I want it on the record that I let it ring. I want it noted that I knew, exactly as I had known in January, what I was doing. In January I let it ring because I was in the middle of a question. On the twentieth of November I let it ring because I was in the middle of an answer, and if I had heard her voice I would have given it to her. It rang eleven times and stopped. Then, after a minute, the phone buzzed once and a small red number appeared.

My sister has never left a voicemail in her life. She calls back. She says leaving them is for people who have time.

> Landed. Tia's asleep on Ben. You are an idiot and I love you. Call me Sunday at six or I will fly back and sit in your lobby. Okay. Okay. Bye.

I listened to all of it. It was the first voicemail I had played on the night it was left since the eleventh of January last year. At the end a woman's voice said that I might save the message by pressing nine, and I pressed nine.

I had known since Saturday what the last field would ask. I understood that sitting there. I had not been putting off the entry. I had been putting off the answer.

I took the tape out of the recorder and put it in the pocket of the raincoat, with the collar that had my name in it. I went down six flights by the stairs and unlocked the office. The night-drop safe ticked. The blinds were down and the vision board was dark. I put the tape in the sticking drawer with the lemon-drop tin and the horn button, and the drawer stuck, and gave. I do not know what the building would have done with it. I know where I wanted it.

Then I sat down in the chair that fits my spine because it fit hers, and turned on the monitor, and logged in as the clerk. HT-4B came up red at the bottom of the roll, where it had been since the sixth of October, and I put my hands on the keys.
