# Delete This Row

Eleven umbrellas stood furled in the aisle of the 14 bus on Tuesday morning, held upright between knees, and by the third stop every one of them was steaming. The bus smelled of wet cardboard and somebody's egg sandwich. When it braked on the slick road the umbrellas swung together like a committee, then swung back, dripping on the loafers of a man who watched them do it and did not move his feet.

I had the sideways seat behind the driver, my bag on my knees. The 14 takes twenty-two minutes from Kessler Avenue to the stop at 31st and Merrow. From the gate of Tolliver Court to the shelter on Kessler is twenty-two steps, which I counted in my first week there and have not stopped counting since. I have never found a use for the coincidence. I have also never found a way to stop noticing it.

That month my whole life would have fit between two stops. A studio at Tolliver Court, 3F, rent $1,125, with a window onto the dumpster enclosure. Two mugs, because the set came as two. A steel tiffin that was my mother's, three tiers and a dent in the bottom clip, which holds rubber bands and AA batteries now. A job. It had edges you could see from the street, and I liked that, and I made a note that I liked it, and did not look at the note.

Between the third stop and the fourth the bus goes past the Gaslight, which puts a chalkboard on the sidewalk on Thursdays and, for reasons of its own, leaves it out on Tuesdays. For that block I read the advertisement over the opposite window, for a personal-injury lawyer with enormous hands. I know it well.

At the next stop someone at the back pulled the cord twice, as people do, and two chimes sounded over my head, and I found I was looking at my own hands. It was not my stop. Mine was the one after. I got off there, stepped across a gutter running the full width of the curb, and walked the last block with my umbrella held out in front of me like a man delivering it.

* * *

Noreen's door was open when I came in at 8:31, which on a Tuesday means a conversation, and her keys were not chiming. She was standing behind her desk with the lanyard gathered in both hands, all fourteen keys in her fists so that none of them could speak.

"Close it, hon," she said.

I closed the door and apologized to it. She let that go.

Her office is at the back of the suite, past the filing room, and holds one desk, one visitor's chair and a plant that has outlasted two regional directors. Above the filing cabinet hangs a wooden sign in white script, LIVE LAUGH LEASE, which came from a conference along with the lanyard. Beside it is the vision board: a cork square carrying a lake with a dock, a camper trailer, a woman in a sun hat who looks as if she has never been asked a question, the word EXHALE in stick-on letters, and a yellow Post-it that said 31 MO in Noreen's round hand.

"Sit." She sat, and the keys came down into her lap in a heap. "Gum? No, you don't. I'm on my ninth and it isn't nine o'clock."

She slid a sheet of paper across the blotter. It had been printed, which told me more than the words did. Noreen reads everything on a screen and prints only what she means to hold on to. A second Post-it was already stuck to the corner, with 4B on it, underlined once.

> From: Randall Pugh | To: Noreen Whitmore | Date: Tue 7 Oct, 7:12 AM | Subject: PHANTOM UNITS: ACTION REQUIRED BY 10/31

> Noreen, following Monday's validation sweep the regional dashboard shows one non-validating line at Harwick Terrace (HT-4B). Going forward we cannot carry phantom units into the Larchmont data room. Please have the line removed ahead of the 10/31 freeze and confirm back to me. I know everyone is at capacity, so I appreciate your bandwidth. Let's not boil the ocean on this one. Best, Randall

I read it the way I read a lease, for what it leaves out. It left out whether anyone had asked what the line was.

"He gets the sweep on a dashboard," Noreen said. "Nobody can turn it off. I asked."

"Technically it's a data quality issue."

"It's a migration ghost, hon. They happen. You heard Brent."

"Brent said it's super common."

"Brent is right about one thing a quarter and I take it where I find it." She tapped the Post-it with a fingernail. "So you go to your desk, you click delete, you stick this on your monitor so you remember you did it, and Randall gets his green. Done by lunch."

"It has a payment history. One payment a month, back to the import in 2003. It has never been late."

"It doesn't exist, hon." She said it kindly, the way you tell a child what the vet said about the goldfish. "That's the whole point."

"Whose point?"

"Everybody's. Randall's, Larchmont's, mine. We close clean, nobody's phone rings, I retire in thirty-one months, and you get a very nice letter. Let's put it in a drawer."

"Is the delete button a drawer?"

"A drawer with a good lock." The gum went round once. "You took a long lunch yesterday."

"I took it upstairs."

The gum stopped. "Upstairs."

"With a ham on rye. I didn't eat it."

"Okay," said Noreen. "Okay. Okay." The three okays were so polite that I looked at her hands, and found they had gone back to the lanyard without my seeing it, the way you find your own hand on a railing.

For no reason I could have given, I thought of my interview. It was August of the year before, in the same visitor's chair, with fewer cutouts on the board. She had taken me through the form: strengths, a time I had found an error, whether I minded working alone. I told her about a county that had scanned a hundred and four leases upside down. I did not mind working alone. Then she turned the form face down and asked the one question that was not on it. "Last thing, hon. If something happened and we needed to call someone about you, who would we call?"

"My sister," I had said. "I guess."

She wrote something in the margin of her copy and told me I would start on the nineteenth. I took it for a phone number, though she never asked me for the number.

"No worries," I said now, which is what I say. "I'll take a look at it."

"Take a look," said Noreen, "and then take it out."

* * *

The delete button is where Brent said it would be, top right of the unit screen, small and gray, smaller than the one for Print. I sat down. The chair took my spine the way it always does. I clicked.

> Delete unit HT-4B and its payment history? This action cannot be undone. Type the unit number to confirm. | (field) | CANCEL | DELETE (disabled)

I typed 4. I typed B. The DELETE button, which had been gray, turned red with a small click of its own, like a person who has been waiting by the door with a coat on. Then I did the thing I am best at, which is nothing. The cursor blinked in the field. I took my hands off the keys and put them on my knees.

"Coffee," said Joyce. She set a mug to the left of my keyboard on a square of paper towel folded in four, which is where she puts them, and went back to her cubicle with her cardigan sleeves pushed to the elbow.

"Thank you. No worries."

"Noted," said Joyce, and it came out warm. I had no idea why I'd said no worries. She had only brought coffee.

I should say that I archive. I have worked with records for seven years and have never once used the delete key on one. A tenant who moves out is marked inactive. A lease that has been replaced goes to a folder called Superseded, with the date in front of its name. Espie says this is giving hoarder, and my answer, that you can always throw a thing out tomorrow and you can never throw it back, has not persuaded her. That was the reason I would have offered Noreen.

There was another, of the kind that belongs on the record once and should then be left alone. In the year before my mother was diagnosed there was a sound in her chest, a wet catch at the end of each breath at night, and my father heard it from the pillow beside hers. A doctor in Edison and then a second doctor told us it was nothing. They said so for months, and kindly. Nobody in the family stopped hearing it. We stopped saying we heard it, which is not the same thing, and the sound was exactly what it was the whole time.

I pressed CANCEL. I printed the row to a PDF, HT-4B_Oct07, and dropped it in the Archive folder, which holds a little over two hundred files and no deletions. Then I sat looking at the monitor, where the row was still red and still there, and said nothing to Joyce, who had not asked.

* * *

At half past two I told myself I was reconciling keys.

The stairs to the basement are behind the mailroom door, concrete, painted gray so many times that the edges have gone soft. The boiler lives at one end and the tenant storage cages at the other, and in between, past the laundry room and a corridor of cloth-wrapped pipes, is Rafa's shop. It is the size of a walk-in closet. A workbench, a vise, coffee cans of screws sorted by length, a radio turned low to a station playing a song about a horse. On the pegboard above the bench hangs the key board. I had seen it once, on my first day, when Rafa gave me the tour and I was busy memorizing the exits.

Rafa Ibarra was at the bench with a toothpick in the corner of his mouth and his reading glasses, mended at the hinge with white tape, down at the end of his nose. He is broad in the forearm and slow in everything, and his left ring finger stops at the first knuckle. He held a small brass key in that hand and a rag in the other, and he was drawing the rag along the key from bow to tip, once, slowly, the way a man does a shoe on a Sunday.

"Mira," he said, not turning around, "somebody came down from four fast yesterday."

"I walked."

"The door on the one landing has a closer that complains. Everybody who comes down from four fast, I hear it."

"I wasn't fast. I was at a normal pace."

"From four, normal is also fast." He laid the key on the rag. "You want something?"

"I'm doing a key reconciliation."

"A what."

"Every row on the roll should have a key on the board. Technically I'm counting."

He lifted one shoulder, which in Rafa is a whole speech, and I turned to the board. It is white pegboard with the hooks in rows and a unit number stenciled under each. I counted twice, as I do. Forty-one hooks. Forty of them held a nickel key on a white fiber tag with the unit typed on it. The forty-first, stenciled 4B, was empty, and on the pegboard beneath it was a small dark ring of oil, like the mark a glass leaves on a table.

"What's that one for?" I said, nodding at the key on his rag. The tag was green cardboard, and it had no number on it.

"It's for the hook."

"A key isn't for a hook. A hook is for a key."

"So. Then you know."

"Rafa."

"Depends who's asking."

"I'm asking. I'm the clerk."

"Then it depends on the clerk." He looked at me over the tape on his glasses with great sweetness. "You ate?"

"Technically I had a coffee."

"Coffee is not eating. Coffee is a rumor." He dug in the pocket of his tool belt, past the flashlight he never uses, and came up with a clementine. He put his thumbnail into it, and the smell filled the shop at once, bright and cold, and he handed me half. "Eat. You're thin for a man who counts keys."

I ate a segment. I said, "Is it a spare for something?"

"Depends—"

"Who's asking. I know. I'm asking."

"Have we established that?" said Rafa, gently, and he hung the brass key on the forty-first hook, in the middle of its ring of oil, where it swung once and was still. The radio finished with the horse. I stood there with a segment of clementine in my hand and a number in my head. Forty-one hooks, forty-one keys, forty doors. The count was right for the roll and wrong for the building, and the man who could have told me why was peeling the other half of a clementine for himself.

* * *

I got home at ten to seven with wet cuffs. Dinner was a pouch of rice, ninety seconds in the microwave, eaten standing at the counter beside my mother's tiffin so as not to make a plate. Both my mugs stood on the drainer. One of them had been used that morning.

The Harwick tenant chat has thirty-eight residents in it, and the office, which is in it the way a smoke detector is in a household. Mrs Han in 5C administers it. I had muted it in my first week and I read it in the evenings, the way you read the date on a carton. This is what the morning had held.

> Sook-ja Han (5C), 6:00 AM: GOOD MORNING TO ALL, INCLUDING THE QUIET ONES.

> Owen Fenske (2F), 7:48 AM: genuine question. who are the quiet ones. I have lived here two years and it is not the people above me, they are NOT quiet. also the 2F dryer ate a sock

> Chioma Eze (4A), 7:49 AM: Owen, leave it.

> Sook-ja Han (5C), 7:52 AM: GOOD MORNING OWEN.

> Owen Fenske (2F), 7:52 AM: ok good morning

I had read Mrs Han's sentence every morning for fourteen months and filed it under courtesy, a thing said to the hard of hearing and the shy. I read Chioma's line three times. She works nights at Merrow General, so 7:49 would have been her coming in from the walk, and I knew her from a lease and a pair of green clogs on a mat. Nobody puts a full stop on a chat message unless they mean to have the last word. I wanted to know what Owen was supposed to leave. I put the phone face down beside the tiffin and finished the rice.

At 10:40 I did what I had been not doing all day, which was open the laptop. I am a man who writes his reasons down. If I was going to leave a red row on a regional director's dashboard after my manager had asked me, with a Post-it, to remove it, I wanted a reason on file with a date next to it, and a date needs a source. In Tenantry the source is the audit trail, a tab on every row that keeps each change, who made it and when. Tenantry does not care where you log in from; that is all the brochure meant by work anywhere. If someone had touched HT-4B during the migration over the weekend, I could say so in a sentence and be done. The row would have had a parent.

HT-4B came up red. I clicked the tab.

> HT-4B | Audit trail | Record created: migrated | Last modified: 10/06 23:58 | Modified by: DMAHAJAN

Under it, in blue, was a link: Show full history. I looked at the link for some time. I had not edited HT-4B on Monday. I had opened it, and looked at it, and closed it, and looking does not leave a line. And at 23:58 on Monday I had not been at any login at all.

I did not click. I closed the laptop the way you close a drawer on something that is not yet yours to explain, washed the mug I had used, and went to bed.

At 23:58 on the sixth I had been asleep. I am always asleep by eleven.
