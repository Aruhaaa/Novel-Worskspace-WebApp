# Good Morning, Dev

A foil tray of sticky rice was steaming on the lobby bench when I came back from the office with the laptop, and for a moment I took it for something the building had left there itself. Then Mrs Han lifted the corner of the foil with one thumbnail and the smell came out: sesame oil, brown sugar, and a note like a cough drop that I would learn was a jujube. The rice was the color of strong tea and packed as tight as a brick, with chestnuts in it, and pine nuts, and dark dates cut in half. She had carried it down from 5C in the arm that did not hold the thermos. She set it beside me on the bench the way you set a casserole on a step where there has been a death.

"Eat," she said.

"Thank you, Mrs Han. I'm not really—"

"A person cannot be said good morning to on an empty stomach." She put a plastic spoon in my hand and folded my fingers around it. She had put the quilted vest on over a church blouse, and the jade pin was in her bun, and she gave me the whole of her attention, which in a woman four feet ten inches tall is a great deal to stand under.

I ate. It was sweet and chewy and scorched a little at the edges, and I remember thinking that a man who was about to be filed should not have been able to taste anything, and I could taste all of it.

I had read the notices at seven on the office laptop, which was by then the only machine that would still let me into the building chat.

> Chioma Eze, 4A, 7:12 PM: ALL TENANTS TO THE LOBBY AT 9 PM TONIGHT. THE CLERK. DEV MAHAJAN. OFFICE GUY, WALL GUY. HE IS BEING LOST AND I AM NOT GOING TO WATCH IT. BRING A CHAIR. YOU WILL SAY HIS WHOLE NAME OUT LOUD, TO HIS FACE. NOT HERE. THIS IS NOT A THREAD.

> Sook-ja Han, 5C, 7:31 PM: GOOD EVENING TO ALL. THIS IS A SECOND NOTICE. MS EZE IS NOT POLITE BUT SHE IS CORRECT. THE LOBBY, 9 PM. THOSE WITH KNEES MAY SIT. I WILL BRING FOOD. NO ONE IS TO WRITE THAT THEY DO NOT KNOW WHO HE IS. YOU DO NOT KNOW. COME ANYWAY.

By a quarter past nine there were nineteen steel chairs in a ring around the bench, HARWICK TERRACE in white paint on every back, and Rafa was still bringing them up from the cage in the basement two at a time with the toothpick going in the corner of his mouth. The bench stood in the middle of the ring like the single item on an agenda. I sat on it. Chioma stood in the gap between two chairs with the red frames pushed up into her twists and the marker in her fist, and gave the rules.

"One. Out loud. Not typed, not texted, not thought about really hard." She held up a finger for each. "Two. The whole name. Not 'hey,' not 'him,' not 'the guy.' Dev Mahajan. Three. You look at him when you say it. Not at his shoes. Not at the rice. In the unit we say the name to the patient, never to the chart, and I don't have a better theory than that."

"Is it a theory?" I asked.

"It's a Friday," said Chioma. "I watched a text do nothing for four days. Sit up."

The rules had not been discovered. They had been improvised, the way you set a ventilator, from what the patient was doing. As far as I could tell the building listened, and it did not listen to a screen. It listened to a mouth pointed at a face.

They came in coats over pajamas. Owen Fenske arrived at twenty past with his headphones around his neck. He took in the chairs, the foil tray, the laptop and Chioma, and said, "Is this a cult?"

"It's a roll call," said Chioma. "Look at the bench."

He looked at the bench. I watched it happen. His eyes went to the rice, to the spoon, to the laptop and back, and they did not stop on me, in the way that eyes pass a coat on a hook. "I see a bench," he said.

"Say it anyway."

Owen sighed through his nose, the sigh of a man who has filed complaints about the elevator and been invited to a meeting. He said, to a spot about a foot to my left, "Dev Mahajan." And his eyes arrived. They moved, and they stopped, and he took a small step back from the shock of it.

"Oh," he said. "You're the space guy."

"Technically I'm the clerk."

"You took the space off the end of my name."

"I did."

"I thought that was the Department." He sat down in the nearest chair. He did not say another word for three hours, and he did not leave.

Mr Grieve came down at ten in Right, one hand on the brass duck, his hearing aids whistling at the acoustics of the lobby, in the full brown tweed with the gold hunter watch across his waistcoat. He did not take a chair. He crossed the terrazzo over the worn brass H and stopped in front of the bench, as he must have stopped in front of a great many people who did not want to be on the record.

"State your name for the record," he said.

"Dev Mahajan."

"Strike that," said Mr Grieve, "and say it as if you meant it."

Until then I had said my name in the voice I use to order a sandwich. I tried again and it came out apologetic, as if I were giving my name to a drawer. "Again," he said. The third time I said it the way I would have said it to my mother, to explain who had telephoned, and I thought the pipes in the wall behind the mailboxes ticked once.

"Quite so," said Mr Grieve. He coughed into his handkerchief, a drawer being opened, and sat down at the end of the bench, and took out the watch. "I shall time it."

Zofia sat on my other side with my raincoat across her knees, the collar turned out under the orange lights, a needle in her mouth and red thread pulled from the pincushion on her wrist. She was sewing in capitals, one stitch at a time. DEV MAHAJAN, inside the collar, where it would lie against the back of my neck.

"What is it for?" I asked.

"Is not spell," said Zofia, around the needle. "Is thread." She bit the end off. "My mother sewed names in all coats. For school. Coat gets lost, child gets found." She turned the collar down and smoothed it flat and put the coat around my shoulders, and I could feel the letters through the lining, a small ridge against the neck. "Is not for building. Is for you."

* * *

Espie came at half past ten with the ring light in her tote and a tripod under one arm and her nails painted a plum so dark it was nearly black. "Ok so I'm going live," she said.

Chioma started to say something.

"No, because hear me out. I have eight thousand people who watched a door that isn't there. They love him. They've never met him and they love him." She set the tripod up by the dead doorman's desk, among the plastic spiders nobody had taken down, and propped the laptop on the foil lid, and showed us that she could bring a viewer up on the screen one at a time. "But typing doesn't count, right? Chioma says it doesn't. So I'm telling them. If you want to count, you have to come on and look at the lens. The lens is his face."

By eleven there were two thousand and some people in the comments, going up the side of the screen faster than I could read. DEV. dev!!! who is dev. is this the door guy. DEV. I felt none of it. A sentence typed into a box is a sentence in a box.

The ones who came on were different. A night nurse in a break room, in a scrub cap printed with pickles, looked into the camera and said "Dev Mahajan," and then "I have a patient," and was gone. A boy in a gaming chair did it as a joke, and Espie said, "Again, like you mean it," and he did, and went quiet. A man in a parked car with the dome light on said it very slowly and added, "Hope you're okay, man." I said thank you to each of them, to the lens, which felt absurd and was right. The ones who did it properly I felt in my hands, like blood coming back into the fingers after you have come in from outside.

Chioma kept the count on a legal pad on her knee, in tally marks, with a stroke through each five like a gate. At half past twelve she held it up.

"Sixty-one."

"Sixty-one," I said. "Which is a prime number."

"Mahajan," said Chioma.

"Sorry."

At twenty to one the glass doors opened, and the keys came across the terrazzo before she did. Noreen had on a quilted coat over a sweatshirt, the burgundy hair flat on one side as if she had been lying on it, and under her arm the lid of a banker's box. She stopped inside the door and looked at the chairs and the laptop with its small lit rows of strangers. She did not look like a person arriving at a gathering. She looked like a person arriving at a hearing.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Okay."

Nobody answered. Rafa took the toothpick out of his mouth.

"I don't know you, hon," she said to me, over the heads. She did not say it unkindly. She said it with the careful politeness of someone reading out a statement. "I want you to know that I know I don't. I was at my kitchen table looking at the roll, the way I do, and there was a name on a line where it shouldn't have been, and I knew what that line was. And I didn't know who you were. I haven't had that feeling since January of 2016." Her jaw worked on the gum. "I asked a man a question once, in an interview. I don't remember the man. I remember the question."

"Who would we call," I said.

"Yes." Her eyes came to me and stopped, and stayed, and I am not going to describe what went by in them. "I wrote it down."

Under the lid was a manila folder gone soft at the corners. She had dug it out of Margie's filing room, she said, in the dark, after four boxes. "It was the fourth. It's always the fourth." She put the form in my hand.

> CORVANE PROPERTY GROUP | EMERGENCY CONTACT | Employee: MAHAJAN, DEVENDRA | Contact: Kowalczyk, Anjali Mahajan | Phone: 919-555-0147 | Relationship: sister (guess)

The name and the number were in my own hand, small capitals, the sevens crossed. The word under Relationship was in Noreen's round purple gel pen, and the second word had been put in brackets and underlined twice.

She took out her phone and looked at Chioma. "You do it," said Chioma. "It has to be you. You're the one who asked."

It rang out. She tried again and it rang out, and in the ring of chairs nobody moved. On the third try the screen jumped, and a kitchen came up on it, sideways and then level.

It was my sister's, in Raleigh, with the light over the stove on and the corner of one of our mother's recipe cards held to the refrigerator by a magnet shaped like a tomato. Anjali stood in it in Ben's old college sweatshirt with the claw clip hanging by three teeth out of her hair and a glass of water in her hand. She had the face of a woman who has been woken by a telephone and has gone straight to the one thing that matters, which was upstairs, asleep.

"Hello? Is this the school? Is it Tia— no. She's upstairs. Who is this?"

"Ms Kowalczyk? My name is Noreen Whitmore. I'm so sorry about the hour." Noreen's voice had gone into the register she uses for a resident with a burst pipe, warm and level, the rasp underneath. "I manage a building in another state. I have your name on a form as the emergency contact for a man named Devendra Mahajan. The form says sister."

"I'm sorry. I think you have the wrong number." Anjali looked at the glass of the phone with the polite frown she gives a parent who has mistaken her for the front desk. "I don't have a brother."

"I believe you," said Noreen. "Say it anyway."

"Say what?"

"His name. I'm going to turn the phone around. I'd like you to look at him and say it."

She held the phone out at arm's length in front of my face, with the screen toward me, so that I saw my sister, and my sister, through the little lens, saw me. She was a thumbnail with a gap in her teeth and a pale scar through her left eyebrow. I did not say anything. I had been told the rules.

"Dev Mahajan," said Anjali.

She said it with her eyes on my face, and then her mouth stayed open on the last syllable. I have seen my sister cry twice in my life, at eight with a scooter and at our mother's funeral, and both times she did it with her chin up, angrily, as though it were a form of argument. This was different. The tears came out of her without her face changing, in rows, and she looked down at them on the counter as at something that had been spilled.

"Why am I— I'm so sorry, I don't know why I'm—"

"Anju," I said.

"Say that again."

"Anju."

"Oh," said my sister. "Oh, you idiot." And then, in the voice she has used since I was five, for the moments when she is frightened and wants no one to know it, "Dev-u. Dev-u, where are you? What's— okay. Okay. Ben! Ben, I need the laptop. There's a five-forty. I'm taking the five-forty."

The screen showed her kitchen ceiling for a moment and went dark. Noreen stood with the phone still held out, as though it might ring again. Then she lowered it and looked at me for the third time that night, and this time she said it herself, once, with her eyes on mine and the gum stopped in her cheek. "Devendra Mahajan."

She went into the office and shut the glass door and did not say another word.

* * *

At ten to two, Left opened.

The doors slid back on an empty car. Above them the floor display, which has never been accurate, read 4B. And I discovered that I was standing. I had not decided to stand. My feet were going, in the borrowed socks, across the terrazzo toward the open car at the unhurried pace of a person who has been told his table is ready. I counted: one, two. I counted because I count, and because it is the one thing in all of this that has always been mine.

The chairs scraped all at once. Chioma got my left arm and Owen Fenske, who had not spoken in three hours, said "Nope" and took my right. Rafa came in behind me and put both arms around my chest, like a man holding a door in a wind. And Mrs Han walked past all of us, a woman who would have come up to the middle of my chest, and stood between me and the doors with the thermos hugged against her vest and said to the elevator, in the voice she uses for the group chat, "This one is spoken for."

Zofia was down on one knee. She passed the tape around my waist, twice, and tied it, and ran the other end across the floor to Chioma and knotted it round her wrist, with a tug to test it, as she would test a hem. "Louder," she said.

They said it, in a ring, over each other, with no chant and no rhythm. Mahajan became Mah-HAH-jan and MA-ha-jan, and in Rafa's mouth Mahahan, and I have never heard it said so warmly. From the laptop on the bench, small and clear, the strangers said it too. My feet took the third step, and the fourth, and each one went into Rafa's arms like walking into a wall of coats.

At ten past two, by the lobby clock, which stopped at four minutes past, the pipes began to sing.

It starts in the risers behind the mailboxes and goes up, and the radiators knock twice, and the elevator doors slid shut on the empty car with no more sound than a page. Through the frosted lettering on the office door I could see Noreen bent to a monitor, the burgundy of her hair lit from below. She had gone in at five to two to watch the line. I will say for her that she did not look away.

The feet stopped. I did not stop them. They were simply mine again, all at once, and I leaned back into Rafa, and he held me, and said "Mira" into my coat. The minute went by. Everyone was still saying it. When the clock began to move again, a few of them did not notice, and went on for another breath, to nobody, in the dark orange light.

The glass door opened. Noreen stood in it with her keys silent in her fist. She read it off the screen to herself in her head, then aloud, in the voice she used for EMBRACE THE CHANGE.

> HT-4B | Tenant of Record: MAHAJAN, D. | Status: CANCELLED (WITNESSED) | Effective: 11/09 02:10 | Source: SYSTEM

She looked up. It was the first full sentence she had said in an hour, and she said it to me across the whole ring of chairs, across the tray and the stroller and the old man with the watch, as she might have said it any Monday, at the front counter, past the butterscotch.

"Good morning, Dev."

* * *

By three they had gone up in twos and threes, and Rafa stacked the chairs. Chioma walked me to Right. On four the door was there, fourteen paces from her mat, and neither of us looked at it. I did not have an address. I had a raincoat with my name in the collar, a notebook, a steno pad, a floss slip and a couch, and for the first time in four days I could tell you which of them I was holding.

At ten past five my phone, which had said Unknown beside My Number since Thursday, lit on the arm of the couch and told me in a green bubble that Anju was at the airport and that I was not to move. The line marked My Number still says Unknown. I assume that if I asked the carrier they would find an error and correct it, and so I have not asked.

Chioma woke me at six with a mug. She sat on the arm of the couch in her scrubs, with the red frames crooked, and took my right hand and turned it over. "Hold still," she said. She uncapped the marker with her teeth. "Name, age, bed. That's how we don't lose anybody between floors."

I held it up in the gray light from her window when she had finished. The ink was still wet. It said DEV, 29, 4A couch.
