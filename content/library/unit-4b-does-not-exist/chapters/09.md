# Mr Grieve Takes Tea

The cane came down the sixth-floor hallway at ten to four, one tap to every two steps, and I stood at the elevator with my hands behind my back like a boy outside a principal's office. I had come up by the stairs, as I do when I am afraid of being early, and being early had done what it does, which is leave me ten minutes and a pulse.

He rounded the corner from 6A in a brown tweed three-piece with a watch chain across the waistcoat, carrying a white saucer in front of him at the height of a waiter's wrist. On it were a cup of tea, full, and one Rich Tea biscuit squared to the rim. The cane hung from his forearm by its brass duck. When he turned his head to take me in, the hearing aids let out two thin notes, like a kettle a long way off.

"Mr Mahajan. You are early."

"I wanted not to be late, Mr Pto—" The P came out of me like a cork. "Tolemy. Tol-uh-mee. Sorry."

"Quite so." He pressed the call button with the duck's head. "The P is silent, like most of my family's expectations. I say it to everyone, and everyone laughs, and I am no longer able to determine whether the laughter is for the remark or for my family."

We rode Right to four in a silence that he conducted. At the west end of the corridor he set the saucer on the deep sill of the landing window with a small click and turned the cup until the handle pointed left. From his waistcoat he drew a gold watch, flicked the lid open with a thumbnail and bowed over it, like a man at a graveside. There is no clock on that landing. I have since learned that it is the one place in the building where he trusts the time.

"Four o'clock, Mr Ambler." He used the voice of a man announcing a train. "I have brought the young man from the office. Mr Mahajan keeps the books. One introduces. It is the least one can do."

"How do you do," I said, to a windowsill.

The steam went straight up. I watched it harder than it deserved. Mr Grieve closed the watch and took my elbow with a hand that weighed nothing and held like a clamp. "We leave him to it," he said. "Observation is a form of pressure."

* * *

Apartment 6A is the corner unit, with a window on 31st Street and another on the alley, and it contains forty-one years of other people's worst days. They stand floor to ceiling on three walls, steno pads in bundles of twenty tied with string, each bundle labeled on a strip of masking tape in a hand as small as a stitch: a month, a year, a courtroom number. On the desk a stenotype machine sat under a dust cloth, the shape of a small covered birdcage, and beside it lay a microcassette recorder the size of a bar of soap with its cord wound round it in even turns. On the mantel and the bookcase and the sill stood six clocks, and all six said a few minutes earlier than they should.

"They're six minutes slow," I said, before I could stop myself.

"I set them correct on Sundays." Mr Grieve lowered himself into a chair with arms. "By Wednesday each has made up its own mind. I have ceased to argue. A man who has spent four decades insisting on the exact time learns, eventually, which witnesses will not be moved."

The carpet at the window had been worn to canvas in an oval, where someone had stood for fifty years looking at 31st Street. He made tea in a brown pot under a cozy knitted in a green I would have called regrettable, and he poured with a steadiness I took for strength and later understood to be habit. "Sugar?"

"No, thank you."

"Nor I. And I send his plain, which is not economy, Mr Mahajan. It is cowardice. One cannot write to a man to ask how many lumps he takes. I have wondered for thirty-six years." The cup came to me on a saucer with a rose on it. "Biscuit. Now. You have brought the question."

"I brought several."

"I said the better one."

I took out the index card. A man who writes his questions on an index card for a tea invitation has told you most of what you need to know about him. "What is in 4B? Who is paying for it? What happens if I delete the row?"

"The first is a question for a locksmith." Mr Grieve drank. "The third is a question for Mr Pugh, or a priest. The second has merit. But all three, you will observe, concern the room." He set the cup down. "The better question is always about the questioner. I will give you a week. Strictly speaking, I may not have a great deal more to give, but a week is a decent allowance."

I put the card in my pocket. It felt, there, like a small flat lie.

"Tell me what you know."

I told him: the row, the receipts, the washer. When I said the name on the undershirts, he stopped, with his hand on the handle of the pot.

"You have seen his laundry."

"On Thursday. There's a washer in the basement. It doesn't have water in it." I heard how that sounded. "The undershirts had his name on a tape, T. Ambler, sewn by hand. Somebody who liked doing it."

"Quite so." He did not move for about as long as it takes to read a short line. "I have wondered about the washing. A man who has been in a room since the spring of 1961 must have clean shirts. It is not the sort of thing a man in my position may ask." He poured, though neither cup needed it. "I had the name from the clerk of the day, Mr Kwan, in the spring of 1990, in that chair, with a book open on his knee. Thaddeus Ambler, of Dayton, Ohio. A salesman. He checked in, and he has not checked out."

"Did you ever see him?"

"Never. I deduce." He said it as one gives a plea. "I came to the Harwick in 1974, Mr Mahajan, when it was a hotel with a barroom and a house detective and a switchboard run by a woman called Dolores who could tell from the way you said your room number whether you had been drinking. The night court sat at Merrow and Twenty-eighth. I was its reporter, and a man who takes down other people's worst hours wishes to sleep where the hours are not so bad. In 1986 they turned the place into apartments, and I became a tenant instead of a guest. The difference lies chiefly in the lease."

"And the plate?"

"In 1989 I left a cup of tea on that sill, as one leaves milk for a cat, on a day I was too tired to take it back downstairs. At ten past four it was warm and empty." He put a finger to the bridge of his nose. "I was no longer tired. I was very much afraid, for about a week, and then I recognized the sensation as manners. One does not leave a man's tea untended. One asks after his health. He answers."

He nodded at the wall over the desk, where a typed card hung in a small frame. I got up to read it.

> THE SAUCER | Handle left: yes. Handle right: no. Cup warm and empty: received. Cup full and cold: not today. Biscuit gone, tea untouched: ask again. | P.G., 14 Sept 1989. Amended 1996 (biscuit clause).

"I set it left each day," he said behind me. "Left is the offer, and left is the acceptance, and I ask a question aloud, and if the answer is no, the handle is turned to the right. Thirty-six years of one-word answers, Mr Mahajan. I do not say it is conversation. I say it is correspondence." The hearing aids whistled as he turned. "I have had three rights. The second was the day I asked whether he would care to try a different biscuit. He is a man of fixed views on biscuits."

I laughed. It was the last time I did that afternoon, and I did it too loudly, and he permitted it with a small movement of one ear.

* * *

"You will want to know about the clerks," he said. "Everyone who sits in that chair does, at about month fourteen."

I did not ask how he knew my month.

"Mr Kwan was a courteous young man who counted everything. The stairs, the tiles, the minutes the Left elevator stuck, which he reported to me on a card. He sat where you are sitting, with his knees together. In 1998 he was no longer at his desk, and I was told he had taken a position elsewhere, and I did not believe it. Mrs Dunleavy was engaged the following January. Lemon drops. She wrote everything down, and then one day she had been written."

"Written."

"I decline to improve upon it." He lifted the cup, put it down. "Both wrote it down. It was the one habit they shared. I should not like to hear, in a few years, that it had been a third."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"Nor were they." He said this without cruelty, as a witness gives the date. "You will wish to know whether I have seen anyone go in. I have not. I deduce, which is not the same as seeing, and a court reporter is trained to know the difference. Let the record show that I am deducing." He folded his hands over the watch chain. "I will add one item that is not evidence. Mrs Whitmore's husband went out for cigarettes in 2016. I knew the man slightly. A pipefitter, in flannel. He fixed my radiator in 2011, unasked, and declined tea. He did not smoke, in my observation. I do not think it was cigarettes."

I said nothing about the socks. I am a person who writes things down and does not say them. But I added, which is what I do, and by the time the sum reached my face he was looking at me with an expression of regret, as if he had passed me a bill.

He coughed. It was the cough with the drawer in it, and it did not stop at the usual place. He took out the handkerchief and held it to his mouth, and I got to my feet and stood in the middle of his carpet doing nothing, as I do. When he lowered the cloth he folded it, and put it in his waistcoat, and did not look at it.

"Pleural," he said. "Four months, six if I am agreeable. I mention it so that the record is current." He said it the way you give a license number, and then he reached across and moved my saucer an inch toward me, since I had not touched the biscuit.

I sat down. "Is there someone I should—"

"There is Mrs Han, who has already brought soup. There is the woman from the hospice, whose name I have not retained. There is the gentleman in four, who has been informed." He regarded me down the length of his face. "I do not require rescue, Mr Mahajan. I should like to be attended. It is a different thing, and the building understands it better than the profession."

* * *

At twenty to six he opened a drawer in the desk and took out a steno pad, new, still holding its cellophane, with paper tinted faint green and a red rule down the middle. He tore off the cellophane with the care of a man unwrapping a medal. "I have ninety-one of these," he said. "I bought a gross in 1996 against a retirement I did not suppose would be so long. I have no further use for them, strictly speaking, except this one."

I took it. It was lighter than it looked.

"A record is only kind if it is left for someone," said Mr Grieve. "Nobody has ever left the next one anything but a key."

He gave me a pencil from a jar of them, sharpened to a needle, and I opened the pad on my knee. The page was very white. It had that faint tooth to the paper that makes a pencil sound like something. I wrote the date, and under the date, because I could not think what else a person writes, I wrote it all on one line. *Sat 18 Oct. Mahajan, D. Record begins.*

He read it upside down. A court reporter can read anything upside down. He nodded once, and I thought for a moment I was dismissed.

"Strictly speaking," he said, "that is the second line. The first is the question."
