# No Saved Messages

The phone buzzed face down on the kitchen counter at 5:58 on Sunday, and I had it in my hand before the buzz could finish. I have done that with every call from my family since last January. It is a habit with a date on it. I will get to the date.

Saturday first, because Saturday was the day I counted everything.

Counting is not new with me, but that week it had begun to spread. From my door at Tolliver Court, 3F, to the head of the stairs is fourteen steps along the breezeway, and I walked them with the laundry bag over my shoulder and my lips, a neighbor with a stroller told me later, moving. Thirty-four stairs down. Eleven items in the basket at the market on Kessler, and when I laid them on the belt I counted them again, and the cashier, who had been counting along with me, said "Eleven," as if we had agreed a price. Since Monday every number had become something I wanted to check, the way you pat your pockets after you have been told about a theft on the bus.

My apartment is a studio on the third floor of a complex built in the seventies for people who were meant to move out of it. I had lived there four years and the walls showed it: nothing on them. The bed was made with the corners tucked. The table had one chair at it and a second folded behind the door, for company. On the counter by the stove stood my mother's tiffin, three tiers of steel with a dent in the bottom clip, full of rubber bands and AA batteries, and leaning against it was a photograph I had never framed. She is in the yard in Edison, the August before the sound in her chest began, holding a trowel and a tomato and squinting. I took it, so she is looking at me, and that is why it has never gone on a shelf where I would see it from the bed.

Under a stapler, on the end of the counter, sat the mail. I open what has a window and stack what doesn't. On top was a postcard from the Gaslight. WE MISS YOU, QUIZZY RASCALS, it said, over a drawing of a pint glass with a face. THURSDAYS AT 8. FIRST APPETIZER ON US. Somebody there keeps a list, and I was on it, and I had not turned the card over in twenty-one months.

I did the laundry, which took an hour and forty minutes and two rolls of quarters. I folded it into thirds. I ate a bowl of rice and an egg, standing, and washed the one bowl and the one pan and set them on the drainer beside the two mugs.

That evening I sat at the table with the one chair and did not turn on the television. Under the kitchen window the radiator ticked as it warmed, the first week the complex had let it, and I listened to it with the particular attention of a man who has lately been knocked at by a wall. It ticked. It did what radiators do. I wrote down the time it started, 7:12, and then felt foolish and tore the page out of the pad and put it in the bin.

* * *

On Sunday I propped the phone against the tiffin at 5:58, and Anjali's face filled it.

"You answered," she said. "You always answer. Do you sleep on it?"

"It's a good phone."

"It's two minutes early, I know, Tia goes in the bath at six-fifteen and she has something to show you." My sister is five foot two and built like a person who lifts small children for a living, which she does. She had her hair in the claw clip, so she had come straight from a day of getting four-year-olds to hold pencils, and the gap in her front teeth showed when she smiled, and the pale scar through her left eyebrow, from a scooter when she was eight, rose with the rest of her face. "Okay. Okay. Tia, come here, bring it, no, the whole page."

Tia's face replaced hers, a small round close-up in dinosaur pajamas, and then a sheet of paper unrolled itself in front of the camera. It was a grid in orange crayon, four rows of four, and in the bottom right square there was a very small person with glasses.

"It's you," said Tia. "It's a spreadsheet. Mama said you live in one."

"Technically I only visit."

"Why are you in the corner?"

"That's where the total goes."

"What's a total?"

"The number at the bottom that tells you whether everything added up."

Tia considered this with her whole face. "Did it?"

"I'll check," I said.

Behind them a large figure crossed the kitchen holding a pumpkin like a sleeping dog. Ben Kowalczyk is six foot four and does most things with great gentleness, including the knife in his other hand. The pumpkin had half a face already.

"Hey, Dev."

"Hey, Ben. It's the twelfth."

"You have to let it settle," said Ben, with dignity, and left.

Anjali took the phone back and walked it to the table. "I've been scanning Mummy's recipe cards. I've done forty-one. You have to see them, they're all in her hand, and the stains, Dev, the stains are exactly where you'd think." She held a card up to the lens.

> DAL TADKA | Serves 4 | 1 cup toor dal, 3 cups water, ½ tsp haldi, salt. Pressure cooker, 7 whistles. Tadka: ghee, jeera, hing, 2 dry red chilli, curry leaves. | (in pen, beside Serves 4: always makes 8)

"Always makes eight," said Anjali. "She wrote it herself. She knew."

I looked at the card for longer than the call required. In the line about the cooker, the seven in *7 whistles* had its bar through the stem. Hers, then mine, and four days earlier a pink carbon in a filing room that said I had written it. I said it was a good card. I said she had always cooked for whoever might come in.

"Which brings me to the twentieth," said Anjali. "Diwali. It's a Monday, I know you know. We're driving up to Edison on the Saturday. Dad asked."

"Dad asked what?"

"If you were coming." She did something with her mouth. "He asked the toaster. He stood in the kitchen and said to the toaster, 'Dev is coming for Diwali?' So I'm counting it as a question."

"It's a Monday. Technically it's the week of the freeze, there's a data room, it's a work thing."

"It's Diwali, you idiot."

"I'll be on a call. In the evening. I'll wear a shirt."

"Fine. Dad will say hello to the ceiling." She let that sit. "He's talking to her tomatoes now. Thursday I watched him from the window, out in the yard in his slippers, telling them something in Marathi for ten minutes. Then he turned every pot a quarter turn, the way she did. He didn't water them. He just turned them toward the light." She looked down at the table. "Somebody should tell him it's October."

"Tell him the quarter turn was right."

"Tell him yourself. On Monday."

"On the call," I said.

"On the call," she agreed, as though it were a concession from us both. Then her eyes went past my shoulder, the way they do. An occupational therapist reads a room the way other people read a menu. "You have two mugs out."

"The set came as two."

"One's dry and upside down. One's been used. You've had that set four years and you still put the second one out."

"That's where the drainer is."

Anjali waited. She is very good at it. It is her job, and she was doing it on me through a phone from six hundred miles away, with a four-year-old's elbow in the corner of the frame. "Is something wrong?"

"It's a data migration."

She laughed, and it did not go all the way down. "Okay," she said. "Okay. Okay." Then, in her ordinary voice: "Sunday at six."

"Sunday at six," I said, which is how we end every call, like a signature.

* * *

When the screen went dark I did what I do on Sundays. I opened the phone app, then Voicemail, then the tab marked Saved, where one message sits, and held my thumb above it.

> Voicemail | Saved (1) | MUMMY | Thu, Jan 11, 9:41 PM | 0:23

I hold my thumb there for twenty-three seconds, the length of the message, and then I close the app. It seemed the fair amount.

On Thursday the eleventh of January last year my mother was in a hospice bed in Edison with my father and my sister beside her, and I was at the Gaslight with the Quizzy Rascals, who needed a ninth. I had told Anjali I would take the Friday train. At 9:41 my phone lit on the table with MUMMY across it, and the host had just asked for the capital of Burkina Faso, and I had my hand up and was spelling it, and I was on the second G. I turned the phone face down. We got the answer right. She left a message twenty-three seconds long. She died at 3:12 in the morning, and at 3:30 my sister called me, and I have never told Anjali that I saw the call. I have never listened to the message. I left the Rascals the next week, and I had not been back to the Gaslight since.

That Sunday, after forty-one recipe cards held up one at a time to a camera, I decided to press it. I cannot tell you why that night. Somebody was at last doing something with what our mother had left, and I had the one piece nobody had asked about.

I touched the line. The screen thought about it.

> Voicemail | Saved (0) | No Saved Messages

I assumed I had touched the wrong tab. I went back to Inbox, and forward to Saved, and the line was the same. I shut the app and opened it. I restarted the phone and watched the logo come up and go. On the laptop I logged in to the carrier's site and clicked through Inbox, Saved and Deleted, and got three zeroes. The cloud backup keeps three copies, and none went back as far as January. In the kitchen drawer, under takeout menus and a handful of soy sauce packets, lay the phone I had owned before this one, a cracked gray thing that I now understood I had kept for exactly this. I charged it for ten minutes, which were long, and it came on with a picture of a lake I used to like, and I opened Voicemail, and it said what the other one said.

What came first was relief. I know the second it arrived, because I was holding the old phone, and my shoulders came down, the left one first, and I let them. A man who has carried one object for twenty-one months and is told it is gone has been told that he may put his arms down. Then I noticed what my shoulders were doing, and I set the old phone on the counter very carefully, as though it could still say something, and stood there with my hand over my mouth.

That week I had cleaned up some sixty rows for go-live: trailing spaces, three phone numbers, an emergency contact for 1C. I did not connect that to the phone for more than a week, and I put the two facts side by side here only so that they are on the record together. I know now what that voicemail was worth to the building, which was almost nothing. I had carried it for twenty-one months, and for none of them had I been holding it.

* * *

At eleven I got up to brush my teeth. My bed is six steps from the bathroom door. I have walked it every night for four years, and I know it the way you know your own phone number. I took six, and the door was not there. I took a seventh.

I went back to the bed and walked it again, with my arms at my sides, counting out loud, and it was seven. I turned and walked it back, and it was seven. Nothing in the hall had moved. The carpet was the carpet, with a stain by the baseboard the shape of Ohio, and the light switch was where the light switch is, and the hall had grown by a step and given no reasons.

I ended up on the kitchen floor, which is not a place I go, with my back against the cabinet under the sink and the old phone on the tile beside me.

"Okay," I said to the empty room, in Anjali's voice, because it was the only voice I had to hand. "Okay. Okay."

Under the window the radiator knocked twice. It was not a pipe banging. It was two knocks, unhurried, spaced the way a person knocks who knows you are in, and I knew the spacing. I looked at the radiator for some time. I did not knock back.
