# Status Pending

My badge said OCCUPANT. It had said MAHAJAN, D., RENT ROLL CLERK, with a photograph from my first week in which I look like a man whose chair has been taken away, and at ten to six on Wednesday morning, on the loveseat in the leasing corner where I had lain down for ten minutes at midnight, the name line read OCCUPANT, and the position line CLERK-RR-001, and the photograph was my face. It was the only thing in the building that hadn't been revised.

I sat up. The ring on my belt was lighter. I did not need to go round it. The replacement key from the Tolliver leasing office, cut on Monday for thirty-five dollars, was gone, and I knew the way you know a tooth.

I had stayed at the office on Tuesday to finish the forty duplicate-hour flags, and I had finished them at eleven, and I had meant to take the 14, and at 2:10 I had been lying on a vinyl loveseat with a coat over me, listening to the pipes. I had not been at home to see it taken. That was a mercy, if the building trades in mercies, and I have never been sure.

At noon I rode the 14 out to Kessler Avenue and walked into the leasing office at Tolliver Court. The woman at the desk was named Pam, with reading glasses pushed up into her hair and a calendar of kittens, and she smiled at me in the way one smiles at an application.

"3F?" she said, and tapped. "That's been vacant since August. I've got no lease on that unit for a Mahajan. Would you like to fill one out?"

"I've lived there four years. My rent's eleven hundred and twenty-five. I pay on the first, by the portal."

"Mm." She tapped again. "I'm so sorry. There's just nothing." She was nice about it. She was nice about it all the way, with her hand moving the mouse and her eyes sliding off me, and she offered me a lollipop from a bowl, as one does to the children of tenants.

I walked up the thirty-four stairs and along the fourteen steps of the breezeway. The blinds in 3F were open. Through the glass I saw a bare gray carpet, an empty counter, a stove with its door ajar, and no table, no chair, nothing on a wall. The bed was gone. The counter by the stove, where a steel tiffin had stood for four years with a photograph leaning on it, was bare. I did not let myself finish that sentence. I could not see the hall from the breezeway. I thought of the stain the shape of Ohio on the baseboard, and hoped it was still there, a thing the building had no use for.

* * *

On Thursday, at six, Noreen came in and found me. I had slept on the loveseat, with the coat. She opened the glass door with her keys chiming, and stopped, and her hand went to the lanyard.

"Can I help you?" she said, politely.

It was the voice she uses with prospects, the very kind one. She meant it. She looked at the coat, and the loveseat, and my face, and for about the length of a breath something moved across hers, a flicker, as of a screen between two channels, and her mouth opened on a word she did not find. "I'm sorry," she said. "Are you waiting for Espie? She doesn't come till nine." And she went into her office and shut the door, and I heard her keys hit the desk.

Joyce arrived at eight with the shortbread and the calculator. She stopped at my desk with her tin in both hands. "Noted," she said. I have heard her say it ten thousand times. I had never heard it with something under it, like a hand under a tablecloth, and when her eyes met mine they were frightened, and she looked away from me at once, and I understood that she was trying to find out what she was afraid of. Espie came in at nine in a coat the color of mustard, nails dark plum with a single gold accent on each ring finger, and stopped in the door.

"Hi! Oh my gosh, sorry, are you the new IT guy? Because the printer is having, like, a whole moment."

"I'm the clerk."

"Ok so you're, like, the clerk," she said, with lovely goodwill. "No because that's so real."

That afternoon the answer came back from the shared inbox in Human Resources. I had written to ask for a verification of employment, because I thought that if I could have it on paper I could show it to someone, and I think now that I was hoping a form would hold me up, as a form always has.

> From: Corvane HR Shared Services | To: RRCLERK | Subject: RE: Employment verification request (Mahajan, Devendra)

> No employee record found for Devendra Mahajan. Payroll shows an active position, CLERK-RR-001 (Rent Roll Clerk, Harwick Terrace), paid biweekly to the account on file. We are unable to discuss position holders by name. Regards, HR Shared Services

I printed it, and folded it twice, and put it in the notebook with the floss slip. At the vending machine in the hall my card was refused with a message in a stately font, ACCOUNT NOT FOUND, and a bag of pretzels hung in the coil above the tray and went on hanging. The man who brought the pizza at six, which I had ordered on the office line and paid for with a twenty from a coat pocket, asked whether I was waiting for someone, and I said yes, and he opened his mouth to say something else, and shut it, and handed me the box with a puzzled look at the bag, and walked out and forgot to take the tip.

People addressed the chair. This is the thing I would like to be able to explain and cannot. Espie came to my desk with a stack of leases and said, brightly, "Does anybody know who keeps moving the stapler?" She said it to the chair, which was empty by then, since I was standing next to it. Joyce put a cup of coffee on the corner, where she had put one every morning for fourteen months, and when I took it, she stood looking at her own empty hand.

I wrote the order out in the notebook that night, under the lamp, in my sevens. First the building had taken what I did not look at. Then what I looked at sometimes: a chair, a bicycle. Now it was taking what I held, and I had done it to myself with two hundred and fifty-one flags in nine days, and the batch had done the arithmetic. It does not give change. Where the sum was bigger than the thing, the rest was simply gone. And I thought, with a wretched clearness, that the sum was not finished.

* * *

My phone had said NO SERVICE since the morning, and by evening it said EMERGENCY CALLS ONLY, and under Settings, in the line marked *My Number*, it said Unknown. Anjali had called at six on Wednesday as she had every day, and told me Tia's tooth had come out into a piece of toast. On Thursday she did not call.

At eight on Thursday night I sent Chioma my name from the office laptop, through the building chat, since my phone had stopped being a phone. *Dev Mahajan.* It was the best I could do. I did not know whether it would arrive.

Her answer reached me at ten to six on Friday morning, on the laptop, with a time stamp from the end of a double shift.

> Chioma Eze, 5:48 AM: who is this

I read it six times. At noon the glass door opened and she came in, in the hospital hoodie, with her red frames sliding down her nose, and a face like a woman who has been told her car was never hers. She stood in front of the desk, and I stood up, and she looked at me politely and did not seem to know why she was looking.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I got a message. It's weird. I know I should know you." And she pushed up the sleeve of the hoodie on her right arm, the one with the burn scar on the inside of the wrist, and read off her own skin, where someone had written in black marker, in capitals going shaky at the end, what she must have written in the night.

"Dev Mahajan," she read. She frowned. "Wall guy."

"That's me."

"I've been writing it on myself since Tuesday. It keeps coming off in the shower." She looked at me as if I were a blood pressure. "I skipped the wall. Two nights. I did a double on the Tuesday and a double on the Wednesday and I got in and fell on the bed and I said, tomorrow, and then it was tomorrow." Her voice was careful, and getting higher. "Mahajan. I'm so sorry. For the record I do not forget people. It's the only thing I'm good at."

I put my hand out, and she took it, and held it hard, with the other hand over her mouth. I thought: a text is a thing you read. I do not believe the building reads. As far as I could tell, it listens. For ten days she had texted me at eight and eight, and said it to the wall at night, and I had counted both as one thing, and on the two nights she skipped the wall there had been nothing for the building to hear.

Zofia came by at two, with a pair of Mr Grieve's trousers over her arm for a hem, and the tape around her neck. She stopped in the door and looked at me, and her eyes went up and down, the way a person's do who reads a thing by its dimensions. She did not say hello. She put out the tape.

"You lost eight," she said.

"Centimeters?"

"Eight." She did not measure. She had it by eye, to a hair. "Since September. Is not shrink. Is leaving." She wound the tape round her fist. "I have your numbers in book. I keep my book, so I keep you. Is only reason." She nodded once, severely. "Eat something."

* * *

On Friday night I borrowed Chioma's phone. She had decided to keep me in sight until her shift, and sat on the bottom stair in her scrubs with her clogs in her lap, watching me not do it. I knew the number. It is the one thing I have dialed more than any other in my life, and I did it without looking, on the stairwell landing, with my thumb.

She answered on the second ring.

"Hello?" said my sister. In the background a dishwasher ran and a child laughed at something. It was her voice, and it was warm, and she did not know me. "Sorry, who is this?"

"It's Dev."

"Dev who?"

I had my mouth open. I heard, behind her, a man's low voice ask a question and a chair scrape, and then her breath. She was being kind. She was exactly as kind as she is with a parent in a clinic who has called the wrong number and is embarrassed. "Is this about the account?" she said. "Because I already told the other person, we're not interested."

I hung up.

I know the silence that came after. It is the one after the ring runs out, before the voice begins, and I sat in it on the eleventh of January last year at forty-one minutes past nine, with my phone face down and my hand in the air, spelling the name of a city. In that silence, in January, a message had been waiting. This time there was nothing in it. I held Chioma's phone against my chest and I thought of my father's voice, which I'd lost at Diwali without noticing, and of how many of the things I had lost I had not noticed, and how this was the first that I had.

* * *

On Saturday at nine minutes past nine, at the desk beside the loveseat, I logged in as the clerk, which was the only thing the building still let me be, and opened HT-4B. The field that had said WHITMORE, G. on Monday, and AMBLER on the Monday before, had changed again.

> HT-4B | Tenant of Record: MAHAJAN, D. | Status: PENDING | Effective: 11/09 02:10 | Source: SYSTEM

I read it as I read every row. I looked at the date. A pending tenant is a tenant who has not yet been entered. I had about seventeen hours.

I went out and sat on the lobby bench among the left-over orange lights, which no one had taken down, and did nothing for most of the day, which is not an easy thing to do in a building that does not see you. A woman I'd said good morning to for fourteen months went by with a stroller and said, to nobody, "Is somebody there?" Mrs Han's greeting had gone up at six: GOOD MORNING TO ALL, INCLUDING THE QUIET ONES. I read it on a borrowed phone. I did not know which I was.

At nine that night the glass doors opened, and Chioma came in. She had changed out of her scrubs into a hoodie and jeans, and she carried under one arm a stack of folding chairs from the basement, five of them, steel, with HARWICK TERRACE painted on the backs in white. She set them down against the bench with a clatter that rang in the lobby like a dropped tray. She straightened. She had the red frames pushed up in her hair and her sleeve rolled to the elbow, with the black marker on the inside of the wrist, and she looked at me directly, for a long time, as at a name she had decided not to lose.

"I'm going to say your name until it takes," she said.
