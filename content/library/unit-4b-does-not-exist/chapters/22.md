# What Margie Wanted

Toast comes first, at the door, and then the rain: that is the order, every time. By the second pace there was water running down a window that faced a brick wall, and by the fifth the light had gone the color of tea, and I heard my sister behind me, counting under her breath in the voice she uses on stairs.

"Six. Seven. Eight. Nine."

"Nine," I said.

"I know. I counted." She stopped on the worn path in the oak boards with her hands pushed into the back pockets of her jeans, and looked at the room the way she looks at a client's kitchen from the door. Floor. Walls. Exits. Anything on the floor that could take an old person's foot.

It was Saturday the fifteenth. On Thursday I had put a note through the flap marked LETTERS, asking whether I might bring a guest, and on Friday morning the answer had come out folded in three, on yellow paper, in the round hand. *Bring her. I'd like to see the face that goes with the voice. M.* Rafa had taken down the key with the green tag at ten and had written *Guest* in the Purpose column of his clipboard. Last time I had written *Depends*. He looked at the word for a while, and nodded, and did not come in.

Anjali and I had counted the corridor first, thirty-one paces from the 4A mat, she in her stair voice and I under my breath, and at the door she had stopped and looked at the brass 4 and the slightly crooked B. "Okay," she said. "It's a door." She is the only person I know who can make that sentence sound like a diagnosis.

"Right then," said Margie.

She stood up from the wing chair with the dent in it. She was a head taller than I was and she stooped from forty years of desks. The oatmeal cardigan had its third button missing and the glasses were on their cord and the big hands hung at her sides, not knowing what to do with a visitor. Behind her the clock said ten past four. Under the window the radiator knocked, and on the shelf over the sink stood the ten white teacups. The curtains in the bay were the oatmeal ones, lined, thirty-eight by sixty-two. "You'll be the sister," she said.

"Anjali."

"I know, dear. I heard you introduce yourself to a wall."

My sister did something I had never seen anyone do in that room. She took the kitchen chair with the tied cushion, the low one, and turned it a quarter, so that she sat at an angle to Margie rather than in front of her, with her knees tilted and her hands open in her lap and her head a little below Margie's. It is how she sits for a child on the floor. "Tell me about your day," she said.

"My day?"

"What happens. When you wake up. What matters. What gets in the way."

I have watched Margie be dry and I have watched her be exact. I had not seen her be delighted. "Nobody has asked me that," said Margie, "since the twenty-ninth of August, 2019. I should say that it doesn't begin. It's ten past four, dear. There's rain. I make tea. I listen to the others. Nils taps in the pipe, and Inez sings on what I take to be Wednesdays. Mr Ambler's wireless has the ball game on and I know the score by heart. I write things down." She sat, slowly, as one who has been waiting. "Mostly it's what the clerk is doing. Lately that's been a good deal."

"Who else do you hear?" said Anjali.

"Vera hums three notes, and I have never found out the song. Wendell whistles the quarter hour, though we have no quarter hours. Constance does Lady Macbeth on Tuesdays and Cole Porter on Fridays, and Hector snores like a bus idling at a stop. Mr Whitmore says almost nothing, which I find restful." She looked at the row of cups. "Only as sounds, dear. Like a family in a house with thin walls."

"That's most families," said Anjali, and tilted her head at the knocking. "Is that the radiator? It's on the wrong interval. It needs bleeding."

"Rafa's never been in."

"Then I'd like it seen to," said Margie, and I understood that it was the second thing she had asked for in a week.

"You've a little one," she said. She had seen the photograph on Anjali's phone lock screen, a small girl in a pink kurta holding a clay lamp as if it might be taken from her.

"Tia. She's four. She thinks my brother is a spreadsheet."

"He is a very orderly one," said Margie. "I've read fourteen of his receipts."

* * *

They talked for twenty minutes, and I will not put down all of it. I will put down the part where my sister said she was tired, which she had not said to me. She said it flatly, looking at the teacups: I'm so tired, Margie. Not of the work. Of being the one who knows where everything is.

"Of course you are," said Margie. "I wasn't taken, dear, I should say that to you before anything. Nobody took me. I stepped in. I didn't want a retirement party or a funeral. I wanted a room where nobody would say how are you, Margie, because then I would have to answer." She turned her cup on its saucer. "Don't confuse being tired with being alone. I did. They look the same from the inside. This is a very good room for being tired. It has been a poor one for the other."

My sister did not say anything. After a moment she nodded, once, and got up, and went to the window to look at the radiator.

Margie touched my sleeve. At the card table she poured me a cup from the brown pot and took down from the shelf over the sink a long ledger with a cloth spine, the kind that has not been made since the clerk's office had a rubber stamp. It fell open where it had been opened before.

> HT-4B | Nov. 1998 | Rent received: cash | Paid From: me | E. Kwan

> In the margin, in pencil, in a small slanted hand: It asks who.

"This is Ellis's," she said, low, with her eyes on my sister's back. "I found it in the desk in January of '99. I want you to understand what it says. I'll say it plain, as nobody said it to me."

I am setting it down here for you, whoever you are, at the desk, because I wish somebody had set it down for me in those words, and because the folder tabbed NEXT is empty and I would rather you did not find it so. This is what she said.

"There are three fields, dear. You've done the count. A floor plan says the room is there. A permit says that somebody allowed it. Each one is an entry and each one is charged, as you know. And then the last, the thing at the end that asks whom."

"Source of Funds," I said. "That's what it's called now. The default is PREPARER."

"Then you'll have run out of preparer by the time you get there. I had, after twenty-one years." She had not touched the tea. "Ellis said me, in '98. He had the plan and the count, he'd made them himself. He never found the permit. He was twelve years in by then and there wasn't anything left in his drawer, and the book asked, and he wrote the only name he had. The building took him whole and certified nothing. It doesn't certify in installments, dear. Have all three before you answer the question."

"The sheet A-4 draws the riser eleven feet wide. Gary says that's a unit. And I have the number off the window."

"Mr Whitmore is a sensible man. Yes. You have the plan and the permit, and you've paid, I think, rather a lot for both already." She tapped the ledger. "It goes in a certain order. The drawer first, which is what you stopped looking at. Then the shelf, which is what you look at on Tuesdays. And when those are spent, it takes whoever you'd miss."

From behind the shelf, from inside the wall, came a small dry sound, a pencil going along a line.

"That's Ellis," said Margie, without turning. "He's in the margins. I can hear him writing."

"What does it take?" I said. "If I answer. What does the building do with a person?"

"It files them. It doesn't make change, and it doesn't ask. If you spend a person on a sum that wanted a mug, the rest is simply gone." She said it as she said everything, as a thing about books. "I'm not trying to frighten you. I'm telling you what's in the column."

The sum was bigger than what was left in my drawer. I did the arithmetic that I have always done, with my lips closed. Six flags had cost a ring of keys and a kitchen drawer. Forty flags had cost a home. Two fields and the question would cost more than any forty flags, and I had already spent the drawer, and the shelf, and some of what stood behind the shelf, and I did not like the figure.

"Mr Grieve is in the hospital," I said. "He'd like to come in. He wants to meet Mr Ambler. He wants to know what sugar he takes."

"Then that's for Mr Ambler to say, dear, not for me. He's the host." Something went across her face that was not quite a smile. "Ask him at four. Don't be solemn about it, he takes it for a bad quarter."

"And you, Margie?" I said. "If it were certified. If there were a door that worked. Would you come out?"

For a while the only sound was the rain.

"You want to rescue everybody, dear. I noticed it in the receipts. You take the long way round a bus stop so as not to walk past a man." She said it gently. "Some of us want the radiator. Ask Mr Ambler. Ask Nils, if you can get him to stop tapping. A door is only worth having if it opens both ways, and only if the person it opens for walks through of his own accord. You'll have to ask them one at a time, and you'll have to believe what they say."

"Margie," said my sister, from the window.

She had come back across the oak. She stood by the card table with her hands at her sides, not folded, and she was looking at Margie's face and not at mine. "Take it out of me. Whatever it is. If it has to come from someone, take it out of me."

I did not say anything. I had a mouthful of tea, and I swallowed it.

"That isn't how it's written, dear," said Margie.

She looked at me. I looked at her. She said nothing, and I said nothing, and I will say for Margie Dunleavy that she did not lie to my sister and did not tell her the truth, and that it was the kindest thing anybody did that day.

Then she got up and went along the shelf of what the clerks had lost, past the keys and the gift card and the white mug, and took something down from the end. It was the size of a matchbox. The label on it was in my capitals.

"I've had this since the twelfth of October," said Margie. "I didn't think it was mine to play."

MUMMY 1/11 9:41.

My sister, who was beside me, did not look at it. She looked at the clock instead, and the clock said ten past four.

* * *

The display above Left said 4B while we were still in the corridor, and then 4, when the doors opened. Anjali walked in ahead of me and put her back to the mirrored wall.

"Okay," she said to the car. "Okay."

In Chioma's bathroom I sat on the edge of the tub with the door locked and the taps off. I opened my hand. It was a plastic cassette not much bigger than a book of matches, scuffed at one corner, with a window onto a brown reel that had not moved in twenty-two months. I read the label. Block capitals, my own, level and a little too careful: the building had written it for me, in my hand, though not with any hand that I remember.

Twenty-three seconds, according to my phone. I knew the figure the way you know your own weight.

I put it in the pocket of the raincoat, against the lining, where the name was.

"Later," I said, to the tub.
