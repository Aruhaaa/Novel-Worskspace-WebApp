# The Recliner

The room smelled of pipe-joint compound and stale coffee, and before I had counted past three I knew I was in a basement. I had expected hotel wallpaper. I got knotty pine, a drop ceiling with a water stain the shape of a kidney, brown carpet tiles, and a space heater ticking orange in the corner like a small animal with a grievance.

I should go back an hour, and a day.

The clocks had gone back on Sunday. The building, which has never been able to settle an account without a clock, found itself with two ten-past-twos in one night and kept both. I lay in the dark at Tolliver, in the stove light, and listened to it: the pipes singing, the stove clock stuck, the radiator under the kitchen window knocking its twice, and then an hour later by the wall, all of it again. As far as I could tell, it billed Friday's rush a second time, and the second pass took what the first had left that was cheap. The toaster. The bathroom scale. A thing I could not name, and knew had gone because the shelf in my head had a gap in it.

On Monday morning my chair at the office had lowered itself an inch, or I had. I sat at the desk that fit Margie's back and felt my feet reach the floor too easily, as though the room had been told to expect a smaller man. Forty new flags stood in the queue: the software had found forty ledger entries stamped with an hour that had happened twice, and raised a banner on each. I do not think Brent had thought about the autumn. Pugh had not said a word. I opened HT-4B out of habit, and it read as follows.

> HT-4B | Tenant: WHITMORE, G. | Rent: $1,840.00 | Balance: 0.00 | Validation: FAIL (3 required fields empty)

"The señora was here last night," Rafa said, when I went down for the key at one. He did not look up from the bench. "Ten o'clock. In her coat. She puts a shirt on the dryer, a flannel, folded, and goes. She says nothing. She says nothing so hard I hear it." He turned the clipboard to me. Under Purpose I wrote *Gary*, and he looked at it for some time before he gave me the key.

* * *

Nine paces. The window at the end of them was a basement window, a slot high in the wall, with a well outside it where rain came down onto gravel. There was a workbench along one side with pipe wrenches on pegs in descending order of size, and a clock from a plumbing-supply house that said ten past four. On the shelf above the bench, in a row, stood ten mugs, each from a different supplier, and I counted them and said nothing about it. In the middle of the floor, facing a television on a rolling cart, was a blue recliner with duct tape on the left arm, and in the recliner, with a thermos on the floor beside him and a pre-game show talking about someone's hamstring, sat a man in a flannel shirt.

"Chief," he said, without turning his head.

He was fifty-two. He had a square gray moustache, cut as straight as a level, and a heavy, quiet, pleasant face that had been in the weather, and one thumb was wrapped in white tape to the second knuckle. The shirt was red and black. The boots were laced to the top. He turned the sound down on a man in a headset and looked at me over the footrest.

"Noreen send you?"

"No. Not exactly."

"She mad?"

"She hasn't said she's mad." I stayed where the nine paces had left me, at the window end, with my back to the rain. "She brought you a shirt."

"I know." He plucked at the front of the one he had on, a man checking whether it was still there. "Came through last night. Smells of that softener. She never used that. Always said it was for people with time." He drew a thumb along his moustache. "How long?"

I had known he would ask, and I had worked out the answer on the stairs, and still it came out smaller than I'd meant. "Nine years and nine months."

He nodded slowly, like a man hearing the cost of a repair. "That's a long Saturday."

"I'm sorry."

"Not yours." He pointed with his chin at a cooler by the wall. "Sit down, you're making the place look nervous."

I sat on the cooler. For a while neither of us said anything, which I think was the best thing he could have done, and the rain went down the well, and on the television a man laughed at a joke about a kicker. He told it in short pieces, with his eyes on the screen. He said that he had been a pipefitter thirty-one years and had never in that time gone into a space without knowing the way out of it. "First time," he said. "Hell of a first."

"I'm not sure what happened," I said. "Noreen said—"

"She'd say she didn't look up." He grunted. "She didn't. That's not the thing. I wasn't missing, Chief. I was done being looked past. I come in with the lunch, and I stand there, and the whole of me is going, *turn around, Noreen*, and I think, I'll go up the stairs and see if she turns. Five minutes. See if she comes looking." His taped thumb went to his mouth and came away. "Then I couldn't turn around myself. It's a funny kind of stuck. You know the way out. You just can't want it enough at the right time."

There were noises through the wall, as there are in any hour. Somebody sang, low, a few bars of something in Spanish, and stopped. Somewhere a pipe was struck, once, twice, a pause. "Inez," said Gary. "She does the mornings, whatever they are. That's Nils in the stack, one-two-pause. Mr Ambler's got his ball game. And Ellis, you can hear him if you're quiet. Writing. Like a mouse in the wall." He said the names the way a man reads off tools in a box. "You get to know them. They're not bad company."

"Margie told you how to get out."

"Margie says it like a lease clause." He nearly smiled. "The door has to be real. All the papers. The plan and the permit and the count. Somebody's got to make it a unit with a number. Then someone's got to say my name at the door, the whole name, out loud, when I'm the one in front, and I got to walk." He looked at the television as if it had asked him a question. "Noreen's scared of me. Of the look. I'm scared of her face. Ten years. I don't know what I'd say to a woman ten years older, that I left on a Saturday."

"She washes your socks," I said. "On the weekends. She leaves them on the dryer for Thursday."

He did not speak. He took a hand off the arm of the chair and put it over his eyes, a big hand with a taped thumb, and kept it there, and I looked at the wrenches on their pegs until the television said something about a quarterback and he took it away.

"Eleven feet," he said, in a different voice, level, a man changing the subject to a thing he could lift. "You walked it? The width of this place? Eleven. Read the drawing for the riser, R-4, on the plan. Somebody drew a riser eleven feet wide. I never saw a riser eleven feet wide in thirty-one years. That's not a riser, Chief, that's a unit. Any journeyman with a tape could've told them. Whoever ran the pipes in 'eighty-six didn't know a flange from a fish."

"I'll look at the plan again."

"Do." He picked up the thermos and set it down again unopened. "One thing. Say it exactly. The anode rod on the number one heater, in the basement. It's due. I told her Thursday at the kitchen table, and she said hush, she had the roofer on her mind." He stopped. His mouth worked under the moustache. "Tell her—" he said, and shut it, and shook his head. "No. Just the rod."

"The anode rod. Number one."

"That's it, Chief."

I counted out. Nine, window to door, not fast. The corridor was the corridor, and the maroon carpet, and my phone said 1:44, and I wrote the nine in the notebook, and my hand was not quite steady.

* * *

I took the stairs. Rafa was on an upturned bucket in the boiler room, with the toothpick, and the big heaters in a row behind him, three of them, gray, tall as men, with their numbers stenciled on in white. I told him all of it. I told him that the husband of the señora, which is what he had always called her, was named Gary, and Gary was inside.

Rafa did not move. "In nine years," he said, "I never hear his name. She says 'my husband.' I say 'señora.' We are very polite." His hands were hanging between his knees. "Ten. You said ten."

"Ten."

"Four times I bleed the stack on four for the knocking. Nils, you say? Four times I put the key in the valve, and I say to myself, is air. Is air." He looked at the stub of toothpick. "Is people."

"I didn't know until Thursday either."

"Mira." He stood up the way a very large animal stands. "I turned one key. I am going to ask, and you will not like the question. If I turn it for the second, is it for him?"

"He didn't ask for you. He asked for a rod."

That was the best I could do, and it worked, because Rafa laughed, a short noise, and went to the toolbox. The big heater on the left was number one, with a hex head on top the size of a fist. He put the wrench on it and it took Rafa and me and a length of pipe slid over the handle, and it came out of the tank with a sound like a tooth, and he held it up under the caged bulb.

I had thought an anode rod would be a rod. This was a stub of corroded wire as long as a finger, gray and furred, the end of it like a root pulled from a flowerbed. He turned it over. He said the word in Spanish I will not set down.

The maintenance log hung from a nail by the door, a ruled ledger with a cloth spine and Rafa's square capitals down every page. He ran his thumb back through the years to a line in January of 2016, and it was not his hand. It was in pencil, in heavy, upright, careful capitals, a hand used to writing on the back of pipe.

> ANODE #1: INSPECT / REPLACE. DUE JAN. (G.W.)

"Is not my writing," Rafa said.

"No."

"I never saw this line." He ran his finger under it. Nothing had been ticked. The next line was a thermostat in March. "It was there. Nine years. In the book I wrote in every day." He was not looking at me, and he had his hand flat on the page, as you hold down a map in the wind. "A man writes you a note in the log, you have to read the log."

He went back to the bucket and sat, and I put my hand on his shoulder for a second and took it away, because I did not know the rule.

* * *

At five I came up in Right, because Left was on four and did not answer, and Noreen was sitting on the corridor floor with her back against the plaster of 4A, a little way from the mat, with her legs out in front of her and her shoes off. She had her glasses in her lap on their beaded chain. She had not put on lipstick. In the lamp-light she looked like what she was, a woman of fifty-eight on a carpet, and I thought of what a person looks like who is trying not to be seen to have been sitting somewhere for an hour.

I stopped about ten feet from her. I did not know how to do it, so I did it the way I do everything, and said it precisely.

"I saw him. I was inside. He's well. He's in a blue recliner."

She shut her eyes.

"He asked me to tell you one thing. He said to say it exactly. The anode rod on the number one heater in the basement. It's due."

For a long time she did not move. Then her face folded, from the mouth first, and she brought both hands up over it, the glasses sliding off her knees onto the carpet, and a sound came out through her fingers that I hope never to be responsible for again.

"He said that in January 2016," she said. "At the table. He said it on a Thursday and I told him to hush."

"I know. He said."

"He said I'd the roofer on my mind." She took her hands from her face and looked at them as though they were somebody's she was minding. "Did he say anything else?"

I thought of a man with his hand over his eyes. I thought of the shape the sentence had taken in his moustache. "He started to," I said. "He said, just the rod."

She nodded, a slow, wet, practical nod, and the nod went on a moment too long. Then she turned over onto one knee, and with a hand on the baseboard got up, and stood, not quite facing the door. She put her palm flat on the plaster at the height of a man's shoulder, seated, in a chair, and left it there. She did not say anything. I picked her glasses up off the carpet, folded them, and set them on the mat of 4A, where she would find them.
