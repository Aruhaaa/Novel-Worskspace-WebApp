# The Tenant of Record

The doorknob was warm. I mean warm the way a hand is warm when it has been round a cup, and I took my fingers back and looked at them as if they might be the ones at fault. Then I touched it again. Brass, worn pale on the side where a thumb goes, a hotel knob from the nineteen-twenties on a door that, at nine o'clock that evening, had been a fire-hose cabinet with a paper inspection tag on its nozzle.

"Is locked," Rafa said.

"How do you know?"

"Because it is a door." He held the key out on his flat palm, the green tag curled against his wrist. "Six years I put oil in that lock every morning. Is a good lock. Nobody has ever asked it anything."

The key was cold. I had expected warm, and was obscurely relieved. "You're not coming in."

"I only oil it." He pushed both hands into the pockets of his canvas coat, a man making sure of where they were. I saw then that he had a folding chair under his arm; it had been there in the elevator, and I had taken it for part of the coat. He shook it open on the maroon carpet and sat with his knees apart and the thermos between his boots. "Mira. I turned this key once, for her. A man gets one. If she asks for me, tell her the lock is good."

"I'll tell her."

"And come out."

"Technically that's the plan."

"Is not a plan," said Rafa. "Is a hope. Go."

The key went in without being asked twice. The lock turned over with a sound like a drawer finding its track, and the door opened inward on no hinge I could hear, onto a brown light, the light inside a paper bag held up to a window. The smell came first. Toast, a great deal of wet wool, and under both something I knew and could not place until I was over the sill: an iron radiator that has been running since before I was born.

I counted. I counted aloud, under my breath, with my lips moving, as I had at the Annex and on the stairs at Tolliver and at my own bathroom door. One put me over the sill. By three the corridor light had thinned behind me to a yellow stripe on the boards, which were oak, narrow, worn into a shallow path down the middle by sixty years of somebody walking to a window and back. The room was long and eleven feet across, I judged, with a hotel's high ceiling. At the far end rain ran down the glass in a steady film with no wind behind it, and the light it let through was the brown of weak tea. The window had curtains. They were oatmeal, lined, hung in the bay as if it were an ordinary bay, and I knew how wide they were without measuring. Thirty-eight by sixty-two.

Five, six. Under the window stood an iron radiator, and beside it a card table with a hot plate on it and a brown pot on the hot plate. At the hot plate, with her back to me, a tall woman in a cardigan was doing something with a kettle. There were two chairs, a wing chair with the nap rubbed off its arms and a kitchen chair with a cushion tied to the seat. On the wall above the radiator ran a shelf of teacups, white, ten of them, in a row, and above the shelf a clock. It said ten past four. The second hand was not moving, and gave the impression of not intending to.

Seven. Through the walls on both sides, or near them, if walls was the word, there were other noises. A woman hummed three notes and then the same three. A pipe was being tapped, slowly, with something that wore a ring. A man a long way off whistled the opening bars of a tune and gave it up, and under all of it, steady as a refrigerator, someone slept.

Eight. Nine. My knees met the iron of the radiator, and I said "Sorry" to it.

"It's used to that, dear," said the woman at the hot plate, without turning round. "You took your time."

She turned. She was a head taller than the figure I had watched lift a curtain from across 31st Street, or else the street had made her small, and she stooped as I do, from desks. Short gray hair, cut by someone who valued speed. Reading glasses on a cord. The hands were the hands I remembered, large, with a writer's knuckles, one holding the kettle in a dishcloth and the other held out a little, palm up, as I had seen it held out to the rain. The cardigan was oatmeal. The third button down was missing, and a bit of blouse showed through the gap like a fact about her that she wasn't hiding.

"I came as soon as the door—"

"I meant fourteen months, dear. The door was only the last four minutes."

I had prepared three openings in the elevator, and each began with the word *technically*. I used none of them. I put my hand into my shirt pocket, where it had been since eleven o'clock, when I took it out of the drawer without deciding to, and held the horn button out on my palm.

She looked at it for about as long as it takes to read a short line. "Third from the top," she said. "It went in the spring of 2011. I kept it in the drawer to sew back on, and never did, and I think I was holding a place." She closed my fingers over it with her large dry hand. "Keep it for me, would you? I'd only lose it again. Is the tin still in the drawer?"

"Two lemon drops. They've fused."

"They do that. Sit down. You're a color I don't care for."

The wing chair had a dent in it the shape of somebody else. I said "Sorry" to that too, and she said "Oh, he's fine," and put a cup into my hands, plain white, with a chip in the handle. The tea was strong enough to stand a spoon in. It was the best I have had, which I put down to the hour and to the fact that it was the first thing anyone had given me in a month without a form attached.

"Not Mr Grieve's, if you're wondering," she said, lowering herself onto the kitchen chair and arranging the cardigan over the gap. "That's out of my tin. But what he leaves on the ledge at four goes down the room warm, and I'll say for this building that in thirty-six years it hasn't once come cold. I can't tell you how I know. One does." She looked at me over the glasses. "How is he?"

"He's dying," I said, because the tea had taken my manners. "Pleural. Four months, he says, six if he's agreeable."

"He would say that." She nodded slowly. "He told me once in the lobby he'd outlast the boiler. I never learned which of them he meant to spite."

I held the cup in both hands. "The note said I was late. Late for what?"

"For reading you, dear. I've been reading you since August of last year." She reached under the card table and brought up a stack of white slips held with a rubber band, and put it on her knee. The top one said HT-4B-0825 in a printed box, and under it, *Received by D. Mahajan*, and in the date the seven had its bar through the stem. "The tenant's copy. Fourteen of those, and the rest is whatever else you've sent me, which, dear, has mostly been mugs."

"I haven't sent you any mugs."

"You cross your sevens. Somebody taught you."

"My mother."

"Then she'd want you to know it's a lovely seven." She said it as one reads out a figure, and it arrived as comfort, and I looked into the tea.

"I thought I'd found it," I said. "The room. I thought it was here all along and nobody had looked."

"It was here all along. It was always here. It was a row that wanted a door, dear, and you filled it in."

"I typed a one."

"You typed a one in the right box, and it stands as long as you hold the desk. Ellis held it open from 'eighty-six and I from 'ninety-nine, and for anyone who'd attended, there it was in the corridor, plain as a cupboard. When a clerk is filed, the door shuts behind them and the number goes dead. Mine shut behind me on a Friday in August. It stayed shut six years. Then someone typed a one."

"Filed."

"Put in the room. It's what the building would call it, if it called it anything. I find I say the word without feeling it."

I set the cup down on the arm of the chair, where it left a ring that I immediately wished to apologize for. "I worked something out in a notebook," I said. "On Monday. Everything I enter is paid for out of something I wasn't looking after."

"Yes."

"That's all? Yes?"

"The books must balance, dear. It isn't a punishment. It's accounting." She said it with the tenderness of a woman who has had to say a hard thing so often it has become a household phrase. "You've found the cheap end of it. Spare keys, a card from a bookshop, a mug. It takes what you are holding least, and not by what it weighs, and if the sum is bigger than the thing it takes, the difference is simply lost. It doesn't give change. And nothing is destroyed. I want you to hear that part, because I took a long time to. Nothing is destroyed. It's filed."

She nodded past my shoulder.

I had taken the long plank along the wall for a baseboard. It ran from the radiator past the wing chair and a good way down the room at the height of my waist, on iron brackets, and along it things had been set out like a museum that could not afford labels. Each had a paper tag tied on with string. Each tag was in capitals I could have drawn blind. A ring of keys: A-017, the spare to 3F, and the side-door key to a house in Edison. A bookstore card, twenty-five dollars. A gym tag. A white mug, the second of a set of two, A-052. On a nail by itself hung a single key, and now I saw the color of its head, which was blue rubber, and which I had no memory of whatever.

Beyond my things the plank went on. Tortoiseshell glasses. A cassette player with the tape still in it. A man's wristwatch stopped at ten past nine. Beyond those, a pair of knitting needles stuck in a ball of gray wool, a hymnal, a church cookbook, a Polaroid of a cat on a windowsill. Twenty-one years of Margaret Dunleavy fitted on four feet of board.

At the end, where my things stopped, lay a microcassette the size of a book of matches. It had a label on it, in my capitals. I did not read the label. I knew the length of it, and where the line broke, and I looked at the tape for as long as I could and then at her.

"Yes," said Margie. "Those go first. I'm sorry."

I nodded. I could not think of anything to say that would not have been an apology to a cassette.

"Twenty-one years, I had," she said, after a while, to the radiator. "There came a day I looked along that board and there was nothing on it that I hadn't been using. I'd run through what I neglected and I'd started on what I loved, and I won't pretend I was brave. Then I had the room."

"I could quit," I said. It came out in the voice of a man asking where the restroom is.

"Of course you could, dear. Nobody's chained you. Then someone else sits in that chair and types, or no one does, and the pipes freeze, as they did in my absence. I heard them go. You'd be surprised what comes through a radiator." She turned the kettle a quarter turn on the plate. "I'm not telling you what to do. I'm telling you what it costs, which was my whole job for twenty years and I was reasonably good at it."

"How many of you are there?"

"Ten, dear. Mr Ambler's the oldest tenant, April of 'sixty-one. Miss Pruitt, who does the humming, 'sixty-eight. Mr Haraldsen, who taps. 'Seventy-two. Miss Marrero, who sings when she's in voice, 'seventy-five. Mr Tipps, the whistler, 'seventy-nine. Miss Fiske, who says her lines, 'eighty-three. Ellis, in 'ninety-eight, and you've read his note. Mr Salazar, who snores like an honest man, 2003. Mr Whitmore, 2016." She stopped. "And myself."

"Whitmore," I said aloud. It came out sounding like a tool.

"Ask Noreen how long she's been not asking."

I opened my mouth, and she shook her head very slightly. "You've worn the face since the socks, dear. It isn't a hard face to read."

There was a little rain on the glass. I told her the rest because there was nothing in my arrangements to stop me. That Corvane was being sold. That the buyer was called Larchmont, and was going through the books with a fine comb, and that on the thirty-first the roll would freeze, and a row that would not validate was a row someone in capitals wanted deleted. That I thought it did not stop at a delete button. There was an integration, a script, that would clean the roll, and I did not have a date for it, and I had not wanted to find out.

She heard it with her hands in her lap. When I had finished she took her glasses off and let them fall on their cord, and looked at the window, and the bay, and the curtains that Zofia had sewn for her.

"Then they'll unmake us," she said. "Ten of us and a radiator." It was very quiet in the room. Somebody, far down, snored, and turned over. "I've had six years. I'd have liked to see a spring. But I'm not asking, dear."

"I'm not going to let them."

"Don't promise. You'll only have to keep it." She put the glasses back on, and the whole face went back to a ledger, and I saw how much work the ledger did. "Go on. You've been here forty minutes by that clock, and that clock is not the sort one relies on. My regards to Rafa. Tell him the lock is good."

"He said you'd say that."

"Did he?" And she smiled for the first time, and it changed her from a column of figures into someone's aunt.

I thanked her. I stood, and said sorry to the chair, and walked the length of the room with the horn button in my fist. I was thinking about a spring. I was thinking about a plank of things, and a name that sounded like a wrench, and a tape I had not read. When my hand found the knob it was the corridor, and the door had shut behind me with the small sound of a drawer, and I was standing on the maroon carpet in the light of the fourth floor.

The flap in the door, the one marked LETTERS, lifted an inch. "Count in, count out, dear," said a voice through the slot, a good voice, level and kind. It was late. I was already in the corridor.

I had not counted. I stood with my mouth open and tried to find the number, as you reach for a step that is not there, and it was not anywhere. I did not know whether I had taken nine or twelve or twenty.

Rafa was asleep on the folding chair with his chin on his chest and the thermos between his boots. A newspaper lay open across his knees. It was the thick one, the Sunday edition, with the date at the top of every page, and I read it twice. SUNDAY, OCTOBER 26.

It had been Thursday. It had been a quarter past two on a Thursday morning, and I had gone in and said sorry to a radiator.

My pocket buzzed against my hip, once, and went on buzzing. I got the phone out. For the length of a breath the lock screen was lit, and across the telephone icon was a red circle with a number in it, and beside the number a name. 41. Anju. Then the little battery in the corner went red, and the screen went black in my hand, and I stood in a corridor that smelled of toast, looking at a reflection of a man with his mouth open.

The first thing I did was count the diamonds in the carpet between us and the elevator. It was the only arithmetic I could still do. Eleven.
