# A Salesman from Dayton

The first thing I heard in 1961 was a ball game, described by a man who sounded pleased about the weather, through a radio that was just short of out of range of itself. It was playing to a room with nobody in it. I will come to the room. The morning comes first, and the morning is why I went.

At nine on Monday the conference TV in the management office showed Randall Pugh against a white village on a cliff, with a dome of such a final blue that it seemed to have been put in afterward. Santorini. He wore a navy quarter-zip and the tan of a man who has been told he looks well in it. Noreen sat on my left, with her lanyard in both hands, and I on her right, and between us on the table lay a form that had been printed that morning and had my name on it.

> Corvane Property Group | Documented Coaching Record | Employee: MAHAJAN, DEVENDRA | Position: Rent Roll Clerk | Concern: Unplanned absence (2 days, 10/23 and 10/24); unresponsive to management contact (1 day, 10/25) | Expectation: Reliable attendance; respond to management contact within 4 business hours | Review: 30 days

> Employee acknowledgment: signature required | Manager: N. Whitmore

"I'm not here to punish," Pugh said, in the voice of a man who has said it before. "This is a coaching conversation, Dev. We're coaching. I'd just ask going forward that when bandwidth is limited you let Noreen know."

"I'm sorry. I was unwell."

"Totally. Totally get it." He had stopped looking at me; he was looking at a corner of his own screen. "Okay. Timeline, so we're all on the same page, and I've also put it in an email because I know how you love an email." He read it out anyway.

> From: R. Pugh | To: N. Whitmore; D. Mahajan | Subject: DATA ROOM FREEZE FRI 10/31: 211 DQ FLAGS (ACTION REQUIRED)

> Team, going forward the timeline is as follows. Fri 10/31: data room freezes, all 211 DQ flags cleared. Wed 11/12: Larchmont physical walk-through, Harwick Terrace. Fri 11/21, 02:10: Larchmont integration run, incl. orphan-unit purge. HT-4B is on the exception schedule for that run, so Dev, nothing for you to do on 4B. The integration runs under the existing RRCLERK service credentials. Just the flags. Let's not boil the ocean. R.

I read it twice, the second time for the number, and found I had memorized it. 02:10. I thought of the lobby clock stopping, and the pipes, and the radiator under my window, and of Margie turning a kettle a quarter turn on a plate, saying *then they'll unmake us*, and I wrote nothing on the form. I signed it. I signed D. Mahajan in the blue box, and Noreen's keys, which had been silent through the whole call, gave a single chime.

When the screen went to its blue Corvane standby, Noreen stayed in her chair. She took a butterscotch disc from the dish on the counter, which was not within her reach, and I watched her get up, and fetch it, and sit again.

"You'll want to be careful what you enter, hon," she said.

"Careful how?"

"Let's put that in a drawer." She unwrapped the disc. The cellophane sounded very loud. "Mrs Han wants to know if the lobby can have orange lights on Friday. For the party. I told her I'd ask the fire marshal."

* * *

I tried to give the flags away. I am not proud of how hard I tried. Joyce was at her cubicle with the calculator, doing something with the old notes in their sleeves, and I put the laptop in front of her and said that there were forty rows of formatting that anyone could do.

"Noted," said Joyce. Then, after what looked like a negotiation: "I would, Dev. I can't. I've tried to change a phone number in there since go-live. It says *View*." She tipped her head. "It says it kindly."

Espie, at the leasing desk, was laying out an application fan, nails the color of cooked beets with a gold fleck. "Ok so I literally cannot edit a tenant," she said. "I'm leasing. I lease. But I could, like, caption them?"

Pugh's answer to my email came back in eleven minutes and was a single line, and I will set it down exactly, because it is the nearest thing in this note to a statement of policy.

> RE: DELEGATION OF DQ FLAGS | SEGREGATION OF DUTIES: ONLY THE CLERK EDITS TENANT MASTER DATA. NO EXCEPTIONS (AUDIT). R.

Noreen read it over my shoulder. She had come up without her keys chiming, which I have only otherwise seen her do on the evening of the sixth. "A clerk who misses the freeze," she said, very quietly, "is a clerk Randall replaces. And whoever he gets will have a delete button and a boss who's allergic to a red row." She looked at the ceiling, toward four. "Don't give him the reason, hon."

I considered the arithmetic. Two hundred and eleven flags in five days is forty-two a day, and I had learned on a Monday three weeks earlier what a single trailing space cost. I decided I could afford a few a day. A few a day was what I told myself on Monday, and I did not do the larger sum until Tuesday, and I would like it on the record that I knew I was doing that.

I cleared six before lunch. They were the cheapest I could find: a ZIP code with the plus-four missing, a lease date saved as text, an apartment letter in lower case. I watched each one go from amber to green. I learned in the morning what the six had cost. The building took a ring of keys from a nail by my door, four apartments' worth, from places I had left, and I could not have told you what any of them opened. It took the second drawer in the kitchen, with the takeout menus, the soy sauce packets, and the old cracked gray phone; not the contents. The drawer. There was a clean-edged hole in the cabinet the size of a shoebox, and in it, when I knelt and looked, was the back of the cabinet, painted, as if the cabinet had never had a drawer there to be missed. The label maker lived in the other one, and was fine. I was grateful to it in a way I can't defend.

* * *

At lunch I went and looked at the row. I do not know why I had not done it first thing. In Tenantry, where for fourteen months it had said OCCUPANT, the Tenant field read AMBLER, T. (MR).

I stood with my sandwich and looked at it for some time. I told myself it was the tea. A plate goes out onto the landing at four every afternoon, and has since 1989, and I had begun to suspect that the building's idea of who stood nearest the door was whoever was being thought of hardest by someone outside it. Nobody in Harwick thought harder, or more regularly, or with more manners, than an old man with a saucer.

Rafa gave me the key off its hook without a word. He turned a clipboard around on the bench for me to sign, which I had not known he kept, and under Purpose I wrote *Depends* and he nodded.

The radio was on the nightstand, a walnut set the size of a bread box, with a cloth front and a dial lit amber, and a man on it was saying that there was a runner on second and a good deal of wet in the air. The paper on the walls was cream with narrow green stripes. There was a bed with a chenille spread, a luggage rack with a gray sample case on it, a bow tie hanging on a hook by the mirror, a black telephone on the night table with its cord laid straight. The calendar over the desk was a free one from a hardware store in Dayton. The days of April up to the tenth had been crossed off in pencil. The eleventh was circled, and nothing came after it.

A man in shirtsleeves sat on the edge of the bed with one hand resting on the telephone, the way you keep a hand on a sleeping child's back to know when it turns.

"Come in, come in, shut the door, you're letting the hall in." He was forty-one by the look of him, with a round, quick, pleasant face and a side parting wet with comb, suspenders, a collar open at the neck. He stood and put out his hand. "You'll be from the desk. I rang down about the biscuits. Ambler. Tad. Rosewell Dental Supply, Dayton."

"Dev Mahajan. I'm in the, I do the records."

"A records man! Good, good, somebody has to." He shook my hand with the whole arm. "Sit, there's the chair. Don't mind the case." He glanced at my sneakers and did not ask.

I did not sit. I had counted nine from the door to the window, under his patter, and written the nine in the notebook before I let myself look at anything else, with the pencil I had carried since the Annex. It was the right number, and I was glad of it, as one is glad of a handrail. Now I stood at the glass and looked at 31st Street in 1961. A green awning stood over the entrance, with HARWICK in gold script, and a doorman in a long coat under it held an umbrella for nobody. The road was wet and black. A tan Pontiac as long as a boat idled at the curb with its wipers going, and a woman with a hat tied under her chin stopped at the hydrant to shift her bag, and in the window opposite, where there is a laundromat now, I saw a barber's pole.

"Lively house," said Ambler at my shoulder. "A woman hums in the hall at all hours, and an old Norwegian taps the pipes, and I've asked down about the whistling. They say they'll look into it." He chuckled. "I'm in no hurry. I'm expecting a call at a quarter past four."

"From Dayton."

"My wife. Lorna." He looked at the telephone, and his hand went to it again. "She hasn't answered my cables, you see. Twelve I've sent. It's the lines, I expect, they said at the desk it's the lines. She'll ring at four-fifteen, she knows I'm here till Friday, and she's a stickler. She'd not leave a man hanging." He said it lightly. It was the lightness I had heard all my life from my father on the subject of the tomatoes.

I asked what the date was. It was the eleventh of April. I asked what year, and he said, "Well! I hardly know, with the way I move about. Sixty-one." He was not being funny. I looked at the calendar, and at the clock beside it, which said ten past four, and at his face, and said nothing at all, and I think it was the decent thing and I think I will never be sure.

"Now," said Ambler, brightening, and went to the sample case. "I can't let a man leave without a sale. It's a rule. Floss. Waxed or unwaxed? The mint's new this spring, you'll like the mint, the dentists swear by it."

I bought the mint. I had a dollar in my wallet, and he held it to the lamp, and turned it, the way Joyce holds a five-dollar bill.

"Funny-looking dollar. They've done something to the eagle." He wrote me up in an order book with a pencil on a string, *Mr D. Mahajan, 1 pkt. floss, mint, 35c*, and he tore the slip off and put it in my hand and put the book away. He did not make change. I remembered afterward that the building does not, and I was in the middle of wondering whether that was the building or the man.

"A fellow leaves me tea, you know," Ambler said, when I was at the door. "Every afternoon at four, on the sill, there's a cup, and a biscuit, a plain one, and I don't know who. A good man. I turn the saucer left, to say it's good tea, and I can't recall that anyone ever told me to. I should like to thank him. Tell him if you meet him he's welcome to come up."

"I'll tell him."

"Do," said Ambler. "Mind the stairs, they're wet."

I counted out, window to door. Nine. Same corridor, same maroon lozenges, and my phone said 1:21, and I had gone in at ten to one by the same phone. I stood on the carpet with a packet of mint floss in one hand and a pencil slip in the other, and the door shut itself behind me with its small sound. I had lost nothing but the thirty-one minutes I had spent, and I could account for every one.

* * *

I went to 6A at six. Mrs Han had been, and left a thermos on the tray, and Mr Grieve sat in the wing chair by the window with a rug over his knees, in the three-piece suit, with the duck-head cane across his lap, his hearing aids whistling very faintly as he turned his head. The mantel clocks all said different times, and each of them was slow.

I told him all of it, the way Ellis Kwan might have: the room, the wallpaper, the radio, the telephone, the calendar. I said that the gentleman was well. That he was courteous, and talked a good deal, and had sold me floss. That he took his tea. That he turned the saucer left every afternoon, because he thought it was good tea, and that he did not remember being told to. That he would like to thank the man who leaves it, and that the man was welcome to come up.

Mr Grieve did not say anything for what must have been a minute. He looked at the window, where the sky was going out over the alley, and his hand on the cane was quite still. He coughed once into his handkerchief, as one opens a drawer, and put the handkerchief away and folded it first.

"Did he say," he asked, "whether it needed sugar?" His voice had gone very formal, as it does when it is trying to hold something level. "Thirty-six years I have wondered about the sugar."
