# Everyone Came as Something

Somebody had put plastic spiders in every drawer of the dead doorman's desk, and by half past five that Friday they had all been found. They sat in a row along the blotter, black, hairy, the size of a thumb, each one placed with the care of a person who thinks it is funny to have been the finder. Behind them, on the lobby bench, Mrs Han had laid out her trays.

There were rice cakes in three colors, pink and white and a green that tasted of mugwort, and squares of jelly cut with the office box-cutter into orange and black, and Joyce's shortbread, which Joyce had wrapped in orange cellophane and left without a note. Orange lights had been strung along the mailbox wall with the fire marshal's blessing, which turned out to mean that they were LED. Mrs Han stood behind the bench like a general behind a map. She had a pointed hat over the jade pin and the thermos in the crook of her arm and a phone held out in front of her at the length of her arm, so that she could see the chat without her glasses.

I had cleared the last flag at 4:51. I want that on the record, because it was the last thing I did on that machine as a person who owed it anything. The banner had come up green across the whole of the screen, and I had read it with my hand flat on the desk.

I went to the party as a spreadsheet. Espie had said that nobody came to a party as a person, and I had made a grid from a flattened box, in four rows of six, with A1 in black marker over my heart and a cardboard arrow on a headband for the cursor, and I walked through the lobby doors in it like a man delivering a very small fence.

"Mr Mahajan," said Mrs Han. "You are late and you are a cell. Eat."

She put a plate into my hands. Then everybody said my name. I do not know how else to give the sense of it. Owen from 2F, under a bedsheet with two eyeholes and a pair of headphones, said "Dev! Spreadsheet guy!" and Espie, a bee in black-and-yellow tights with a pair of felt wings, the nails striped to match, said "Dev, no because that is so good, hold still," and filmed me. Zofia came up in her man's gray cardigan with a tape measure worn across her chest like a sash. She said that she had come as a seamstress, and had been told that this was a costume, and did not understand. Rafa stood by the mailboxes in a black hoodie with white felt bones stitched to it, front and back, by a woman who has never in her life done a thing carelessly, and the ribs were exactly spaced. He raised his paper cup to me. He did not say anything, but he raised it, with the toothpick behind his ear.

"Dev," said Chioma, from the bench. She wore a green scrub top and the clogs and the red frames, and she had pinned a sign to her chest that read OFF SHIFT. DO NOT ASK ABOUT MY LUNG. "For the record, you came as a cell. You are a cell. There's no joke in it."

"There's a small joke."

"There's a pun." She handed me a napkin. "Eight o'clock, Mahajan. I'll take it in person tonight."

Owen cornered me by the jelly. Owen Fenske is a graduate student in something to do with watersheds, and he complains the way other people breathe, and I will say that I had always liked him for the consistency. "Someone fixed my name on the portal," he said. "There was a space after it. Fenske-space. For two years, I've been Fenske-space. I put in four tickets. Last week it was just gone."

"That was me," I said.

He looked at me with his eyes through the sheet, and put a hand on my cardboard shoulder, which crumpled. "Then you're my favorite person in the building," he said. "Also the lights are the wrong orange."

I had paid for that space with a spare key, in the first week of the experiment, and I did not tell him.

Mr Grieve sat in the folding chair nearest the radiator with the cane across his knees and a rice cake on a napkin he had not touched, in the brown tweed three-piece, with a card pinned to the lapel. COURT REPORTER, it said, in a very small neat hand. "Strictly speaking, I have come as myself at a more respectable hour," he said. "Mrs Han tells me I am to eat something with a pink center and hold no opinions. I find that I am equal to the second." He looked at my grid for a time. "You are a most convincing cell, Mr Mahajan. I should hate to be the one to merge you."

* * *

I should say what the week had cost, because I was a man with a plate in his hand in a lobby of people who knew his name, and I have to put it on the page somewhere. On Tuesday the building took a toolbox I had never opened. On Wednesday, a box of spare light bulbs and the liner for the shower curtain. On Thursday it took the second chair, the folding one that stood behind the door for company, and the table at Tolliver Court was set for one for the first time honestly. And on Friday morning, on my way to the bus, I noticed that the bicycle cage at the foot of Building F held a chain lock, still locked, looped through the rack and through nothing at all. I would have told you I did not own a bicycle. I had owned one. It was blue, it had ten speeds, and I had ridden it last in a university town, and the lock sat there still fastened, the way a coat is left on a chair to say that a seat is taken.

That was the bicycle. The other thing I found out at six.

Anjali called as she had called every day since the twenty-sixth, and I took it on the stairwell landing with the door to the lobby open an inch on the noise of rice cakes and a bee. She was in the kitchen in Raleigh; I could hear the extractor fan. She said Tia had been told about the costume and had demanded a photograph of a spreadsheet. She said she had a long day. She said, "Ben says hi."

"Tell—" I said.

There was nothing after it. I stood on the landing with my mouth in the shape of the next sound, and I heard myself breathe in the gap, and then I said, "Tell him hi back."

"Tell him yourself on Sunday," she said. "He wants to see the arrow."

When she had gone I scrolled the thread with my thumb, back and back, forty screens of it, past the fourteenth and the twelfth, through recipe cards and a photograph of Tia's tooth, until a text from the twelfth of October gave me a line. *Ben says you have to let it settle.* I read the word the way you read a plumber's name on a work order. Ben. I said it twice in the stairwell, and it was two sounds I had been told. I went to the building chat, which I had muted for fourteen months, and opened it, and typed *Does anyone*, and looked at the cursor for as long as I could stand it. Forty people, any of whom might have a sister. I deleted the words and went back into the lobby.

* * *

By half past eight Noreen was not in it. She had been there at seven, in a pair of black cat ears with a felt tail pinned to the seat of her slacks and whiskers drawn on with eyeliner, and she had taken three butterscotch discs from the bowl on the bench that nobody had put there, and by eight-thirty the plate sat where she had left it. Mrs Han lowered the phone. "Mr Mahajan," she said. "You will find Mrs Whitmore."

Left was on the ground floor with its doors open and its light on, and it was a Friday, and it did not stick. I rode up with my arrow crumpled against the rail. The display read 4, and then, for as long as it takes to blink, 4B, and then 4.

She was at the far side of the corridor, facing the wall. She stood nose to the wood with her left hand flat on it, at the height of a man's chest, the keys in her right hand silent, and the cat ears bright against the beige. I should say that to Mrs Han, or to Espie's camera, or to anyone in the lobby with a phone, there was a hose cabinet there and Noreen Whitmore standing in front of it in a headband. I could see the door because I had been through it. So could she.

"You can see it," she said, without turning round.

"Since the twenty-third."

"I saw it for three years, hon. From January of 2016 until August of 2019, plain as a cupboard. Then she went in, and it was a cabinet again. Six years of a red cabinet with a hose, and my husband inside it. You don't know what that was." She took her hand off the wood and looked at the palm. "Thursday week I came off the elevator and there it was again. Somebody had given it back its knob."

"Mrs Han sent me with—" I said, and did not have the end of the sentence.

"Tell her I'm powdering my nose." In her right fist the lanyard moved, and I saw that one of the fourteen keys had been drawn out and held apart, a small brass key with a green tag, the twin of Rafa's. "Margie had it cut in 2016. I'd asked her where Gary was, and she looked at me a long time, and then went to the hardware on Merrow with her coat on. Nine years I've carried that. I've never once put it in the lock."

I said her name, and she said "Okay, okay, okay," in the way she does when she's going to be polite until it kills her, and told me what it was.

"January the sixteenth," Noreen said. "A Saturday. Ten past four, I was in the office because we had a leak in 6C, and I was on the phone with a roofer who wanted to know if I understood the term *flashing*. Gary came in with my lunch. He did it Saturdays, because he knew I'd forget, a Tupperware of chicken and rice, with the lid on. He stood at the counter. He said something. I had my hand over the other ear. I said, 'Put it on the counter.'" She said it in the voice she must have used then, brisk, absent, kind. "I didn't look up. That is the whole crime. I didn't look up."

"Mr Grieve thinks—"

"I know what Mr Grieve thinks." She laughed, one hard note. "You think I put him in the wall. Everybody does. I could see him think it every time I took his mail up. He's a man who takes down what he hears, and he's heard the word *wall* a great many times in forty years."

"I didn't," I said. "I mean I did. For about a week."

"Well, it's a good theory. It has a body." She leaned her shoulder on the plaster beside the door. "When I put the phone down it was twenty to five and the office was empty and the Tupperware was on the counter with the lid still on. His truck was in the lot until Monday. Margie came in at five past, from the filing room, and said, 'Noreen, dear, I think you'd better sit.' She was good. She was kind, even. She told me about the clerks, and the room, and what happens to a man who stands in the wrong place in this building at ten past four with nobody looking at him." She looked at the door. "I've known since that afternoon, hon, and I have not once asked. That's the part I want to be accurate about. I don't know how you'd get him out, or whether you can. I know what I'd rather not be told."

I thought about a woman of fifty-eight who had been pinning a joke over a hole for nine years. I thought about the interview, the form on the desk, my voice in the little room saying *my sister, I guess*.

"I hired you knowing," she said, as though I had said it aloud. "The building was dying, Dev. The pipes froze twice in the winter of 'twenty-two. We were at eighty-one percent and falling, and nobody in the building was keeping the books. And I asked you who we'd call. I'd asked it of nine men. I wrote down 'sister,' and then I wrote 'guess,' and I underlined it, because I thought: that's a man nobody will miss. God forgive me, I was relieved."

"No worries," I said.

It came out on its own, like a hiccup, and we both heard it. Noreen laughed once, a bark, and put her hand over her mouth, and her eyes above the hand were wet. "Oh, hon."

"I'm sorry, I didn't mean—"

"Don't you dare apologize to me." She wiped under her eyes with the side of a finger, careful of the whiskers. "And the one. 'Put a one, it won't hurt anything.' I knew what it'd cost, I've watched it cost Margie for twenty years. I wanted that door open. I'd have said anything. I said it to your face."

The elevator opened at the far end. Chioma came down the corridor with the sign on her chest, DO NOT ASK ABOUT MY LUNG, at the pace of a woman who has been on her feet at night for ten years and has learned to arrive without hurrying. She looked at Noreen in her cat ears and at me in my cardboard, and last at the door, the beige door, with a quarter inch of lamplight under it. She did not say hello.

"For the record," said Chioma, "I have questions."

And she sat down on the carpet with her back against the plaster, folded her long legs, put her phone face down beside her, and waited, like a person at a bedside who has all night.

Noreen looked at her. Then she looked at the key in her hand with its green tag. "He's got a blue recliner in there, I'd bet money on it," she said. "He always wanted one, and I said no, it's a bad advertisement for the marriage." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she did a thing with her jaw. "I wash his socks on the weekend. I leave them on the dryer for Thursday."

Nobody said anything. Chioma reached out, without looking, and took hold of the hem of Noreen's cardigan, as you would take the sleeve of someone about to step into a road, and held on.

I did not stay. It was not mine. I went down in Left with the arrow bent over my chest and, in the lobby, sat on the bench among the orange lights, where the party was thinning into coats and foil, and took out my phone.
