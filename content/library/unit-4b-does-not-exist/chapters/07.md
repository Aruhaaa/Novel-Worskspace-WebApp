# The Extra Washer

The light in the Harwick laundry room is the orange of a parking structure at night. It comes from a single fixture in a wire cage, and under it everything looks like a photograph of itself taken in 1974. Six washers stand along the east wall with their lids up. Six dryers face them across a strip of linoleum the color of weak tea. At the far end, past the last washer, where the wall turns toward the boiler, there is a seventh, and it is avocado green.

I got there at 6:55 on Thursday evening with a notebook, a pencil, my phone and a printout folded in thirds. "Thursdays," Rafa had told me on Wednesday, at the foot of the basement stairs, with the toothpick traveling from one corner of his mouth to the other. "Bring nothing." I had taken this for a figure of speech. To be fair to myself, I had considered a thermos, a folding chair and a second pencil, and had held myself to the one pencil.

Each machine wore a card reader. The coin changer on the wall had said OUT OF ORDER on a strip of masking tape since before my time. A folding table stood in the middle of the floor with a laminated sign taped to it, and on the lid of dryer 4 lay a white bra, underwired, which has lain there since before Labor Day. Every resident of Harwick has agreed not to see it. It is the nearest thing the building has to a constitution.

> LAUNDRY ROOM 7 AM TO 10 PM. Please remove lint. Please do not dye. Please do not sit on the machines. Items left over 48 hours will be donated. Thank you, The Management.

Rafa sat on a folding chair beside the green machine with a steel thermos between his boots and a canvas bag across his knees. The bag was the size and shape of a sleeping dog. Stenciled on it, in blue gone to gray, were the words HARWICK HOTEL LINEN SVC.

"You brought a notebook," he said.

"You said nothing."

"A notebook is nothing. Sit."

I did not sit. I went to look at the green washer and read the plate on it. It was brass, the size of a playing card, screwed to the panel above the dial and engraved in the plain capitals of an old trophy shop. It said 4B. The enamel had chipped at one corner and showed white underneath, and the screws had been painted over with the rest of the panel more than once, which told me the plate was older than the paint.

I took out the printout. I had pulled it at four that afternoon from Corvane's shared drive, and I had been rather proud of the idea, the way you are of a spare umbrella.

> CORVANE PROPERTY GROUP | Fixed Asset Register | HT / Basement / Laundry | LW-1 to LW-6, washers (6) | LD-1 to LD-6, dryers (6) | Card readers (12) | Folding table (1) | Last physical verification: 30 June | Asset count: 25

I counted the washers, then the lines on the register, then the washers again. Six and six. "Technically," I said, "it isn't on here."

"It's a washer."

"It isn't an asset."

"An asset is a thing somebody wrote down," said Rafa, and shifted the toothpick. "This is a thing that washes."

"Who pays for the electricity?"

"Who pays for anything?" He looked at me over the white tape on his glasses with great gentleness. "You tell me. You have the roll."

At 6:58 he got up, as slowly as he does everything, and lifted the lid of the green machine. The tub was dry and empty. The enamel had gone cream at the rim, and a white plastic cone stood in the center for an agitator. "Look," he said. I looked. I put my hand in, because a man who is shown a thing is entitled to touch it, and the metal was cold. "Behind."

Behind the machine two hoses lay in coils, bound with zip ties, their ends capped with brass fittings that someone had painted the color of the wall. Neither went near a tap. The drain hose hung four inches above the floor drain, over nothing, like a man holding a door for no one.

"Nothing," I said.

"Nothing," Rafa agreed, and shut the lid with the heel of his hand.

At seven exactly by my phone, which keeps carrier time and is therefore six minutes ahead of every clock in the building, the machine started. No coin went in. No card touched the reader. The dial moved itself a quarter turn with a small ratcheting click, and from inside the tub came the sound of a washer filling. It is a loud sound and a familiar one, water meeting a drum. There was no water. I stood with my palm flat on the lid and felt the vibration come up through it, steady and honest, a machine doing the job it was built for.

I opened a spreadsheet on my phone. I am aware of how that sounds, but a man with a stopwatch and a free evening is only a few keystrokes from a spreadsheet. Column A for the phase, column B for the clock. By 7:35 I had four phases down and a growing suspicion that the cycle was an honest one.

"Mira," said Rafa, from his chair. "You are doing math at a miracle."

"I'm timing it."

"You time the bus also?"

"The 14 is twenty-two minutes."

"And does the bus care?"

I put the phone away. A few minutes later I took it out again, in the dark of my pocket, with the screen turned down, like a man checking the score in church.

At twenty past seven Zofia backed through the door, towing a two-wheeled wire cart with her hip. She had on the gray cardigan, the tape around her neck and the pincushion at her wrist, and the cart held a heap of men's slacks, charcoal and navy and one pair of mustard corduroy, each with a pencil tag pinned to the waistband. She did not look at me first. She looked at the green washer, and she gave it a nod, short and level, the kind you give a colleague across a shop when you both have a long evening ahead.

"Tonight is slacks," she said.

The washer went on filling with nothing.

"Rafa. Mister clerk." She had the tape out and round my chest before I had finished saying good evening. "Don't breathe." I didn't. "Same. Is same as Thursday last, no worse." She turned on Rafa. "You."

"Me, no."

"One centimeter more at waist."

"It's the bread."

"Is always the bread," said Zofia, with enormous sympathy, and wrote it in the notebook with the rubber band.

She loaded a third of her cart into washer 2, tapped the reader with a plastic card from her cardigan pocket, where it lived among the pins, and sat on the folding table under the orange light with a needle and a pair of trousers across her lap. She hemmed by hand. She did not look at the green machine again. It was the way you do not look at a pot. Every few minutes her eyes went up to its lid and came down again.

"Do you come every Thursday?" I asked.

"Thursday I wash. She washes. We don't talk." She bit a thread. "Is professional."

From a quarter to eight until about ten past, nothing happened, and I want that on the record, because in my experience it is what most of it is: a basement, a thermos, a woman sewing, a man working a toothpick, and an avocado-green machine going through a perfectly ordinary cycle on nothing. Rafa poured coffee into the cap of the thermos and gave it to me. It tasted of cinnamon and the cap. He told me, apropos of nothing, that the way to wait is not to watch the clock but to watch somebody's hands, and I watched Zofia's, which went in and out of the wool at a speed I will never have at anything, and I felt a little better.

At 8:07 the spin began, a low, stuttering roar that rose and then settled, and the other washers in the row hummed along with it, faintly, as glassware does when a truck goes by. Zofia stopped sewing. Rafa took out the toothpick. I held my phone against my chest. At 8:12 the motor wound down, and a latch inside the lid went over with a flat clunk.

> 19:00 fill | 19:06 wash | 19:28 drain | 19:32 rinse | 19:50 drain | 19:54 spin | 20:12 stop | Total 72:00

I looked at the column for some time. It footed. I do not know what I had expected, but I had not expected arithmetic to be on its side.

Rafa lifted the lid. Warm air came out of the tub, and with it the smell of toast. It was not the smell of laundry. The drum was full.

"Hold the bag," he said, and I held it open.

He took things out one at a time and folded each on the table with his eyes on his hands. They were dry. They were warm, with the particular warmth of cloth taken out of a dryer by someone standing right there, a temperature I had not expected to meet in a basement where no dryer had run. I will list what I saw. Three sleeveless undershirts, white gone ivory, each with a woven red tape at the neck reading T. AMBLER. A chambermaid's apron in gray-blue cotton, with a bib and a pocket, and in the hem, in white thread, V.P. A pair of heavy wool socks, navy, with a ribbed turnover, which I took for a sailor's without knowing why, until I found in the cuff a laundry mark in black thread, N.H., and a number, 72, that could have been a room or a year. A silk scarf, green and gold, printed with small fans, which Rafa folded into a square the size of a coaster.

"You're not reading them," I said.

"Somebody told me a long time ago. Fold it, don't read it." He squared an undershirt. "A man who reads starts to ask."

"I'm reading them."

"Yes," said Rafa. "You're the clerk."

I had seen the name Ambler once before, on a sill, in the mouth of an old man in tweed. I did not say so. I took the first undershirt from the pile and held it by the shoulders, and it hung from my hands like something that had been recently worn by a person of exactly my height.

Zofia had come to stand at my elbow. She held her tape over the red letters without touching the cloth and looked at the stitching on the name. "Hand," she said. "Small stitch. Every stitch same. Somebody who like to do it." She considered. "Wife. Or very good mother."

"Which?"

"I measure. I don't guess." She went back to her slacks.

The last thing in the drum was a balled pair of gray work socks, thick, with a reinforced heel. Rafa put them in my left hand. "Fold," he said. "I do the string."

I folded them the way my mother folded socks, one inside the other, the cuff turned over the toe, and I did not look at them. I was looking at him. He drew the string of the bag tight, knotted it with a single movement of the thumb and swung it over his shoulder. Zofia, behind us, said something to the green machine in Polish, low and brisk, as one says good night to a coworker, and then we were in the corridor, and then in the elevator, and I still had the socks in my hand.

We took Right. The car smelled faintly of toast by the time it reached four. On the fourth floor I counted without wanting to, the way a tongue goes to a tooth: eighteen, which went into the notebook later, between the seventeen of Tuesday and a nineteen I would have the next morning.

Rafa set the bag down at the foot of the hose cabinet, squared to the wall, as you would leave a delivery. He did not look at the wall. He looked down the corridor toward 4C and the blue bicycle, with the courtesy of a man in an elevator pretending not to hear another man's phone call.

Two knocks came from the plaster just left of the cabinet, at chest height. They were unhurried, like someone confirming a delivery. My hand came up. Rafa watched it come.

"Margie used to say," he said, to the bicycle, "if you ever hear the radiator ask a question, answer it."

"Margie Dunleavy." It was the first time I had said the name in front of anyone but Joyce. "What does it ask?"

"Depends who's asking."

I knocked twice on the plaster with my right fist. This time I thought about it first, and it made no difference. Nothing answered. The smell of toast thickened by one degree, the way a kitchen does when the person at the stove turns around. Rafa breathed out through his nose and said, "Good. Now it's polite."

He went to the stairwell door and held it open with a boot, his eyes on the floor. I stood at the cabinet with my left hand shut. When I opened it I had not yet looked at the socks, and now, under the corridor light, I did. They were a man's, heavy, gray, with a darn at one heel in thread that did not quite match. On the cuff of the left one, in black marker, in the upright capitals of a woman who has been told all her life that her handwriting is nice and has made it her business to deserve it, were the words G. WHITMORE.

Whitmore is not a rare name. I had told myself so in 3B, over a carbon of curtains. I told myself so again, and it did less each time. I laid the socks on top of the bag and squared them to its edge, as Rafa had squared the rest.

In my first week, at the coffee machine, with the gum going round, Noreen had told me that her husband had gone out for cigarettes in 2016. "And he's still looking for the right brand, hon," she had said, and laughed, a big laugh that took a moment to get started and then ran a beat too long. I had laughed too, because that is what one does. She had said it the way you pin a joke over a hole.
