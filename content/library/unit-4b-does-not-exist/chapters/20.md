# Anjali Takes Off Her Coat

A small woman in a navy puffy coat was standing at the lobby's glass doors with a wheeled suitcase, looking in.

I had been on the bench since ten. The text at 7:02 said the plane was still at the gate and they kept saying ten minutes. The one at 9:48 said Landed. The one after that said Cab. By my phone it was twenty past eleven, and I saw her before she saw me, which in twenty-two months of Sundays on a small screen had never once happened. She was shorter than the screen makes her. She had the claw clip in and her strong forearms pushed out of the sleeves around the suitcase handle, and she was reading the names on the brass mailboxes through the glass the way she reads a chart.

I went to the doors and pushed one open. "Anju."

She looked straight at me. She gave me her whole attention, with polite concern in it and no recognition at all, the way you look at a stranger who has called you by a name that might be yours. "I'm sorry," she said. "I'm supposed to be meeting someone. I had a name." She patted the pocket of the coat for her phone, and a gap showed between her front teeth when she frowned. "I had it written down."

I stood in the open door with the wet air coming in on my shoulder. I knew exactly what I was looking at, in the sense that I had watched Joyce and Espie do it, and I could not do anything with the knowledge. There was nothing in the rules about saying it to your own sister. She had already heard it from me.

"Dev," said Chioma, behind me, from the elevator. She had come down in the hoodie with the frames pushed into her twists, and she said it without hurry, as she would say it to a patient on the edge of a bed. "Mahajan."

My sister's face changed. It did not brighten. It came back, from wherever it had gone, the way a screen does after a power cut, and she looked at me, and then she hit me in the arm with the heel of a hand that has spent ten years lifting children, and then she took two fistfuls of my raincoat and held on.

"I almost didn't come," she said into the collar. "I booked it, and I sat on that plane with a boarding pass with my own name on it, and I thought, who is this for? I had the dal in my bag and I thought, why is there so much of it."

"You said it on the call. You said my name."

"I know I said it. I remember saying it. I remember crying in the kitchen." She pulled back with her hands still full of coat. "I remember that I was crying, Dev-u. I don't remember who for."

I will put down what I thought, because it was the first thing I thought and I have not found a better one since. As far as I could tell, a name said aloud holds for a while, and a while is not the same as for good. At one in the morning there had been sixty people around me. By the second hour of a flight, with nobody's mouth near her, there had been less of me in my sister than the night before.

* * *

Chioma's kitchen was the width of a hallway, with a table that sat three if two of them liked each other and a red kettle on the back burner. Anjali put the suitcase by the radiator and took out of it a rectangular container with a blue lid, wrapped in a bath towel, a freezer bag and a second towel. She set it on the table the way you set down a sleeping child.

"Dal," she said. "I made it at two in the morning, after. Mummy's, with the lemon at the end." She peeled the lid and the smell came up: cumin in hot ghee, hing, the sourness of the lemon. I held the door frame. I had not smelled it since the winter before last. "I made enough for two," said Anjali. "I stood there with the spoon and I couldn't remember why I'd made it for two."

"For the record," said Chioma, getting down bowls, "that's the best reason I've heard for making anything. I'm Chioma. Sit. Take your coat off, you'll boil."

"I'm fine."

"It is seventy-eight degrees in here. The radiators are, let's say, enthusiastic."

"I'm fine," said my sister, and kept the coat zipped to her chin, and ate.

I told her a version. I had had all of Saturday on the bench to choose which, and I chose the one with the fewest numbers in it. I started with "Technically," and she held up one hand and said, "Start with the building." So I said there was a unit that was not on the plan and was occupied. I said I kept the books, and that what I entered in the books was charged to me, and that it was not money. Small things at first. "What things?" "A mug." "Dev." "Some keys. A gym tag." I did not say the voicemail. I did not say the Rascals or the bicycle or the first name of her husband, which I had had to look up in a text. I did not say the price. I said that last week the building had stopped being able to see me, and that on Saturday night it had been talked out of it.

She listened as she listens to a parent explain a child who does not like to be touched: head a little to one side, hands loose, interrupting only to ask for the next fact. I would estimate that she believed a third of it. The rest of the believing she did with her hands.

"Where's the wall?"

"Which—"

"The wall, Dev. You said it answers."

Chioma lifted one shoulder at me. "Bedroom," she said.

The bed stood against the east wall, and the plaster above the headboard was covered in pencil, in rows, in small square capitals, from the baseboard to as high as a woman can reach lying down. Six years of it. Anjali stood at the foot of the bed in her coat with her arms folded and read, as I have seen her read a school report.

> Rain tonight, Margie. Bus late. Radiator on. Two knocks if you're up. C.

> Rain tonight, Margie. Mama's scan was clear. Told nobody but you.

> Dry tonight, Margie. Cold. Wear the cardigan.

She put her knuckles to the plaster above the pillow, and knocked twice.

From the other side, at the same height, unhurried, two knocks came back.

She did not jump. She went still, in the way that she goes still when a child she is treating does the thing it was not supposed to be able to do. "Okay," said my sister. "Okay." She sat down on the edge of Chioma's bed in the coat, with her hands between her knees. After a while she said to the wall, in the voice she uses to introduce herself to a classroom, "I'm Anjali."

The voice came through the plaster, dry and flat and Midwestern, a little muffled, like a radio in the next room of a house. "Pleased to meet you, dear. You'll be the sister. He crosses his sevens, you know. I thought it must have come from someone."

"Our mother," said Anjali.

"Yes," said the wall. "I thought it must."

* * *

She was quiet through the rest of the dal. Chioma washed up with her back to us, which is how Chioma gives privacy. I waited until the water ran.

"Anju. I have to tell you something and I need you to let me finish."

"You always say that. You never finish."

"In January last year. The night before. She called me at nine forty-one. I was at a trivia night, I'd been asked to make a ninth, and I saw it come up, MUMMY, on the screen. I was in the middle of a question." I looked at the table. "I let it ring."

Anjali did not move. The spoon stayed where it was, over the bowl. The kettle ticked as it cooled. I said I was sorry. I said I had been going to say it for twenty-two months and each time I had made a coffee.

"She left me one too," said Anjali. "It was about the dal."

That was all. She put the spoon down. She did not say what the message had said, and I did not ask, and I understood that I was being told something about a door that opened one way. "Have you listened to yours?" she said.

"No," I said. It was true in the way that a form is true.

"Okay." Her voice had slowed and her shoulders had come down and her eyes had lowered to the level of mine, which is her professional calm, and which I have seen turn a screaming four-year-old into a boy stacking blocks. Her hands were not calm. She had pulled a paper napkin from the holder and folded it in half and in half again until it was a coin, and she went on folding it long after it would fold.

* * *

At three we went down to the office, because Anjali said she wanted to see where I had been working when I stopped being seen. On Friday I would have taken that for a figure of speech.

The office was dark, with the blinds down, and Noreen had not gone home. A blanket lay squared on the leasing loveseat. She stood up behind her desk as we came in, under LIVE LAUGH LEASE, and the keys chimed. She had washed her face in the staff bathroom and combed the Merlot and put on lipstick, and I understood that she had done it as other people dress for a funeral.

"Mrs Whitmore," said my sister.

"Noreen, please."

"Mrs Whitmore. On the twenty-fourth of October, a Friday, at about eleven in the morning, you told me my brother was at his desk."

"I did."

"And then you said you'd circle back."

"I did."

"I treat four-year-olds who can't tell me what's wrong," said Anjali, "and some of them lie to me better than that. They at least look at the ceiling."

"I looked at the banner," said Noreen. The banner, EMBRACE THE CHANGE, hung in the front window behind us in reverse. "I'm sorry. I lied to you because I was frightened, and I told myself I was being professional. In my line they feel the same."

Anjali looked at the form, which Noreen had put out on the desk, squared to the blotter. "You wrote 'guess.'"

"I did. In pen. I'm sorry for that too."

"It was the right guess."

"It was," said Noreen. Her hand had found her lanyard.

"Don't do it again."

"Okay. Okay. Okay."

* * *

Chioma left for her shift at half past six, in the green clogs. "For the record, nobody sleeps near that wall without saying goodnight to it," she said, and shut the door on us.

Anjali blew up the air mattress herself, with a foot pump, on the floor of Chioma's front room, and would not be helped. She lay down on it in the dark with her shoes off and the coat over her like a blanket, zipped, and I lay on the couch above her with my hand with its Sharpie on my chest. The streetlight came in the window. Two floors down a bus braked on the wet road and pulled away.

"How long have you been like this?" said my sister.

I considered pretending not to know what she meant. "Since January last year, I think."

"Same."

That was all for a while. Then she told me she had told the clinic a week. Ben had said take two. "He said I haven't taken a day since the funeral, which is true, which is the worst thing about him." And, after a time: "Dad asked if you were eating. That's the whole of what he's said about any of this." I tried to hear my father say it and got the announcer on a train platform. I said nothing.

"Take two," I said.

"I'll see."

The radiator under the window knocked twice. "Is that her?" said Anjali.

"It's the radiator."

"Is that her?"

"It's both."

She put one hand out from under the coat and knocked twice on the floor with a knuckle, and in the pipes there was a long sigh, and then nothing.

In the morning I heard her in the corridor with the door open, on the phone. She said, "Two, then," and "No. I'm fine. Ben, I'm fine. Kiss her." Chioma came in at twenty to eight off shift, gray with it, and stopped in the doorway of her own kitchen. By the door was a row of three hooks. On them hung a scrub top, a hospital lanyard and a canvas bag.

Anjali came in from the corridor, put the phone in her pocket and unzipped the coat. I had not seen her without it in that city. She took it off, shook it out once, and hung it on the hook between the scrub top and the lanyard, and put her hand flat on the shoulder to settle it, the way you pat someone who has agreed to stay.

Chioma looked at the coat, and at her, and put the kettle on, which is as much as she says about anything she is glad of.
