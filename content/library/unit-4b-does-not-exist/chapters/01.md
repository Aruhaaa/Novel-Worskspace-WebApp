# Validation Error

A note to file is what you write when you want it on record that you told someone. It carries a date and a subject line and no particular reader, and it goes into a folder where it sits unread until the day it matters, when everybody reads it at once. I have written a few hundred of them, about late fees and a dishwasher in 2D and a tenant's ferret, and until this one I never needed anyone to believe me.

This one begins on Monday, 6 October, because that is where it began. At ten to nine that morning I was at my desk in the management office at Harwick Terrace, 114 East 31st Street, and the software that had kept the rent roll for twenty-two years was about to be switched off. RentWise 2.4 ran green letters on a black screen from a computer in the basement that I had never seen and had come to think of as plumbing. Corvane Property Group, which manages seventeen buildings, was being bought by a bigger company called Larchmont Residential Partners. Larchmont wanted a clean data room, Corvane wanted Larchmont's check, and Tenantry 9 was the broom.

Noreen had hung the banner on Friday. It was a strip of blue vinyl with grommets, stretched across the front window in the white capitals of a gym that is about to raise its prices, and it said EMBRACE THE CHANGE.

"Today we do not mourn RentWise," she told the room, coming out of her office at 8:45 with her lanyard chiming. Noreen Whitmore has managed Harwick Terrace since 2006. She carries fourteen keys on that lanyard and you hear her arrive the way you hear an ice-cream truck, and she keeps her hair cropped and dyed a burgundy she says is called Merlot and I believe is called something else. She was working a piece of nicotine gum. "We thank it for its service and we put it in a drawer. Dev, hon, you're my canary."

"Technically, the canary is the one that doesn't make it," I said.

"Then be a hardy one," said Noreen, and went to refill her tea.

"Noted," said Joyce Lim, from the cubicle by the night-drop safe, without looking up. Joyce is accounts receivable. She has a plain black bob and a calculator whose enter key has worn down to bare metal, and in all the time I had known her she had said that one word in answer to a birthday, a fire drill and the death of a tenant's mother, meaning something a little different each time.

At the leasing desk by the window, Espie Salcedo was filming a coffee. That month her nails were pumpkin orange with a tiny black bat on each ring finger, and she held the phone at arm's length behind a ring light clamped to her desk, an umbrella drying open on the radiator beside her. "Ok so we're giving go-live," she told it. "We're giving new era, we're giving, like, caffeine? No because, look at the foam."

I said good morning to the room in general and sat down, and my knee met the drawer, and I said sorry. Technically it was the drawer that hit me. The desk is gray steel and came with the job, along with a chair that fits my spine better than any chair I have chosen myself and a drawer that sticks. In the drawer there was a tin of lemon drops older than my employment and a loose horn button, and I had never found a reason to throw out either. On the front counter the butterscotch dish had been topped up over the weekend, its gold cellophane going soft in the damp, a bowl of small yellow coins that nobody eats and nobody lets run out.

* * *

Tenantry does not have a button that says Check Everything. It has a button that says Run Validation Sweep, in a green meant to make you feel supported. At 8:52 I pressed it. The rent roll came up as a table of forty-one rows, each with a unit and a tenant and a balance, and a progress bar crossed the top of the screen in about four seconds while the rows turned over down the list. Each got a small green tick and a small click from the laptop speaker. I will admit that I enjoyed it. I like a column that agrees with itself.

Forty of them agreed. The forty-first sat in the middle of the fourth floor, where in thirteen months and seventeen days I had never had a reason to open it, because it was the only line on the roll that had never given me any trouble. It went red. A banner dropped from the top of the screen like a window blind.

> HT-4B | Unit 4B | Tenant: OCCUPANT | Rent: $1,840.00 | Balance: 0.00 | Lease start: 01/00/1900 | FLOORPLAN_ID: (blank) | PERMIT_NO: (blank) | DOOR_COUNT: (blank)

> VALIDATION ERROR. HT-4B: 3 required fields are empty. Row not valid.

I read it left to right, the way I read everything, pushing my glasses up by the taped hinge. The lease start was 01/00/1900, which is the date a database invents when it has been told nothing. The tenant was OCCUPANT, which is what a person writes when the database has beaten them to it. Three fields that every unit in the building has, a floor plan number, a permit number and a door count, were empty. I do not know what a unit without a door count is. I know what it is on a form: incomplete.

Then I did what I always do with a bad row and opened the payment history, because that is where a bad row gives itself away. The rent was $1,840.00 and the balance was nothing. Every month on the ledger, back to the first import in 2003, there was one payment of $1,840.00, received on the twentieth. No month late, no month short, no month skipped. I had forty tenants who paid on time when reminded, and one who had never needed reminding, and it was the one the new software could not find a floor for.

Behind my left shoulder a lanyard chimed, and stopped. I did not turn around. For about as long as it takes to read a banner the keys were quiet, and then they started up again and moved off. "Look at all that green," Noreen said, to the room, and shut her door. I took it for encouragement.

* * *

Lunch & Learn is Corvane's monthly training lunch, and that month's topic was Tenantry. At 11:30 Noreen wheeled the conference TV out of the filing room on its cart, Espie set out two trays of wraps from Halloran's across the street, and Brent from Tenantry appeared on the screen in a headset, his face lit from below by something on his desk, his virtual background swallowing his left ear.

"Hi team, super excited," said Brent. "So I'm just going to do a quick refresher on data hygiene, because in Tenantry, clean data is happy data."

He covered trailing spaces. He covered double spaces, and phone numbers with the word *cell* typed into them, and the sin of putting N/A in a date field. "Tenantry will accept it," he said, "because it's a super forgiving platform. And then it will remember."

Espie raised a hand with a bat on it. "Can it auto-fix vibes?"

"Super question," said Brent. "Not at this time. There's a Notes field. Two hundred fifty-five characters."

"So no," said Espie, to her wrap.

"One more super important thing." The ear went again, and came back. "Any entry over a thousand dollars needs a Source of Funds attestation. Basically Tenantry asks where the money's coming from. It defaults to the preparer, so super easy. You won't even see it."

"Noted," said Joyce.

I wrote SOURCE OF FUNDS in the margin of my handout and drew a box around it, because I write everything in the margin of the handout. It seemed an odd question to put to a rent payment, and I assumed it was somebody else's compliance. Brent added that the sweep results posted automatically to the regional dashboard, so Randall could see where we stood at a glance. Randall Pugh is the regional director. I had met him twice, both times on a screen, both times in front of the sea.

When the questions came I put up my hand. "Technically I have one red row after the sweep. HT-4B. Three required fields blank, and a lease start of the zeroth of January nineteen hundred."

Brent's face did something under the lighting. "Does it have a tenant?"

"It has an Occupant."

"Yep, OCCUPANT, that's the placeholder. That's a migration ghost. Super common. Just delete it."

"It has a payment history."

"Super common," said Brent. "There's a delete button top right. It'll ask you to confirm."

"Let's circle back on that," said Noreen, who had not looked up from her wrap in some time. "Brent, hon, we have so much to cover."

"Super," said Brent.

* * *

By half past twelve the wraps were down to the ones with cilantro, the TV was back in the filing room, and I had walked to Joyce's cubicle with a pen in my hand for no reason. She was counting money orders into stacks of ten and squaring each stack to the edge of her blotter.

"Joyce, do you know anything about 4B?" I said, quietly, as if the safe might hear.

"Noted," said Joyce.

I waited. She squared the stack. Then, as if she had been rude, she put it down. "Four-B pays on the twentieth. In cash. It's in an envelope in the night drop and I put it in the bag with the rest. I've never asked."

"Who's it from?"

"I've never asked," Joyce said again, a degree more gently, which for Joyce amounts to a hand on the arm. "It balances."

She went back to her money orders. I stood there a little longer than a man with a question should, and then I printed the unit list, forty-one lines on two pages, because I trust paper to be where I left it, and took it and a ham on rye I would not eat to the elevators.

The lobby clock is six minutes slow, like every other clock in the building, and nobody has ever fixed one. It said 1:19. Harwick has two elevators, called Left and Right, and Left is the older, and on Mondays it sticks. I pressed its button anyway, being a creature of routine, and the button was warm. Not hot: warm, like a coin that has been in a pocket. The lobby was empty. The mailbox wall sits over a radiator, which I decided was the reason. Left came down with its Monday groan, opened halfway, thought about it, opened the rest, and carried me to four without incident. I mention the lack of incident because I had been braced for one.

The fourth-floor corridor runs east and west from the elevators, with maroon carpet in a diamond pattern that repeats every three feet, and doors. I turned west. The list in my hand said that along this wall, from the end of the hall, I would find 4A, then 4B, then 4C. In front of 4A a rubber mat held a pair of green hospital clogs, toes to the door, which belonged to Chioma Eze, a respiratory therapist who works nights and whose name I knew from a lease and nothing else. A blue bicycle with a bell on the handlebar leaned against the wall by 4C. Between them, where the list said B should be, there was a red cabinet with a glass front and FIRE HOSE in white capitals, the hose folded inside it like a concertina, the brass nozzle on top.

The corridor smelled of toast. Not burnt: clean and brown, the smell of bread at the moment you decide it is done. Every door in the hall was shut. I stood and listened for a toaster, a radio, a person, and heard the lights.

I would like it on record that I checked the corridor for witnesses before I started, which is how I know I felt foolish. I am a person who counts. I know my pace is thirty inches because I once walked a hallway beside a tape measure on a quiet evening, for reasons I would not defend. I put my heel against the sill of 4A and walked to 4C.

Nine. At thirty inches, that is twenty-two and a half feet, which is two eleven-foot intervals and a man's shoe, which is how far an A should be from a C with a B between them. I mention it so the record shows the arithmetic came out. I turned around and walked back.

I reached the green clogs on the twelfth pace.

I stood there with the list in one hand. Twelve paces is thirty feet. Corridors do not grow eight feet in the time it takes a man to turn around, and I am aware of that. I did what you do. I walked to 4C again: nine. I turned. I walked back: twelve.

Then I ran a control, which is the thing I am proudest of that day. The carpet gives you a ruler for free, one diamond every three feet, and I counted diamonds both ways: seven and a bit going, seven and a bit coming. The pattern had not changed. Going out, a diamond took me a pace and a bit. Coming back it took nearly a pace and a half, and it felt, I can only say, like walking the same diamonds with a longer wait in each.

I ended in front of the cabinet, because it was in the middle and I had been walking toward and away from it for ten minutes. Up close the glass was clean, and the brass nozzle had a small paper inspection tag tied to it with string, the date punched out in a hole the size of a grain of rice. I was reading the tag when the knocks came.

They came from inside the wall, to the left of the cabinet, about the height of my chest. Two. Knuckles on plaster, unhurried, spaced the way a person knocks who knows you are in and only wants to confirm the delivery. I would like to say I stood and weighed it. I know what I did instead, which was that my right hand came up and knocked on the plaster beside the cabinet, twice, at the same interval, and then I was standing in a corridor with my fist in the air, looking at it.

Nothing came back. I listened for what I think was ten seconds. The smell of toast went out of the corridor between one breath and the next, the way a radio goes quiet in the next apartment, and the lights hummed.

"Sorry," I said, to a fire-hose cabinet, and went down by the stairs.

In the lobby the clock said 1:54, which was two o'clock. Nobody asked where I had been. In the office Espie was filming Joyce's shortbread tin and Joyce was not enjoying it, Noreen's door was shut, and on my monitor the banner was exactly as red as I had left it. I sat down. I pressed my cowlick flat with my palm, and it came back up. Then I put both hands on the desk where I could see them and told no one anything.
