# Count In, Count Out

At seven in the morning the fourth-floor corridor is nine paces long, with a door in the middle.

I know the figure because I count it. Four paces from the 4A mat, where Chioma's clogs sit on the rubber, then the door, then four more to the blue bicycle at 4C, and one for the door itself. Nine. The laser says 22.00 feet, as it always has, and the maroon diamonds repeat every three feet and take a pace and a bit apiece, as diamonds should. The corridor smells of toast. It has every morning since the twenty-first of November, and I have stopped writing it down.

The door is beige, with a brass knob and a brass 4 and a brass B, and the B is straight. At the west end by the stair, the fire hose cabinet stands behind its glass with the paper tag on its nozzle, in the place where Rafa says it has always been. He says so to the fire marshal's office in a level voice. No one has argued.

It is Sunday the fifteenth of March, the first dry day since October. I have come down from 6A by the stairs, in my socks, which Zofia disapproves of. I stand on the mat and knock twice.

Two knocks come back, unhurried, like someone confirming a delivery.

Then I say the names, in the voice Mrs Han uses for the chat. Thaddeus Ambler, and a ball game goes up a notch. Vera Pruitt, and three notes are hummed. Nils Haraldsen, and a pipe is tapped with a ring. Inez Marrero. Wendell Tipps, and a man whistles a quarter hour the building does not keep. Constance Fiske delivers a line to nobody, with feeling. Ellis Kwan, and a pencil, very small, runs along a margin. Hector Salazar idles like a bus at a stop. Gary Michael Whitmore. Margaret Dunleavy.

Inez and Gary do not answer, because they are out. I say them anyway. I do not think a list is closed when somebody leaves it.

"Good morning, dear," says Margie, last, as she has since December. She maintains that it is the only civilized position in a roll. "Come in. I want you to look at something."

I count in.

Nine paces. The oak boards, worn in their path. The card table, the hot plate, the brown pot. On the shelf over the sink the ten white cups stand in a row, eight the right way up and two turned over on their saucers, rinsed, which Margie did herself, once in November and once in January. She says it is how you tell a table that a place has been vacated and not forgotten. Under the window the radiator knocks twice, on time. Rafa bled it in December, the first time he had walked into the room, because my sister had said it wanted seeing to and had said it like a work order. He took his cap off at the door and put it on again in the corridor, and said it was a good room, in the voice of a man giving an appraisal.

Margie stands in the bay with the curtains tied back. The window is clear. Since February it has been a wooden sash painted cream, with a street in it.

Woo-jin Cho wrote up the bricked opening in January as a Life Safety deviation: a dwelling unit with no natural light or egress, section 4.3, which was accurate in every particular. It was his fourth deviation in nine years. It is the first anyone has acted on. The permit came through the Annex in nine days, and Mrs Tuck stamped it herself and wanted exact change. When the last of the lighter brick came out, I was on the sidewalk with Rafa and Mrs Han, looking up, and a man at the bus shelter said, "Somebody finally fixed that window," and I said they had.

"Dear," says Margie. "Are they always that color?"

I come and stand beside her. Across 31st Street are the bus shelter and Halloran's awning, and along the curb the maples, a block of them, each branch tipped with a red-brown fuzz. "Red maples," I say. "It's the buds. They do it in March, before the leaves."

"Good heavens." She has her glasses on their cord and her big hands flat on the sill. "Six springs I've been told about, and not one of them was a color." The rain is still on the glass, because it is always ten past four in here. Outside, in the same pane, it is dry. Margie says the window cannot decide, and she has stopped asking it to. "I find I mind neither."

Zofia offered in December to sew the third button back on, with the tape around her neck and the needle already threaded. Margie declined. "I should only have to look at it, dear. Leave it in your drawer." I keep it there with the lemon-drop tin, which I fill on Saturdays from the counter at Halloran's, between the batteries and the waterproof notebooks. Margie says a button is a thing someone is keeping for you, and that this is the whole point of a button.

"Count out," she says, as I reach the door.

"Nine," I say, and I am out in the same hour I went in, twenty past seven by every clock in the building.

* * *

Inez Marrero stepped out in January. In the hotel's staff register, in the banker's box in the server closet, I found a daughter's name, and then, after a month of the Annex and the county, a granddaughter, Marisol Reyes, thirty-five, who drives a bakery van. She came on a Wednesday and stood at the door with her hand flat on the wood and said her grandmother's name, the whole of it, and it was Inez's hour, and Inez walked out at thirty-three in a cashier's gray cardigan, two years younger than the woman crying in the corridor. She asked the building for the cashier's window. The building does not make change, but it has one. Behind the doorman's desk is a brass grille that had been painted shut since 1986, and Rafa took the paint off it with a heat gun in four days. She sits in it on Wednesdays, which was the day she sang, and she sings, and the tenants stop at the mailboxes to say good morning to the cashier.

The rest have been asked, one at a time, as Margie said they must be. Some declined, and some have no one outside to say their names at the door, which in effect is the same answer. I say the names every morning. Perhaps someone will come. I do not make promises. I put in work orders.

At noon Zofia stops me on the third-floor landing with the tape around her neck. "Arms," she says. I lift them. She measures the shoulders, the waist, the wrist and the length of the arm, in the order she has done it since October, and writes in her book with the pencil from behind her ear.

"Same," she says.

"Same as when?"

"Same as before. Is rare. Most people, after, are never again same." She closes the book. "You are in socks again."

I have been given a number of words in this note, and I think that is the best of them.

At ten past four Noreen comes out of the office, with the keys chiming, and sits on the lobby bench beside Gary. She does it every afternoon, Sundays included. They look at each other for ten minutes by the lobby clock, and they are learning it the way people learn a language late: she like a manager, with a schedule, and he like a plumber, from the side, with an eye to the pipes.

"You've got gray at the part," says Gary.

"Merlot does not cover everything."

"I'm not complaining. I'm reporting."

"Keep reporting," says Noreen.

He works three days a week with Rafa now, on the books as maintenance, and has replaced the anode rod in the number one heater and, for good measure, the number two. He sleeps at home, in a bed, beside a wife ten years older who, he reports, snores, and he reports it with the look of a man who has been handed a prize.

"I do not," says Noreen, not looking away.

The lobby clock says 4:10. It is 4:10. Mrs Han has reported this as a fault.

At a quarter past, Chioma takes the black receiver off the doorman's desk, which has no cord to the wall, and which I have stopped asking about. She holds Mr Grieve's gold watch open in her left palm, the only clock in the building that was ever right, and uses a voice I have heard from her only on the ward, steady and professional with something under it. "Mr Ambler? Front desk. I'm sorry. The line to Dayton is busy. We are holding for you." She tips the receiver for me to hear. A salesman's voice, a long way off, says, "Quite so. I'll hold," and behind it a ball game goes up a notch.

"Thank you for your patience, Mr Ambler," says Chioma, and hangs up, and writes in the logbook Mrs Han bought for the purpose: Sunday, 4:15, held.

"Does he ever ask how long?" I say.

"Never once. For the record, it's the best customer service I have ever given." She closes the book. "It's Mr Grieve's promise, kept by relay. Don't write that down."

"I'm writing it down."

"I know," says Chioma.

I use the mint floss from the packet Mr Ambler sold me in October, sparingly. When it runs out I will have to buy another from him, and I mean to.

At five I go down to the office and sit in the chair that fits my spine because it fit hers, and which I think is by now a little mine. The blinds are up. Joyce left the shortbread tin on my blotter on Friday. She says my name now. It is the only thing she has added to her one word, and she adds it every morning at the end: "Noted, Dev."

The Larchmont sale closed on the twenty-fourth of November and nothing happened, which is what a closing is for. Randall Pugh vested in January and left the organization with a farewell email in capitals, subject GOING FORWARD. He never learned what he had been wrong about. I regard it as the cleanest piece of corporate hygiene I have witnessed. Noreen did not leave after all. She says staying was Gary's idea and she wishes she had thought of it. She has twenty-seven months to her pension, and announces the number on the first of the month like a weather report.

In October a man in a quarter-zip told me that the whole point of the exercise was that it did not exist. I read the second line most mornings. It does not say what he wanted. It says what the row is.

On the nineteenth, at 23:58, I post the rent: $1,840.00, cash, with a receipt in my own hand and the sevens crossed. It still costs. A unit with a door is not a unit with a free door, and the books must balance, which Margie says is not a punishment. The difference is that I choose now. In December I put down a left glove, one of a pair I had carried for eleven winters and never worn. In January it was a tie I had been given at a funeral. I pick the thing in the first week of the month and I stop looking at it, which is harder than it sounds. You cannot easily not look at a glove you have decided not to look at. I spend most of the month working on it, and by the batch it is on the shelf with a label in my capitals. In February I noticed that I was choosing the way a man chooses from a box of chocolates, weighing, and a little pleased by the weighing. This is the part I would like you to read twice. It is easy to like being the one who decides what goes. The building will let you. It has no objection to a clerk who enjoys the work.

On the second Tuesday of January I went to the Gaslight. Table 4 seats nine, and the team at it, who call themselves Les Quizerables and are all pharmacy students, were one short.

"You look like you know things," said a girl with a lanyard of pill-bottle charms.

"Technically I know one kind of thing," I said, and sat in the ninth chair.

It turned out to be music. I knew the answers to the round, and I do not know where I learned them. At 9:41 I turned my phone face up on the table to see what it would do. It did nothing. I stayed to the end of the round, and the pharmacy students said I was hired.

Here is the rest, for you, whoever holds this desk next. It goes in the folder tabbed NEXT in the green cabinet, which was empty when I found it, and which I would rather you did not find empty.

Count in and count out. Whatever you are counting, count it both ways. Keep the row. Be kind to Noreen. She is trying, and as far as I can tell she is better at it than she thinks. Cross your sevens, because Margie will know you by them. The building takes what you hold least, and it counts looking and not using, so look: at the key you turn twice a day, at the mug, at whoever rings you. Say the names of the people in the room every morning, aloud, to the door. Do not type your own name into the last field unless you are quite sure there is no one left who would say it. And ring someone on Sunday. It does not matter whom. It matters that they pick up, and that you counted on it.

A note to file is what you write when you want it on record that you told someone. I have told you.

* * *

It is two minutes to six. The pad is on the blotter, and the phone is in my hand with her message still saved on it, which I have not played since the first of December and do not need to. I know the number by heart, better than I know my own, which the phone still reports as Unknown.

The first Sunday, the twenty-third of November, I said wrong number again, and she said, "Again?" and laughed, and asked whether I was all right, in the voice she uses on a parent in a hallway. The third Sunday she told me what Tia had for lunch. By Christmas Tia was saying, "Mommy, it's the phone man," and my sister was saying, "I know, baby." She does not know why she picks up. She told me in February that six o'clock on Sundays felt like something she had forgotten to write on the calendar. I said that was a sensible thing to feel. She said she had thought so too. She has not asked my name, and I have not offered it. I am not sure yet whether that is mercy or cowardice, and I have decided I may be allowed to find out slowly.

I dial. I count the rings.

One. Two.

"Oh," she says. "It's you."
