# A Controlled Experiment

By nine on Sunday evening I had a spreadsheet of 212 possessions with a column headed Condition, and nobody had asked me for it.

It is a stranger thing than it sounds, to find out what you own. I had gone through Tolliver Court 3F room by room with my phone in one hand and a label maker in the other, and by the end the studio looked like a place I had never lived. Every item had a photograph, a number from A-001 to A-212, and a line. The bed. The chair, and the folding chair behind the door. My mother's steel tiffin, A-044, Fair, contents thirty-one rubber bands and fourteen AA batteries. Eleven shirts. Fourteen pairs of socks, which I put on a separate tab and called the control group, and I would like it noted that I did this with a straight face.

The method was not complicated, and I had been using its relatives at work for years. If the building billed me for what I entered, then I could enter something small, count before and after, and the difference would be the bill. It is a reconciliation. The only unusual feature was that the account was me. I had thought of asking an independent party to witness the count, and found I was the only candidate on the list.

At the last minute I added a column headed Last Used, because when you photograph a drawer you ought to say when you last opened it. I did not know yet that it was the only column that mattered.

I chose three edits and chose them to be the size of the ones I had made on the Monday before my mother's voicemail went. Each was true. I want that understood. I was not going to put a lie into the roll to find out what a lie cost. I wanted to fix three things that were wrong and see what the fixing took.

Owen Fenske's name did not end in a space, and now it didn't. Priscilla Obi in 5E, a nurse I had met once in the mailroom at six in the morning, had two digits of her phone number the wrong way round, and now she did not. Mrs Ruiz's daughter had moved to a new number in August, and the note to say so had sat in the file since September. I saved each one. The green tick came up each time, and nothing whatever happened, and I stood in the middle of the room and listened to the refrigerator.

At 11:40 I closed the laptop, as I do every night, and sat at the table with my hands flat on the lid, like a man keeping a book from opening. At 11:57 the fan came on.

I lifted the lid. The Tenantry session was open. I had logged out at ten. The cursor was moving, and my hand was on the table beside the laptop, and it crossed the page at a steady walk, the way you cross a room you know in the dark. It opened HT-4B and clicked Payments. It typed in the Amount field with a rhythm I knew because it was mine: the small pause after the point, the two zeros a beat late. It chose CASH. It did not touch the next field, and the next field filled itself. PREPARER. At 23:58 it pressed Save, and the screen went green.

I had taken my hands off the keys at the first click. The odd part, the part I have not got used to, is that I did not feel robbed. It was like watching someone else do my job, quietly and well, with no wasted motion. It was the best I had ever seen it done. I ended the session with the mouse, and the cursor went to the Log out link at the same unhurried walk, and the fan stopped, the way a refrigerator stops.

From the drawer under the stove, where I had put the label maker after the inventory, came a whir and a small chunk. I opened the drawer. It had printed a label and pushed it out, like a tongue. HT-4B / 10-25 / RCVD D.M. I peeled the backing and pressed it inside the cover of the notebook, because I could think of nowhere else it belonged.

At 2:08 I was sitting on the kitchen floor in front of the radiator under the window, with my back against the cabinet and the notebook on my knee, in the posture of a man waiting for a phone to ring. At 2:10 the clock on the stove, which says 2:10 to me more often than I like, stopped saying anything else. The pipes sang. It came up through the building from somewhere under the parking lot, a long, low, rising note, like a finger going round the rim of a glass, and the fin of the radiator under my hand went warm. It knocked twice.

I knocked back. Twice, with the knuckle of my right forefinger, on the fin. A silence followed, in which I could hear my own pulse in the frames of my glasses. Then something let out a sigh in the pipes, long, like a person settling into a chair at the end of the day, and the stove clock said 2:11.

* * *

I started the count at six, with the tiffin, and worked outward with a pencil. I will give the result in the order I found it, because the order mattered later.

Spare keys, A-017. They had hung on the hook by the door, and the hook was there. Two keys on a ring with a leather fob: my spare to 3F, and the key to my father's side door in Edison, which my mother had pressed into my hand the day I moved out, saying you never know. Last Used: last spring. The hook hung with nothing on it like a coat rack in a room with no coats.

The gift card, A-091. Twenty-five dollars at a bookstore, from the Corvane holiday party, in the drawer with the takeout menus, unspent. Last Used: December. The menus lay flat against one another where it had been.

The gym tag, A-130. A plastic fob for a gym on Kessler Avenue that I joined in March of the year before last and had visited four times. Last Used: unknown.

One mug, A-052. The second of the set, the clean one that I never used. There was a mug on the drainer, the used one. Beside it the rack held a space the size of a mug, and the rack had not closed up around it.

Four lines I could not tick. Two hundred and twelve, now two hundred and eight. Beside each missing line stood a Last Used in my own typing, and the shortest gap was six months. Everything that was gone was a thing I had not touched in half a year. Everything I touched was still there: the mug I drank from, the keys in my pocket, the phone. If it was the building that had done this, it had taken what in the apartment had nobody's attention.

I wrote the problem down, because it was one. Three edits, four items, and one rent. It did not match, and I did not have a theory of why. A control group of socks, and the socks were all there, and I noted with some pride that I had been right about the socks.

The fifth thing I found at seven, when I opened the messaging app to text Anjali good morning, which I do not do. The list of conversations was one shorter than it had been on Saturday. I searched for Quizzy Rascals and got nothing. I searched for Rascals, and Quizzy, and Table 4, which returned a calendar entry from the second January before. The thread was not in the trash. Two years of capitals of Burkina Faso and who-had-the-tiebreaker had not been deleted, because deleting leaves a hole, and this had left none.

I took an envelope off the pile of mail and wrote the roster the way I used to write it on the beer mat for the league sheet. The Rascals filled Table 4 at the Gaslight, which seats nine, and we filled it: eight of them and me. Priya. Big Ted. Lorraine, from the billing office at Merrow General. Amir. Femi. Hae-won, who did the music rounds. That was six. I could see the table. Nine chairs, and seven with somebody in them, six and me. Two chairs were occupied. I knew they were, the way a tongue knows a gap in the teeth, and I could not tell you who had sat in them. I could not tell you whether I had liked them. I sat with the pencil above the envelope for twenty-three seconds, which is the length of the voicemail, and then I put the envelope in the notebook, beside the label.

At the office Joyce had the night-drop envelope on her blotter, with 4B on the front in pencil. She had counted it twice. "Eighteen-forty," she said. "To the dollar. Noted." There were two red-seal two-dollar notes in it, 1953, with consecutive serial numbers, which she did not like. "I won't bank what's worth more than it says," said Joyce. She had a row of them now in the clear sleeves in her tray, a silver certificate, a five, the two twos, laid out edge to edge like a lineup, and she looked at them the way you look at a lineup when you know it is not the right one but cannot say which man is wrong.

* * *

I wore a shirt for the Diwali call, the blue one with the collar. I propped the phone against the tiffin at five to seven and sat down at the table with the one chair, and Edison arrived.

I knew the dining room at once, by the light, which is always a little yellower than any other light in the world, and the long table with the leaf in it. On the table was everything. Puris puffed like small gold hats under a cloth. Aloo sabzi, shrikhand in a glass bowl, a plate of chakli, besan ladoo from a neighbor in a sheet of foil. The dal sat in the big steel pot that comes out for funerals and weddings, and there was a lot of it. I knew the card. Always makes eight. There were four people at the table.

"Happy Diwali," said Anjali. She had her hair down, which is rare, and a pair of gold hoops, and she was bright in the way she is bright for Tia, at a volume she can hold for about an hour. Under it she was very tired. I saw it at the corners of her eyes and she saw me see it. "You wore a shirt."

"I said I would."

"You said you'd wear a shirt. Nobody said it'd be that shirt. It's fine. It's a good shirt." Tia pushed into the frame. She wore a pink kurta with sequins at the collar and held a saucer with a tiny clay lamp in it. "I lit it by myself," she said. "Papa helped. Papa held the match."

"Happy Diwali, Tia."

"Happy Diwali, Uncle Spreadsheet. Did your total add up?"

"I'm still checking."

"Check faster," said Tia, with reproach, and was removed by Ben.

At the far end of the table my father sat in the cardigan with the elbows gone, with a steel tumbler of water in front of him. Suresh Mahajan is sixty-eight and has been getting smaller, in Edison, since January of last year, the way a coat gets smaller on a hook. He did not come to the screen. He lifted one hand off the table, flat, palm out, at about the height of his shoulder, and held it there. I knew the gesture. It was what he did at the stop on Oak Street when a bus he did not need pulled in: no, thank you, go on.

"He's being Dad," said Anjali, quietly, turning the phone a few degrees toward her. "He's glad you're here. It's this, or he'd have gone to the garage."

"Tell him the quarter turn was right," I said. "On the pots."

She did. "Papa. Dev says the quarter turn was right." My father's hand stayed where it was, a little higher, I thought, and then it came down, and he picked up the tumbler and looked into it.

We talked for an hour. I will not put all of it down. Ben had carved the second pumpkin and was proud of it, and Anjali had put one of Mummy's recipe cards in a frame in the hall, and I said it was a good idea, and she said it was a good card, the one for the chai, with a thumbprint of turmeric in the corner. At the end she held the phone close and said "Sunday at six" and I said "Sunday at six," and the screen went to the picture of my own face, in the blue shirt, looking like a man who had just been handed a form.

I sat with the phone for some time. I tried to hear my father. I could see him: the cardigan, the tumbler, the hand. I could see the hand perfectly. I went for the sound of him saying my name, the way he says it, which is Dev-a, with the weight on the second part, and then Devendra, which is how he says it when I have done something, and I got a voice that was not his, a flat voice on a train platform announcing the next stop. I could not recall one sentence. I had not had a conversation with my father longer than a prescription since the funeral. If you had asked me that afternoon what I was holding least, I would have named a mug, or a postcard. I knew now that it was a man at the end of a table, and I knew it the way you know the balance in your account, without looking and to the cent.

Fourteen months. Eighteen-forty a month. I tried to think what that was, in a currency like this one. The second chair. The Gaslight. The postcard I had not turned over, and the second mug, and the Rascals, and a father's voice that had gone out of range. I had thought I had a small life because I was a small person. It had not crossed my mind that someone was drawing it down. Zofia had measured me by eye in the lobby on my first day and with a tape in October, and had said, with the sympathy of a woman telling a man his hem was coming down, that I had lost two centimeters. She had been right to the centimeter. I had thought she was being kind.

I took the notebook out and wrote the rule, plainly, because a rule should be plain.

> Everything I enter is paid for out of something I wasn't looking after.

I looked at it for as long as it takes a kettle to boil, and then I wrote the next line.

> What have I been paying the rent with?

And under it, smaller, in the handwriting I keep for things I would rather not have read:

> It took two of the Rascals and I did not notice which.
