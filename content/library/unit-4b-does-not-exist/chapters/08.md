# The Bricked Window

The ticket said 241. The sign above the counter at the Municipal Annex said NOW SERVING 238. Between those two numbers I sat down on a bench bolted to the floor and understood that I had made a plan on the strength of a website.

The website, last updated in the spring of 2022, said that Permit Records was at Window 3, that it opened at nine, and that a records search with up to three printed pages cost $4.25, cash only. I had $4.25. I had assembled it the night before out of the jar of quarters and a drawer of singles and carried it in a sandwich bag, like a man bribing a very small official.

I had put in for the afternoon on Tenantry that morning, reason Personal, no description. Noreen approved it from her phone inside a minute with a thumbs-up and no remark, which in Noreen is an essay. Before I left I went up to four and counted the corridor at twenty past eight. Nineteen. The Annex is nineteen minutes downtown by bus. I wrote both numbers in the notebook, side by side, and crossed out the second, because a man has to draw the line somewhere.

The Annex is a flat building of buff brick, put up in the years when governments liked to look as if accountants had built them. On the second floor a hall of windows, numbered one to six, ran away from a red take-a-number machine that had given me my 241 with a sound like a hiccup. About thirty of us sat on benches. Nobody read. Everyone held a ticket the way you hold a boarding pass for a flight you do not believe is going to leave.

The man beside me wore a maroon satin bowling jacket with LEON stitched over the heart in cursive. He had a roll of drawings between his knees. "Deck," he said, before I asked. "Twelve by sixteen. Six months. Now they want the joist hangers in a different galvanization." He shook his head at the sign. "What's yours?"

"A certificate of occupancy."

"Oh, you're one of the easy ones." He said it wistfully. His ticket said 242.

The sign went to 239 at 12:20 and to 240 at 12:41. At 1:09 it said 241, and a bell rang, and I stood up with the sandwich bag in my fist.

Window 3 belonged to a woman of about sixty-five in a lavender cardigan buttoned to the throat. Her white hair had been set in a style that had agreed with itself years ago and had not been consulted since. Reading glasses hung on a chain. At her elbow stood a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST GRANDMA, and the nameplate on the counter said B. TUCK, RECORDS SPECIALIST. She did not look up when I reached the glass. She finished the line she was on first, the way a surgeon finishes a stitch.

"Parcel address."

"114 East 31st Street."

"Nature of request."

"A certificate of occupancy. And the floor plan for the fourth floor."

"I don't have floor plans. I have plan sheets, as filed with the permit." Her fingers moved on the keys, two at a time, and every stroke landed. "A floor plan is what an owner has. A sheet is what the county has. They are different documents and the county does not mix them up."

"Then the sheet, please."

"Level four is A-4. Anything else?"

"Any permits that touched the fourth floor. Since the conversion, in eighty-six."

"Mm." The printer behind her woke up and began to work through a stack, and she took each page from the tray and squared it against the counter before passing it through the slot, naming it as she dealt it, like a croupier. "Certificate of occupancy."

> MUNICIPAL ANNEX, PERMIT RECORDS | CERTIFICATE OF OCCUPANCY | 114 East 31st Street | Issued 3 Nov 1986 | Use: Multiple dwelling, seven stories and basement | Dwelling units: 40

The roll at Harwick says forty-one. I read the figure twice, as one does, and then I saw that in the margin beside it someone had written in pencil, in a neat hand, 41 per owner, verbal, and drawn one ruled line through it.

"There's a note," I said.

"There is a struck note," said Mrs Tuck. "A struck note is a corrected note. The document says forty."

"Who struck it?"

"Whoever had the pencil." She took a sip from the mug. "In 1986 we had pencils."

"Plan sheet A-4." She slid it across. It was a photocopy of a photocopy, in gray, of the fourth floor of a building I had walked through every working day for fourteen months. The elevators and stair in the middle, the corridor in a double line, the doors as small arcs. Five apartments were drawn and lettered. Four A. Four C. Four D, E and F. Between 4A and 4C sat a hatched rectangle with the legend R-4 RISER, and nothing else. I looked at the lettering for some time. On a plan the letters come in order, like hours. Someone had skipped B.

"There's no B."

"Architects skip letters." She did not look up. "I had a man in who wanted to know why his building had no thirteenth floor."

"Why hadn't it?"

"I don't know, and it's not on the sheet. The sheet shows what was built. It doesn't give reasons." She passed me the last page. "Permit abstract. That's the only one that touched four."

> BP-1986-04417 | Issued 14 Aug 1986 | Work: Infill existing window opening | Location: North elevation, level 4, bay 2 | Inspection: FINAL, signed 29 Sept 1986

"Final means closed," said Mrs Tuck. "It can't be reopened except by a new permit. In my experience that is the most dependable word in a building."

"Is there a unit 4B in your records? At that address?"

She typed. The screen thought about it. I have seen a good many screens think, and this one thought with the face of a woman who has been telling the public for thirty-one years that there is no such thing. "There is no unit 4B at that address," she said. "Forty dwelling units, no 4B. Is that what you needed?"

"Can anyone look up a permit number? Not just me?"

"Anyone. It's a public number. It's on the website, and it's all a system needs. You type it in, the county tells your system whether it's real, and your system takes the county's word. It never reads the file." She set the mug down. "Landlords ask if they can use one for something it wasn't issued for. The system will let them, I'll say that. I tell them a number is only as true as the work it's attached to." She squared the pages. "That isn't county policy. That's me. Four-fifty."

"The website says four twenty-five."

"The website went up before July." She tipped her head at a laminated sign by the glass, and it said what she said. "Exact change. We don't make change at the window, I'm afraid. We never have."

I had four singles and a quarter. I went out into the corridor with my bag of coins and stood in front of a vending machine that sold pretzels, gum and a sleeve of crackers. A laminated notice under the coin return read THIS MACHINE GIVES CHANGE IN DOLLAR COINS ONLY. THE CITY REGRETS THE INCONVENIENCE. I put in a single and pressed the return, and received a dollar coin, heavy and gold, the same amount of money in a form no window could use. I did this twice. I now had two dollar coins, two singles and a quarter, and was no nearer to fifty cents.

"Machine does that," said Leon, from the doorway. He held out a quarter between two fingers, as one offers a tip to a child. "Pay me back by putting in a good word at Window 1. My deck is structural."

"I don't know anyone at Window 1."

"Nobody does. Say it anyway."

Mrs Tuck took the coins without comment. She counted them against the ledge, turned the wheel on a date stamp, and brought it down on a slip with a flat bang. "Certificate of occupancy, one-fifty," she read. "Plan sheet, two. Permit abstract, one. Four-fifty. Cash. Paid." She tore the slip across the perforation and gave me the top half, and she looked at me for the first time, over the glasses, for about as long as it takes to read a license plate. "You'll want the bay numbers," she said. "People always do. It's a five-bay front."

* * *

Halloran's, across 31st Street from Harwick, sells ham on rye, lottery tickets, umbrellas on a cardboard spinner and, next to the batteries, a waterproof notebook. I took the table in the window, from which, if you lean, you can see the whole front of the building. I ate a ham on rye I had been owing myself since Monday. Then I unfolded the receipt to check the sum, because I check the sum.

> MUNICIPAL ANNEX | PERMIT RECORDS WINDOW 3 | 10/17 1:52 PM | CERT OF OCC 1.50 | PLAN SHT A-4 2.00 | PERMIT ABSTRACT 1.00 | UNIT FILE 4B 0.00 | TOTAL 4.50 | CASH 4.50

I had stood at that window and had the slip read back to me with a full stop after each price. I had counted three lines. I counted four now. The fourth was in the same dot-matrix gray as the rest, and the price against it was nothing, which is the price of a thing that does not exist, and I will admit it was exactly right.

I laid the papers out on the table the way I lay out a reconciliation. A certificate that said forty. A plan that drew five doors and a riser. A permit that said that in the summer of 1986 someone had been paid to close a window on the fourth floor, and that an inspector had come and agreed. A pencil line, crossed through, that said forty-one, and the pencil had been someone's. Against these I had a roll of forty-one rows and a cash envelope on the twentieth. It is a very small list, and I stayed with it until the girl at the register turned the sign over and said they were closing at seven.

At 5:41 I saw Noreen come out of the lobby with her umbrella up and her bag on her shoulder. She stopped on the step. She did not look at the street. She looked up, at the fourth floor, as you look up at a clock, and then she turned right and walked away from me toward the bus stop, with her keys held still against her coat. She did not see me. I am not a person people see across a street.

* * *

At seven I stood under the shelter across from the building, the one at 31st and Merrow, and counted windows.

From there you can see Harwick as no one who works in it ever does, in one piece. Seven stories of red-brown brick, the cream band at the second and the seventh, the lamps going on in the lobby. Rain came off the shelter's roof in a flat sheet. A bus braked in the gutter and threw a wave at the curb, and I stepped back on to the last dry inch of the bench. I let two 14s go by. The second driver opened his door and looked at me, and shut it.

Five bays across. I went up to the fourth floor and counted again. Bay 1, at the west end, had a lamp lit behind its curtain, which I took to be Chioma's, on the strength of a pair of green clogs. Bays 3, 4 and 5 were dark. Bay 2 was filled with brick. It was newer than its neighbors, a shade lighter, laid with mortar a quarter inch wider than the rest, so that the opening still showed in the wall the way a patched pocket shows on a coat. I had a permit number for it, and the number could not have been plainer if it had been painted there.

At 7:40 the patch began to glow.

I want to put this carefully, as I tried to put Espie's video. It began in the mortar, which went the color of strong tea, line by line, and then the brick between the lines, from the edges inward, the amber spreading toward the middle of the opening the way a print comes up in a tray. I have seen that once, on a school trip, and I had forgotten how slow it is, and how it seems to be nobody's doing. Within a minute the whole oblong was lit from behind, a window in a wall that had no window. There were curtains. They were oatmeal, lined, hanging inside the brick and not in front of it, if that makes sense, and I can only say what I saw.

Someone was standing at the curtain. It was a tall figure in a cardigan, standing a little stooped, as people do who have spent a life ducking doorframes. A hand took the hem of the curtain at the corner nearest the alley and lifted it, an inch, as you lift a blind to check the street. It was a large hand, with the knuckles of someone who has done a good deal of writing by hand and some gardening. It lifted two fingers. It lowered them.

I raised my own hand. I did not decide to. Then, because a man should answer the question put to him, I put up two fingers, and I brought them down.

The curtain fell back. I had my phone out by then, and I do not remember getting it out. I took one photograph at 7:41, and my thumb shook, and I took another. The amber was going out of the window at the edges. At 7:43 it was a lighter patch of brick with wide mortar, and rain on it, and a streetlamp's reflection in a puddle on the sill of bay 3. I lowered the phone and looked up with my own eyes. Brick. A bus went by, the 14 again, ticking, with its doors shut and four people in it who did not look at me, and I counted the bays against its lit windows. Five.

On the ride home I turned the phone toward the ceiling light. The first photograph showed what I had seen: the lit oblong, the curtains, the cardigan, the lifted hem, the hand with two fingers raised. The second showed brick. They sat next to each other in the roll, 7:41 and 7:43, as two photographs of the same wall taken two minutes apart by a man who had lost his mind between them.

I looked at the hand a long time, and then I had a thought, which I am putting down because a note to file leaves nothing out. A bricked window is the kind of place in which people get put. I thought it with a man's gray socks in my head and a joke about cigarettes and a struck line in a margin, and it took about the length of one stop. I put it away the way you turn a card face down in your wallet. I did not say it to anyone.
