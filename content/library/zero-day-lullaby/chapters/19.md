# Laundry Chute

The sluice room smells of bleach and boiled rubber. Ilse arrives in it at a run, in clogs two sizes too big, with a white bundle clutched to her chest and her deaf side turned to the door she has just come through. Anything could be behind her. Pille is the one who looks back. She does it twice, quickly, the way she looks at a monitor, and then she closes the door with her hip and does not let it bang.

They have come the back way, through the charting room and the equipment store and out by a door Ilse took for a cupboard. Pille does this ward with her eyes shut. She has done it, on certain nights, with someone's baby in each arm.

"He'll do the bays first," she whispers. "He's polite. He'll want to see that everyone is asleep."

A row of steel sinks. A bedpan washer the size of a wardrobe, ticking as it cools. On two chairs pushed together under the window lies the second porter, Mihkel, twenty-three years old, with his boot heels up on a linen cart and Saturday's paper tented over his face. He snores in two parts, a long rasp and a short whistle, like a kettle that has been taught manners.

"Your ward," Ilse says, very low.

"Kadri has the ward. Kadri has Bay Two and the cot in high dependency, and she's better at this than I am, so don't tell her." Pille steps round a mop bucket without looking at it. Her pencil has slid sideways in her bun and she shoves it straight. "What I can't have is that man on my floor with me on it. He knows my tram, dear. He's practically family. Mihkel, sweetheart, you have been a joy. Sleep."

Mihkel would sleep through the Last Judgement and then ask who had moved his chair. They go past him on their toes.

At the back wall is a steel hatch the size of an oven door. A printed sign says NO PEOPLE, and under it, in biro, in a hand Ilse knows from a drawing of a kettle, someone has added *(this means you, Rein)*. Pille hooks the hatch open with one finger. Warm air breathes out of the dark. It smells of bleach and milk and something else that Ilse decides to leave down there with the rest.

"Where does it go?"

"The linen bay under the east wing. Three floors." Pille shakes out an imaginary tablecloth. "Rein did it in 2016 for a bet and won two hundred euros and a week of light duties. Feet first, knees together, arms in. Like a bridesmaid."

"Is that how bridesmaids go?"

"It's how I went at my cousin's, and look how well I turned out."

Ilse looks at the hole. Her pulse has begun its old trick, and she feels it in her wrists, in her throat, in the bundle against her ribs. Ninety-four. "And then?"

"And then there are sheets. Go on, I'm right behind you. Mind the step." Pille hears herself, and her face folds. "Oh, I've caught it off him. Go!"

Ilse sits on the lip of the hatch. The steel is cold through the gown. She tucks the bundle under her chin, crosses her ankles, and lets go.

It is dark, and it is much faster than a bridesmaid. The tube turns once at the second floor and slams her shoulder into its wall with a boom she feels in her teeth. A clog leaves her foot. Then the dark opens under her and she drops through it into cotton, an enormous soft gasp of it, which closes over her to the chin and is warm.

Pille arrives a moment later, with a squeal that runs the length of the tunnel and comes back again, and lands across Ilse's shins.

"Oof. Sorry, dear. Sorry." Her head comes up out of the sheets, cap gone, bun askew. "Everyone in one piece? Nobody think about the bottom layers. It's mostly milk."

The heap is warm because the bay sits over the boiler pipes, and because the night's linen has been tipped here from three floors of sacks and nobody has yet carted it away. Ilse climbs down the slope of it on her hands and her one remaining clog. She still has the bundle. She has not let go of it at any point, which strikes her as a small and ridiculous triumph. Under the gown, the recorder lies warm against her ribs.

The bay is long and low, a cellar of white-lagged pipes and wheeled cages stacked with canvas sacks. As they pass the cages Pille takes an empty sack from the nearest and tucks it under her arm, the way another woman might pick up an umbrella. At the near end stands the service lift, a steel door with a little red number above it and, beside it, a green fire door with a push bar. At the far end, past a gallery where clean sheets hang from ceiling rails to air, are roller doors and the faint orange of a lamp.

Above the lift, the red number says 3. As Ilse watches, it says 2.

"That's him," says Pille.

"How do you know?"

"Nobody else is awake enough to use it. Hold on." She has already crossed to the fire door. From the pocket of her tunic she takes a black rubber wedge, as another woman might take out a handkerchief, and kicks it under the door so that it stands open on the cold stairwell. "Facilities put a safety on this lift in 2019. If that door is held open while the car is on its way down, the lift decides the building's on fire. It stops wherever it is, shuts its eyes, and waits for a man with a key. Rein found out with a thermos. Two hours."

"And the man with the key?"

"Comes at seven. Toomas. He has a bad back."

The red number says 1. Then the cable sings one long rising note, and stops. Something settles with a sigh between one floor and the next, and the bay goes quiet except for the pipes.

From inside the steel, high up, come three small knocks. Patient, like a man at a neighbour's door.

"Excuse me?" says a voice in the shaft. It is muffled and perfectly courteous. "Hello? I believe there is something the matter with your lift."

Pille's eyes go round with delight. "Oh, he's lovely," she breathes. "Run."

They go down the bay between the cages and in under the first rail of the gallery. Clean sheets hang here in rows from hooks in the ceiling, hundreds of them, delivered at midnight and left to cool before the morning round, and the draught from the dock door at the far end makes them all lean one way together, slowly, like wheat. Ilse runs with one foot in a clog and the other in a wet sock. She is halfway down the second row when she hears the hatch.

A clang, high behind her, the lid of something flung back. Then a boot on a rung, and another, the thin ring of a maintenance ladder inside the shaft. Of course there is a ladder. A lift in a hospital has more ways out than the hospital has. Two feet land on concrete.

Then nothing.

Pille's hand closes on her sleeve and they stand where they are, in the white, with their mouths shut.

He is in the bay. Ilse knows it the way she would know a stranger in a dark flat, by the shape of the quiet. But where? The cellar is all echo, concrete and pipe, and her good ear is turned toward the dock while the other one is an empty room. Every sound she gets arrives from the right or from nowhere. A man could be at her left shoulder, with his hands held a little in front of him, and she would not know until he coughed.

So she does not listen. She reads. It is what she does with a spectrogram when the loud bright lines are no use: she looks for the shape that does not belong. The sheets all lean toward the dock, all together, lazily. She lets her eyes go soft and travels along the rows.

Third row, to the left and ahead. A run of five sheets leans the other way, one after another, a slow push through the white, as if a hand were parting a curtain.

She does not point. Pointing is noise. She lifts the bundle in both arms and throws it as hard as she can into the deaf side, over the rail and down the next aisle. She does not hear it land. She sees a sheet jump forty feet away, and swing, and hang.

The slow push stops. It stays stopped. Then it turns, rustling, away from them and toward the thump.

"Now," whispers Pille.

They run. Behind them, a long way back and a little sad, the voice says, "Oh, dear."

"He's unwrapped the baby," says Pille.

"It was cables."

"It was a lovely baby. I'd got fond."

The dock door is chest-high and half open, and past it are snow, a ramp, the orange light, and a white van with its lights off and KETT in small letters on the side. The engine is not running. Through the windscreen Ilse sees the hands first: two mustard-coloured gloves on the black wheel, at ten and at two, precisely as the instructor taught them. They do not wave. The side door slides open before they reach it.

"Taavi, lamb," says Pille, falling in. "You are a prince among men and I will marry you."

Taavi says nothing. He nods once toward the back seat. He waits until the door has been dragged shut, which takes two tries because of the snow, and then, without lights and without a word, he lets off the handbrake.

The van is Madis's and it is electric, because Madis likes to be smug at traffic lights. It does not start. It simply begins, along the service road past the clinical-waste cages and the oxygen store, in a silence so complete that Ilse can hear the snow ticking on the roof. Taavi drives at a walking pace with his shoulders up round his ears, his eyes moving from mirror to windscreen to mirror. He is twenty-seven and panics well. He does not panic. He has not made one joke, not a meme, not a bullet point, and the absence of them sits in the van like a fourth passenger.

"Taavi," says Ilse. "How long have you been—"

One glove lifts from the wheel, a finger raised, and goes back. Wait.

At the end of the service road is the staff barrier, a red-and-white arm with a laundry lorry's sensor loop cut into the tarmac in front of it. And beside the arm, on the public side, with its engine ticking and its lights out, idles a black Audi. Its plate carries the blue strip and the two letters of a ministry. Exhaust unwinds from its tail into the orange light. Inside, the driver is lit from below by a phone. He is not looking at the barrier. He is looking at a screen.

Taavi does not slow and does not speed up. The arm rises for the van's weight on the loop. They glide under it, so close to the Audi's tinted rear window that Ilse could knock on it. In the back seat Pille has stopped breathing. In front, Taavi's gloves have not moved. The driver does not look up. The Audi stays exactly where it is, ticking, patient, a dark thing that has not yet been told what it is waiting for.

They reach the main road. Taavi switches on the lights. Ilse turns in her seat and looks back at the long block of the hospital, the third floor lit in a row like the decks of a ship, nothing moving in it. Three seconds of dark for the whole floor, and nobody has yet said whose hand is long enough to do that. Seven hundred metres on, Taavi lets out a breath through his nose, one long thin sound, and still says nothing.

It is Pille who breaks. A snort first, then her hand over her mouth, then the laugh itself, the huge laugh Ilse first heard in the corridor six nights ago, a laugh with no floor to it. She laughs until the van rocks, until her bun comes down and the pencil falls into her lap. She says "the lift," and "Toomas, with his back," and "the baby," and cannot go on. Her shoulders shake. She laughs until she is gasping.

Then she stops, quite quickly, as if a tap had been turned. Without looking, she reaches down beside her feet for the empty canvas sack she carried out from the bay under her arm the whole way, which is the most nurse-like thing Ilse has ever seen, and is sick into it, neatly, with her eyes shut.
