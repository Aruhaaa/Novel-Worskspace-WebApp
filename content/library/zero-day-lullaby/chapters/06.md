# The Shell Rack

In Singapore it is four in the afternoon and raining, and Dr Mei Lin Ong has arranged her chair so that the rain is behind her.

On the wall screen in the weighing room a window full of glass towers runs with water, lit from within like racks of servers. In front of it sits a woman of forty-one with her black hair in a clip, a grey cardigan, a lanyard worn the right way round and a glass mug of hot water with nothing in it. She has drunk hot water for as long as Ilse has known her, which is since 2022 and a stolen backup tape. Ong recovered the tape and returned it together with a spreadsheet of everything Tamm Incident Response had done wrong with the chain of custody. It was a very good spreadsheet.

The weighing room is a glass box at the end of the third floor where the spice merchants once weighed pepper against silver. The great brass scale still stands on its plinth, one pan hanging lower than the other since 1890. It is ten in the morning on Wednesday. Tuesday went in sleeping and in not putting things in tickets; Jaan left early with a bread bag held in both arms as though it were asleep, and Helle said he was off to feed the ducks. Four green cardamom pods lie in Ilse's coat pocket now, whole. She has not cracked one.

"Ilse. Good morning. Good afternoon, from here." Ong lifts the mug a centimetre in salute. "You wrote to me about a rack."

"Fourteen B."

Ong drinks some hot water. "Noted."

Ilse has learned to hear the weather in that word. Noted on a typo is a shrug. Noted on a late invoice is a sigh. This one is a barometer falling.

"Rack 14-B is leased on a standard colocation contract," Ong says, "by a company called Hartmann Edge Pte Ltd. Incorporated in 2023. Its registered address is a floor of post boxes in the financial district, which I am told are very fine post boxes."

"Who pays?"

"Hartmann Edge pays. On the due date, in full, by transfer, from a bank I would describe as unfashionable."

"Can I see what the rack does?"

"No."

"The badge logs. Who goes in and out."

"Not without a warrant, or the customer's consent, or a court order I have not been served with. I run a clean building, Ilse. It is the only thing I own." She sets down the mug. "I cannot show you logs."

"And then?"

Ong looks at her for the length of a squall. "Unofficially," she says, "the cooling bill for that rack is low."

"Low."

"A rack of that rating is billed as though it held forty servers. It draws about as much power as a kettle and rejects about as much heat. Whatever is in 14-B, it is not what the contract says. It is a bread bin with a very good address."

"Noted," says Ilse, and for the first time in four years Dr Ong's mouth moves.

A third square opens on the screen. The voice comes before the picture, big and warm and out of breath. "Hello? Hello hello hello? Is this the right pitch?" Then the picture: a man of thirty-five in the passenger seat of a parked car, in a shirt of many colours, with three telephones laid along the dashboard like birds on a wire. In the back seat, framed neatly in the window behind his head, stands a white goat. The goat regards the camera.

"Dr Ong, you said ten minutes. I have given you nine and a half and a goat."

One of the telephones shivers on the dashboard. He glances at it, then at the other two. "The bank, the regulator and the football," he says to Ilse's face, which has not asked. "In descending order of importance and ascending order of how often they ring."

"Mr Abara," says Ong. "I took the liberty of inviting a third party, Ilse. Chidi Abara is head of security at Kobo Lane, in Lagos. He lodged a complaint about my building."

"Complaint? No, no, no, doctor. A polite request for an explanation." He beams at Ilse with a smile that fills the screen. "Chidi. Don't mind Referee, he's for my uncle's party on Saturday. Now listen. You know when you phone your bank, yes? You wait, you hear the little music. You hear it, you don't hear it. You switch your head off, you're a goalkeeper in the last minute of a nil-nil, you're not thinking about the music. Then my sister rings her loan officer last week and says, Chidi, your hold music sounds like a church in a tunnel. And I say, Ngozi, it's forty dollars of marimba from a stock library. And after that I can't stop hearing it."

"Ilse," says Ilse.

"Ilse. Good. Now. Kobo Lane. Payments, Victoria Island, forty million customers, a call centre that never shuts. Every morning at two-forty Lagos time one call comes into our customer line, sits on hold for twenty-six minutes, and never says a word. Never presses a key. Never hangs up. The call centre says, a robot. I say, a scandal. Then my gateway starts getting knocked on at the same hour by addresses out of Dr Ong's building, and I think: Chidi, that is a through ball, and you are standing in the wrong half."

"Show me the music," Ilse says.

Chidi holds one of the three phones to the lens. It shows a ribbon of colour with a thick bright clutter along the top, forty dollars' worth of marimba. "My nephew's spectrum app. Look under it."

Under the clutter, faint as a watermark, a staircase. Eight steps, and again, and again.

Ilse pulls her own ribbon onto the screen beside it. Hers is from a ward and his from a hold line half a continent away, and they match step for step. "Watch the last one," she says. They watch. The last step of a staircase in the corner of Chidi's phone sags, a hair low, like a washing line when a bird lands.

"Ninety-four," says Ilse.

"Ninety-four seconds! I counted it on my fingers. Look, I ran out of fingers." He holds up all ten and then, ruefully, a thumb from the other hand. In the back seat the goat bleats once, with feeling.

"Noted," says Ong, very quietly, and that is two.

Ilse gets up and goes to the whiteboard where, in other centuries, the merchants' clerks chalked the price of pepper. She draws three dots in a row. Under the first she writes TALLINN, under the second SINGAPORE, under the third LAGOS. "A ward," she says. "A rack. A hold line. The ward hums it. The rack receives it. And the hold line..." She draws the arrow. "Sound in Tallinn. Bits in Singapore. Sound again in Lagos. It's one tune at both ends of one relay."

"And at two-forty in the morning it goes out of my hold line and down a phone line," Chidi says slowly, for once not sounding like a commentator. "To whoever dials in."

"To whoever stays on hold for twenty-six minutes and says nothing. A call that long is not a robot." She caps the pen. "It's an ear."

Nobody speaks. In the weighing room the radiator ticks. Behind Dr Ong the rain keeps falling past a thousand lit windows.

"What I have," Ilse says, "is a hundred and seventeen nights. From the fourth of September. It carries children's records. Weights, birth dates, names. The children of people who sign things."

Chidi whistles, one long falling note, and stops himself, glancing at the goat as if it might have a view.

Ong's hand has gone still on the mug. "The rack is older than your September," she says.

"How much older?"

"Unofficially." She is looking, not at Ilse, but at the rain. "By some time."

"Here is what I would like," Ilse says. "Something that doesn't exist. Each of us tells the others what we see. Nothing in writing, nothing in a ticket, nothing in a case file."

"Unofficially," says Ong.

"Unofficially."

"I'm unofficial all the time," says Chidi. "Ask my regulator." He grins, then, as if confessing to a priest, adds: "I should say. I found your Singapore address myself. Nobody told me. I do a little weekend scouting on other people's pitches. For prizes."

"Prizes," says Ong.

"The Dutch pay in euros. It's legal! It's called a bounty, doctor, there's a website."

"Noted," says Ong. "Unofficially." She has not touched the pen beside her mug, nor the pad beside the pen. Ilse looks at the blank page for as long as is polite. A woman who writes nothing down is either very stupid or very careful, and Ilse has never met anyone less stupid.

On the desk at her elbow her phone lies face up beside the notebook. Before the call she opened the privacy panel and switched off every microphone permission but the recorder's, the way she used to check the stove before leaving a flat. The list is still on the screen. As she looks, the toggle beside an entry called Sound Check slides itself from grey to green. A small green dot lights at the top of the glass.

She taps it off. It slides back. She taps it off again, and again, a game in which she is allowed no points. Without a word she lifts the phone and lays it in the pan of the great brass scale. The pan sinks. A hundred and ninety grams of something that listens.

Through the glass wall, Jaan has stopped in the corridor with a mug in his hand. He is not looking at the call. He is looking at her. Ilse tears a page from the notebook, writes WHY THURSDAY?, and holds it up to the glass. He reads it. He sets the mug on the radiator, takes a pencil from his vest and a page from Helle's invoice pad, and writes with his big back bent like a man at confession. Then he presses the sheet flat against the glass from his side.

TONIGHT. BRING THE NIGHTJAR LAPTOP.

She knows exactly where it is: in a sleeping bag at the bottom of her wardrobe, behind the winter boots, where he told her in 2019 to keep it dry. He taps the paper twice, once for each thing she is to remember, and is gone down the corridor, favouring the hip.

"Ilse?" says Ong. "Is there something I should note?"

"A colleague."

"Noted." The mug goes to her mouth and comes down again.

"You want to hear it?" Chidi has seized the nearest phone. "Listen. This is my hold line, live." He holds it to the microphone of his laptop, and out of the screen comes a tinny marimba loop, cheerful and a little sad, around and around. Ilse turns her right ear to the speaker and listens for the lullaby underneath. There is the marimba. A faint hiss. The click of the loop turning over. Ong's mug has stopped halfway to her mouth. On the brass scale the pan holds its hundred and ninety grams, perfectly still.

"You hear that?" says Chidi.

No one answers. Ilse shakes her head, slowly.

"No? Me neither. That's the problem."
