# Kringel at Midnight

The bellboy brings it up on a saucer, as if it were a cup of something.

He is perhaps nineteen, with a neck too long for the collar of the Hotel Tuletorn and the manner of a boy who has been told not to look at what he carries. The hotel stands behind the railway station on a street of tyre shops, eleven rooms and a lift that sighs at every floor, and Marit has taken the room at the back because it looks down on the tracks. She likes a thing that moves in the dark where she can see it. Freight wagons are going by below, one after another, with no lights at all.

"A gentleman left it at the desk, Mrs Saarinen," says the boy. "He said you'd know. He paid for the saucer."

"For the saucer."

"He said an envelope should never go up bare." The boy looks at the carpet. "He tipped very well, but in old notes. Soft ones. Like they'd been in a drawer."

She takes the envelope. It is cream, the weight of good cotton, her false name on the front in a hand she knows, upright and pale as a row of fence posts. She gives the boy two euros, which he does not expect, and when the door is shut she stands at the window and opens it with her thumb.

One sheet. Hint does not waste a sheet.

*Rebane, Pille. Harju Children's, neonatal, nights. Flat on Paldiski mnt, fourth floor. The nurse. Close her. The Committee prefers quiet, and prefers it before the weekend.*

No signature. He has never signed anything he could be asked to read aloud.

She reads it as she reads a forecast, for the word that carries the weather. Not *find*. Not *watch*. The woman on Pikk Street was to be kept alive because she was a door. The nurse is not a door. The nurse is somebody who held one open in September and then, in all likelihood, spent three months lying awake about it, and she is to be shut.

Marit folds the sheet in four and burns it in the basin with the hotel's matches. Down on the tracks a wagon goes by marked in white chalk with a number and a word she cannot read. She finds that she is not thinking about the nurse at all. She is thinking about the door on Pikk Street, which showed no mark. About the old man on his landing, whom nobody on her card had named. About a key ring that went down the stairs in a locksmith's pocket and came back out of it, she would bet her pension, to open some other door that night.

Somebody had given that man a key. The firm had four rings, and one was in a dead man's pocket, and three were not. She knows how you read thin ice: not where it has broken, but where something heavy has already stood.

She puts on the waxed coat. In the bag with the knitting, between the grey sock and the sock needles, she lays a second sock, the one for show.

* * *

The block in Mustamäe is the colour of porridge that has been left out. Nine floors, a hundred windows, a stairwell where the light is on a timer that gives you eleven seconds and then takes the dark back. Marit has done a great many stairwells. She finds the name on the box, KUUSK, H., fifth floor, and climbs with her hand on the rail, and every landing smells of somebody's dinner going cold.

The fifth floor smells of almonds.

It comes out under a door at the end of the corridor, warm and sweet and slightly burned at the edges, laid over something sharper, nail varnish remover perhaps, and under that a third smell that Marit has met in kitchens in three countries and has never been able to name until now. It is fear that has been going on a long time. It smells like a house in which the radio is kept on to stop the quiet getting in.

She rings. Inside, a timer goes off, someone swears in a small voice, and the door opens on its chain.

An eye. An orange frame. "It's midnight," says Helle Kuusk.

"I know. I do the stairs."

"You what?"

"The stairs. For the housing association. They cut the hours, so we do them at night when nobody's in the way." Marit lifts the bucket she has borrowed from the landing and shows it. In the bucket, wrapped in a cloth, are a mop-head and a tin of floor wax. She has not had to say a false thing with her face in many years, and it comes easily, because it is nearly true: she has always been a woman who gets into houses by being useful. "There's been a complaint. Someone's been leaving things on the fifth. I only want to see if the fire door shuts."

"The fire door." Helle looks along the corridor at it. It is, in fact, propped with a doormat. "Oh, that's me. I prop it for the smell. I'm baking. I'm so sorry, I always forget."

"It's a good smell."

"It's kringel." The chain comes off. "You'd better come and tell me it isn't burnt. Nobody else will. Come, come."

It is that easy. Marit has noticed this about frightened people. They will let in the first person who looks as though she is carrying a mop.

* * *

The kitchen is small and yellow and has been recently, thoroughly cleaned. A table for two. A calendar from a dentist. On the fridge, held by a magnet shaped like a strawberry, a photograph: a young man in a black gown and a flat hat on a lawn, grinning with all his teeth into a sun that is not an Estonian one. His mother's chin. His mother's ears, which stick out a little and have been cut into a fashionable hairstyle to hide it.

And the oven door stands wide. Helle works at it with a cloth over her hand and a tray of pastries that she turns, and turns back, and tilts to the light. The pastries are long twists, plaited like hair, glossed with egg and heaped with almonds and raisins. There are four trays already on the table. There is a fifth on the windowsill. There is flour on the floor, and on Helle's cardigan, and in the cleft of one orange earpiece.

"Do you know I haven't baked at night since Gert left?" She talks as she works, over her shoulder, a fast bright run of sentences with no commas in it. "Not once. Eight months. At night I used to do the whole week, Sunday to Sunday, because he'd come in at eleven with his friends from the dormitory and eat everything standing up. Now there is no one to eat it. I take it to the office. The office eats it. Well, it did." Her voice goes up and comes down. "I'm sorry. You don't want to hear. Have you eaten? Sit, sit. Put your bucket anywhere."

Marit sits. She puts the bucket by the radiator. She does not take off her coat. In the heat of the oven her cheeks are already beginning to sting from the cold of the stairs.

"Your son is away."

"In Melbourne. Studying logistics. Which is a thing I can say in front of my sister." She laughs, and the tray rattles. "He has a visa. It's a student visa, you know, it has to be renewed, and they like to see enough money in the account, and a stamp, and a letter, and I'm sorry, I'm talking and talking, aren't I? It's the almonds. They make me garrulous."

"Is it a difficult visa?"

"It is when the stamp is late." Helle puts down the tray. For a moment, with her back to the room, she is perfectly still, and in the stillness the oven ticks as it cools. "It is always late, this year."

Marit takes the sock out of the bag.

She does it without ceremony. A grey sock, half made, the heel turned, four needles standing out of it like the quills of a small animal. She sets it on the table between the trays. Helle turns round with the cloth in her hand and looks at it. Her eyes go to the sock, to the needles, to the hand that laid it down, with its little finger gone at the first joint, and then, slowly, to Marit's face.

She has expected a gun. It is in her eyes: she has been expecting one for four days. She has been expecting somebody at that door with a gun, and what has come instead is a woman of fifty-two with a mop and a sock, and the relief and the terror arrive in her together and cancel, and she sits down on the kitchen stool with her whole body.

"Oh," says Helle. "Oh, it's you."

"Who do you think I am?"

"You're not from Friday."

"No."

"Then you're from the other ones." The orange glasses come off. She rubs her eyes with the heels of her hands, and the flour goes into her lashes. "They said there would be others. In the beginning. A man in Vienna said it to us across a table, very pleasantly. *Ladies and gentlemen, you will be dealing with two kinds of friend.*"

Marit says nothing. It is the oldest thing she knows. You say nothing, and you let the sea come in over the gunwale at its own speed, and you do not offer a hand until the person has decided to take it.

"I'm not going to run," says Helle. "I've got nowhere to run. My sister's in Haapsalu, and she would only tell me I should have married a dentist." She wipes her glasses on the cardigan and puts them on again, crooked. "You'll want tea."

"I will have whatever there is. I have all night."

"All night," Helle repeats. She nods, as if it were a reasonable sum. "Yes. So have I."

* * *

The talking comes in the end like a stuck window going up. It goes up in jerks. Helle makes tea; Helle burns her wrist on the kettle and runs it under the tap; Helle talks about the cooperative that owns the block, the leak in the bathroom, a woman on the sixth who feeds pigeons and will be the death of them all. Marit sits and lets her. A boat rocks until the wind has finished with it. Then at ten to one, with the fifth tray cooling on the sill and the almond smell thick in the room, Helle sits down opposite her with both hands round her cup and says, to the cup:

"Friday at four."

Marit waits.

"He comes on Fridays. At four. He rings the bell twice, he wipes his feet, he takes his shoes off in the hall like a good boy, and he sits just where you're sitting. A pleasant man. A good watch. He says he is from the Directorate, and he says the word like a brand of washing powder, you know, a thing that has always been in the cupboard." She turns the cup. "He began in September. Before that, it was the visa. Gert's stamp was lost, you see. Lost in the post, in the ministry, in Canberra, I never understood where. And this man was so kind. He said he knew somebody who knew somebody. In two days the stamp turned up." The cup stops. "Since then, every Friday, he asks me how the week was. And I tell him."

"What do you tell him?"

"Where Ilse went. Whom she saw. What she said at lunch." Helle's mouth goes thin. "She is clever, my firewall. She doesn't say anything at lunch. But she has a way of putting her head to one side. I could tell him that. I have told him that."

"And the old man?"

"Jaan?" The name does something to her face. It goes still, and then it goes loose, the way a sleeper's does. "Never Jaan. They never asked about Jaan. I said to myself, they leave Jaan alone, that is why I do it, that is the arrangement. I was so careful. I asked and asked. *And Mr Tamm? Is he quite safe?* And the man said, my dear Mrs Kuusk, he is the safest man in Tallinn." She puts the heel of her hand to her mouth. "On Wednesday I was in the office at six. I went home. I made soup. And then they rang me at ten to nine, and I was eating it."

Marit looks at the fridge. A boy in a gown, on a lawn, in a bright foreign sun.

"You did this for the stamp."

"For the stamp. And then for the next stamp. And then because I couldn't stop, you know, because if I stopped he would have to ask why I'd stopped." Helle laughs, a dreadful little sound. "That's how you do it. They don't make you do a thing. They only make you unable to stop."

"And before the stamp." Marit chooses the words slowly. They are the ones Hint slipped, on the terminal bench in Helsinki, and corrected so smoothly that she almost let them go. "Before it was a visa. What did the firm make?"

The cup goes down. Helle looks at it for a very long time.

"We made a key," she says. "Jaan made it. Ilse helped him. I did the paper. It was 2017. We were so proud, you wouldn't believe, we had champagne in the weighing room. A key that fitted one particular lock. And a man in Vienna paid us for it, and said he had two buyers, so that it would be fairer. Two names on a contract. I signed under Jaan's. I never read their names, only mine. I read mine three times, to be sure I had spelled it right."

"Two buyers."

"Two kinds of friend."

The oven ticks. In the corridor the stair light clicks off, and nobody comes to put it on.

Marit has been a woman of few words for most of her life, and finds that this is a night on which she needs none. She sees it whole, the way you see a shore when the fog rises: a thing made in a spice warehouse and sold in Vienna and bought twice. By a Directorate whose man takes his shoes off in a hall. And by someone who calls itself a Committee and pays in softened notes and says *our instrument* by mistake. Whoever made the weapon sold it to the hand that is now hunting its maker. They made the lock, and the people who bought the key have sent her to close the door.

She does not ask why. She finds, with something near surprise, that she is not able to anymore.

"Mrs Kuusk," she says.

"Helle."

"Helle. Do not be here on Friday at four."

Helle's head comes up. The eyes behind the orange glasses are very large. "I can't be anywhere else."

"Then be somewhere else *inside* your head, and keep the door shut. I cannot do more tonight." She stands. She puts the grey sock back in the bag, needles last, the points through the wool. "The kringel is not burnt."

"It's very good, I think." The ghost of the old Helle comes into her face and stays for a second. "Take one. Take them all. It's only me and the freezer."

Marit takes one. It is still warm, long and glossy and plaited, heavy with almonds, and she wraps it in a paper napkin from the table and puts it in her coat pocket. The others she leaves where they lie, four trays and a fifth on the sill, for the woman at the oven to push into the freezer, or eat standing up, or carry into a dawn that is coming whether or not anyone bakes for it.

On the landing the light goes on for her without being asked.

The street is white and empty. A tram bell sounds somewhere past the blocks, thin and a long way off, and the snow is coming down again, straight and fine. She turns up her collar and walks. The pastry sits in her pocket against her hip, warm, and smelling of almonds, and she does not eat it.
