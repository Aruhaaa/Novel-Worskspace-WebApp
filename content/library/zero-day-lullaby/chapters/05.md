# Night Shift

The herring arrives at seven minutes past ten, in a jar, inside a handbag, inside a grandmother.

Pille Rebane smells it before she sees it: vinegar, onion, dill, drifting down the corridor at the hour when the ward lights go amber and the parents have been sent home to dream in grams. The grandmother is seventy-eight, in a quilted coat the colour of mustard and a headscarf knotted like a verdict, and she has got through two sets of doors and a lecture on hand gel to the incubator room, where she stands over her granddaughter's perspex roof holding the jar up to the light as a priest holds up a chalice.

"Mrs Ilves, dear," says Pille. "She's on tube feeds. She has the stomach of a thimble."

"It is for the mother. She isn't eating. A rake in a nightdress."

"The mother is home asleep. You sent her."

"Then it is for the child when she wakes. For the iron."

"For the iron." Pille takes the jar in both hands with the gravity due to a relic. "Rein!"

Rein the porter arrives from nowhere at the speed of a wardrobe on castors. He is six foot four and patient as a hill, and in eleven years on the night desk he has removed from this ward a birthday cake with its candles lit, a live crab, a harmonium and, once, a priest. He bends and offers Mrs Ilves an elbow. "Come, Memm, the lift is warm," he says, in the voice of a man who has done this before, and the grandmother goes, issuing instructions over her shoulder about the correct temperature of milk.

Pille puts the jar in the staff fridge, on the shelf she labelled BIOHAZARD (HERRING) years ago in anticipation of exactly this, and goes round.

She does her rounds backwards, so that she finishes with Ott. He lives in Bay Two, thirty-one days old and 1,640 grams, with a face like a walnut with a grievance, and he came off the oxygen on Friday. "Good evening, your majesty," she tells him. "Any complaints?" Ott yawns, which is a complaint, and goes back to sleep with one fist against his cheek. In Bay Four she stops by the far shelf. A grey owl and a tartan owl sit with a clean circle of shelf between them where a white stone used to be. The tall woman took it away on Monday, in a freezer bag. Thirteen speakers now. With one fewer stone to supervise, the owls look sulky.

On her way back she passes the charting room, its glass wall lit blue by the records machine, and does what she has done every night since Monday: she doesn't look in, and sees it anyway.

She does not like to think about September, so she thinks about it all the time, the way a tongue visits a tooth. It was a Thursday, ten past two in the morning. Evald was in Madeira with his wife and the ward was quiet as a drawer. A man in a Pesa-blue fleece rang the buzzer and held up a toolbox to the camera like a baby to be admired. A pleasant man of forty with a pleasant face, and a lanyard turned round the wrong way so that the name wouldn't show. "Aksel," he said. "Sound-pack calibration. I did warn Evald. Didn't he say? Typical." He knew her name. He knew Evald's. He needed an hour at the records machine in the charting room, for something he called the audio endpoint, and she had been on her feet since the evening before and was thinking about toast.

She let him in. He sat in the glass box under the lights with his back to her, whistling, and at ten to three he unfolded a napkin and put fifty euros under her mug. "For the coffee fund," he said. She said no. He said it wasn't for her, it was for the fund, and the fund was surely a thing worth supporting, and she said all right. The kettle in the staff room is new. It has an excellent whistle, and every time it sings she hears Aksel's pleasant voice saying typical.

Before he left he put a knitted owl on his head and said, "Put me on the Wall of Heroes." The Wall of Heroes is in the staff room: photographs of every plumber, porter and man who ever unblocked the milk-kitchen sink. She took his picture on her telephone, and he grinned and stuck up a thumb. She never pinned it to the wall. She has not looked at it since.

* * *

At twenty to one the buzzer goes, and on the little screen at the desk a man in a soft leather jacket is holding a laminated card up to the lens like a child showing a drawing.

"Leonid," says the speaker. "From the ministry. Facilities. I am so sorry for the hour."

Pille would let in a lamp post that said please like that. He is not tall. Neat grey hair, black wool gloves with the fingertips cut away, and a smell of clean machine oil that reminds her, absurdly, of her grandmother's sewing machine. His voice is low and unhurried. His hands are very clean and very still. He stands in the corridor looking at the speakers on their shelves the way a man looks at a lock.

"I am doing a small survey of the sound equipment," he says. "For the insurers. They are particular."

"At one in the morning?"

"The insurers do not sleep." A small apologetic smile. "Only a few questions. In the past months, has anyone serviced the speakers? A technician, from outside the hospital?"

"No," says Pille. "Nobody. Evald does everything. Evald's a saint, he's got a very small car." Her eyes go to the dome at the end of the corridor, and to the charting room, and to the owl on the nearest shelf, and back to the dome. "We have a lot of pigeons. On the roof. Which is nothing to do with it."

"A very small car," Leonid agrees. "And in September, for example. In the autumn. There was no one?"

"September was chaos, dear, I couldn't tell you. We had the measles scare, we had the, the boiler. No. No one."

He nods, with the patience of a man listening to tumblers fall. "You have been very helpful." He turns to go, and turns back. "You take the tram home?"

"The four."

"A cold night for the four."

At the door he stops on the threshold, where the floor drops by two fingers' breadth for the trolley ramp. He is not looking at her face. He is looking at her clogs. "Mind the step," he says.

She looks down. There is only the lip of rubber that has been there nineteen years, the one she has tripped on twice. When she looks up he is gone, and the door has sighed shut on its arm, and her knees have turned to cooked noodles. She makes tea in the kettle that cost fifty euros. It whistles at her like a witness.

* * *

At three o'clock the hum begins again.

Evald did something with the app on Monday afternoon. He told her he had turned the speakers down by two thirds, and he had; he had not turned them off. The tune comes in under the ward's ordinary noises, under the incubator fans and the monitors and the creak of the radiator, soft as a woman humming through a wall. Thirteen throats now instead of fourteen. Pille stands in Bay Two with her hand on the cot rail, the way she saw the tall woman do, and feels it come up through the steel into her palm.

Ott's mother cannot be here at three in the morning, and his chart says he is to have skin when possible. Pille unbuttons her tunic, lifts him out in his tiny hat and nappy, and ties him against her chest in the stretchy wrap. The recliner squeaks, and the squeak is unbearable with that hum behind it, so she lowers herself instead onto a folded duvet on the floor beneath the window, her back to the radiator, her knees up. His heartbeat comes through her breastbone fast as a bird's. He smells of warm bread and milk.

She hums. She cannot help it: it is what you do with a baby against your chest, and she picks, God help her, the only tune in the room. Three notes. Then the next.

The nearest stone, on the shelf three metres off, wavers. It is like hearing a second singer find the key, a note sliding to meet hers. She goes sharp on purpose. It goes sharp. She goes flat, and it goes flat with her, a hair behind, like a child copying a grown woman across a kitchen. She stops. It holds her last note a moment longer than it should, then lets itself down to where it was before, a shy animal returning to its burrow.

"Oh, my dear Lord," whispers Pille Rebane.

The laugh comes up in her, the huge one, and she smothers it in Ott's hat, because the laugh is terror in its good coat. The thing is listening. It has been listening all along, to her shift after shift, to the cot-side secrets and the swearing at the kettle and the gallows jokes about the pathologist, and the ring on its foot has glowed the plain white of nothing at all.

Ott sleeps. Ott has heard worse.

When her hands have stopped she lays him back in his cot, tucks the owl in beside him and goes to the charting room. The machine knows her. She signs in under her own name. In the corner of the screen is a tab called Events that nobody has ever opened, and she opens it. A long grey list scrolls up, line after line of small print she half understands: Audio endpoint, active, 02:59. Audio endpoint, stopped, 03:41. The same pair again, and again, down and down until the scroll bar gives out and the list goes on without it. She holds her telephone up and photographs the screen, twice, steadying her elbow against the glass, in case anyone should ever want to know what it says.

Her thumb slips on the gallery. Back through the pictures it flies: the Christmas tree in the lounge, Rein in a paper crown, a pickled herring in a jar. Then September. A pleasant man in a Pesa-blue fleece with a knitted owl on his head, grinning, one thumb up.

She told Evald. On Monday afternoon he came round asking whether anyone had been near the charting-room machine, and she said no, and then, because she is a woman who cannot leave a silence alone, she said, only the speaker man, in September, I've even got a photograph, he had an owl on his head. And Evald had gone the colour of the milk-kitchen tiles and said, show it to nobody. How kind, she thought at the time. How he looks after us.

She has not told the police, because she does not know which police. She has not told the director, because the director has a pen and a way of looking at the pen. She has not told the tall woman with the one good ear, who came out of the snow with a cardamom pod in her pocket and asked about technicians in a voice that already knew. The camera in the corridor was watching when Aksel came, and she has been praying that it keeps only thirty days.

Pille takes her telephone to the window. Beyond the glass the snow comes down through the car-park lights in slow handfuls. She scrolls to the call she made at seven minutes past three on Monday, the only call of her life to a woman with half her hearing, and rests her thumb over the number. Not now. Nine o'clock, in daylight, when she is not the woman who took fifty euros and a kettle.

"Because someone should know what is on my floor," she says to the sleeping ward, and the thirteen white stones, listening, hold their notes.
