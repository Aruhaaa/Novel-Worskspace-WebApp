# The Flat Note

The nurse does not say hello. She holds her telephone up to the ceiling of a room Ilse has never seen and lets the line carry whatever is up there.

Ilse lies on her left side with the deaf ear buried in the pillow, the way she has slept since she was six, and presses the phone to the ear that works. What comes down the line is thin as a hair. A hum, closed-mouthed, eight notes and then the same eight again. It is not a woman. A woman wanders. These notes land in the exact middle of themselves and leave on time.

"Do you hear it?" The voice is whispering, which in a hospital at seven minutes past three is a courtesy to the dark. "I'm so sorry. I know what the hour is. Pille Rebane, night nurse, Harju Children's, the neonatal ward. I have your number from a card."

"Which card?"

"An old gentleman left it on Thursday with a box of kringel for the staff. Enormous hands, I could have fitted both of mine inside one. His vest was buttoned wrong, every button a hole out, and I didn't like to say." A trolley wheel squeaks somewhere behind her. "He sat an hour in the parents' lounge first, looking up at the shelves like a man at a christening. Then he said, 'If the speakers ever sing, call Ilse.' I put the card in my apron and forgot it, because the speakers sing all the time, they have a sound pack, rain and sea and a mother's heartbeat. But never this. At four minutes past three every one of them began the same tune, together, and I stood there in my clogs like a cow at a gate for three whole minutes before I remembered."

Ilse is sitting up. She does not remember deciding to. "How many speakers?"

"Fourteen. I counted twice."

"Are the babies awake?"

"Asleep, all of them, like little loaves. It's only me that's awake."

"Don't unplug anything. Don't let anyone restart anything. I'll be there in twenty minutes."

"He said you'd say that," says Pille Rebane, and the line goes back to the hum.

Ilse dresses without the lamp. In the hall she takes a green cardamom pod from the saucer by the door and puts it in her coat pocket, whole, where it joins the lint and Friday's tram ticket. Then the laptop bag, the recorder wrapped in a sock against the cold, the stairs.

The taxi takes eleven minutes, most of them behind a plough whose amber light turns the snow the colour of weak tea. The dashboard says minus eleven. The driver's radio promises more of the same. Ilse sits with her good ear to the window, a poor arrangement for conversation that suits her fine, and watches Tallinn go past in black and white: shuttered kiosks, a tram asleep at its terminus, snow lying along its roof like a draught excluder.

Harju Children's Hospital is a long block of lit windows on the Pelgulinna side, with snow on every ledge as thick as icing. The neonatal ward is on the third floor behind two sets of doors and a sign asking visitors to sanitise their hands and lower their voices. Pille Rebane opens the inner door before Ilse has pressed the buzzer twice. She is round and quick and smells of chlorhexidine, with a pencil pushed through a grey-brown bun and a laugh somewhere behind her face that has been told to keep it down.

"You're tall," Pille says.

"Yes."

"He didn't say you were tall."

"What did he say?"

"That you'd be the one who listens." Pille watches her turn so that the right ear faces the nurse and the left side faces the door, and has the grace not to ask. "Blue covers over your boots, there. Hands to the elbow, like a surgeon."

While Ilse scrubs, Pille fishes in her apron and comes out with a card gone soft at the corners. Tamm Incident Response, Pikk Street. On the back, in pencil, capital letters: ILSE, and a mobile number. The sevens are crossed. In all of Tallinn one man still crosses his sevens.

"Did he give a name?"

"Only yours."

Ilse turns the card over twice and puts it in her pocket with the pod.

The ward is warm as a kitchen. It smells of milk powder and warm plastic and the sweetish rubber of oxygen tubing, and under that, now that she stands in it with her coat off and her hands raw with gel, it hums.

The hum sits in the air the way a smell does. She turns her head to find where it comes from, and the left half of the world, as always, offers her nothing. A sound with no direction is something a woman with one ear learns to live with, but she has never liked it.

"Where is it loudest?"

"Everywhere," Pille says. "That's what's wrong with it."

The corridor runs the length of the ward, with the bays opening off it like rooms in a very small, very hot hotel. In Bay One two incubators glow green, and on the shelf above each sits a knitted owl. In Bay Two, owls. In the milk kitchen, owls. In the parents' lounge a whole parliament of them, grey and brown and one in tartan with a single button eye. Between the owls stand the speakers: smooth white stones the size of a fist, each with a thin ring of light round its foot. The rings glow the flat white of things doing nothing at all.

Ilse unwraps the recorder, sets it on the nearest shelf between two owls and presses the red button. The clock above the milk-kitchen door says 03:24.

"The Thursday knitters," Pille says, following her look. "Seventy-one owls at the last count. They're meant to go home with the babies, but the babies already have more owls than they can carry. I think the shelves are where the supervising gets done."

"White means idle?"

"White is idle. When the sound pack plays, the ring breathes, slow, in and out, like it's asleep. It isn't breathing now."

"Are you frightened?"

"Dear, I've worked nights in this place nineteen years. Dead is very quiet. This is worse." The laugh arrives at last, enormous, far too big for the hush, and Pille claps a hand over it and turns an apology on the nearest incubator. The baby inside, no bigger than a bag of sugar, sleeps on.

In Bay Three a cot stands made up for a four o'clock admission, its sheet turned down into a neat white envelope. Ilse lays her palm flat on the steel rail. It is trembling. A thin steady thrum at the pace of the notes, like the chest of a cat that has made up its mind to purr. She has never been able to place a sound by ear. But steel carries what air only mentions.

She goes down the bay rail by rail, cot to cot, one hand out like a woman feeling for a switch in a dark room. In Bay Four the thrum thickens. Under the far shelf the rail hums so hard her teeth take notice. Above it, between a grey owl and the tartan one, sits a white stone with its ring dimly lit.

"That one," Ilse says.

"You haven't even looked at it."

"I looked at the rail."

She takes out her phone, wakes the spectrogram she has used for ten years and holds it toward the stone. A ribbon of colour starts to crawl across the screen from left to right, the way a seismograph draws. She has explained it to juries as sheet music written by the sound itself: height is pitch, a bright line is a note, and time goes by sideways. Tonight it draws a staircase, eight bright steps, then the same staircase again, and again.

Pille hums along with it, three notes, half a beat behind. "I know that one," she says. "I know it from somewhere."

Ilse's own mouth has made the shape of the fourth before she shuts it.

"Watch the last step," she says instead. "The eighth."

For a minute and a half there is nothing: staircase, staircase, staircase. Then the last step of one staircase sags, a hair below where the others put it, like a washing line when a bird lands on it, and tightens again.

"Oh," says Pille. "Like a hiccup."

"Wait."

The ward clock has a red second hand. Ilse counts from the sag. Staircase, staircase, staircase. At ninety-four it sags again, on the dot. She does not need a third but waits for one anyway, because waiting for the third is the only discipline she trusts. Ninety-four.

"Like an appointment," she says.

"I don't like appointments I didn't make." Pille's pencil has come out of her bun and is turning in her fingers.

"Has anyone been near the speakers since the summer? A technician, anyone with a tool bag?"

"Nobody. Only Evald, and he's IT, he's always about." Pille's eyes go to the end of the corridor, where a dome of smoked glass the size of half a grapefruit watches the doors, and come back. "Nobody," she says again, to the owl.

Ilse says nothing. She files the dome where she files the things she does not write down.

The inner doors thump. A man comes down the corridor in a puffer jacket the colour of a traffic cone, a lanyard slung on the outside of it, over pyjama trousers printed with small sailing boats. His hair has the shape of a pillow. This is Evald Olesk, head of IT, who lives six minutes away in Pelgulinna and has made it in four.

"Pille, you should have rung me first."

"I rang the desk."

"And the desk rang me, so." He looks at Ilse's laptop bag and at the blue plastic on her boots. "And this is?"

"A friend," Pille says.

"From outside," says Ilse. "Tamm Incident Response."

"We have a vendor for this." He doesn't say which. His hands are already moving. "It's the sound pack. Pesa pushed a firmware on Thursday, it's cloud-driven, they've done it before. I had whale song in the isolation rooms for six hours in October. A scheduling bug, a time zone, somebody in Tartu pressing the wrong button. It's the bays, the isolation rooms, high dependency, the corridor, the milk kitchen, the lounge, the staff room, and look, the babies are fine, it's harmless, it's a lullaby. I'll go down to the plant room, restart the building controller, and it's cleared in ten minutes."

Thursday, Ilse notes, and sets it beside the card.

She counts while he talks, folding a finger under her thumb for every room he names. Four bays. Two isolation rooms. High dependency. The corridor, which has two. Milk kitchen. Lounge. Staff room. Twelve speakers, and fourteen on the shelves. He says nothing of the charting room, the glass-walled box beside the nurses' desk where the records computer sits, and nothing of the equipment store at the far end. He talks with his whole body, a hand for every room, but whenever a sentence nears those two doors the hands go into his pockets.

"Please don't restart anything yet," she says.

"With respect, I don't know you."

"No. If the hum stops after a restart, you won't know what stopped it. Ten minutes."

"And if the Director asks why I let a stranger loose in the ward at half past three?"

"Tell her the nurse called a specialist."

"The nurse did," says Pille.

Olesk looks from one to the other, and the air goes out of the puffer jacket without the jacket moving. "Ten minutes," he says. "I'm timing."

Ilse goes back to Bay Four. She lifts the stone from between its owls (the tartan one topples, and she stands it on its feet) and works the cable out of its foot. The ring dies. The hum does not. It was never only this one's. It comes from the isolation room across the corridor and from the corridor itself and from every shelf in the ward, and it does not stumble. The next staircase goes up and comes down exactly as before, and on her phone the stripes do not so much as flinch. At the ninety-fourth second the last step sags, on the dot, in somebody else's voice.

She stands holding the dead speaker in both hands like a bird that has flown into a window.

"It isn't being played," she says. "If it were played, it would have a player, and I've just unplugged the loudest one. They're not singing it to each other either. They're reading it, off something, the way you read a ticker along the bottom of a screen. One symbol after another, and none of it meant for us." Above the milk-kitchen door the red hand goes round. "And the clock is still reading."
