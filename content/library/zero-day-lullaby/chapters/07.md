# Foil

The tram to Ülemiste has snow on the inside of its windows. Somebody's breath has frozen into ferns on the glass, and Ilse sits with her deaf side against them and her good ear to the aisle, the way she has sat on every tram since she was six. A recorded woman announces each stop in Estonian, then in English, in the voice of a person who has never once been late.

The phone is in her coat pocket, against her hip. It would have been tidier to leave it in the brass pan on Pikk Street, but a phone left alone in a room listens to the room, and she would rather it listened to a tram. Nobody on a tram says anything worth stealing.

At the stop after the bus station a woman in a fur hat that has outlived several furs lowers herself into the seat beside her.

"Is it free, this place?"

"Yes."

"Good. I am not going to talk to you." She settles a string bag on her knees like a cat she has decided to tolerate. "My grandson gave me a pebble for the kitchen. The white one, from the television. It knows everything, he says. In the morning it says good morning. So I say good morning back, and it says nothing. Such manners."

Ilse turns her right ear to her. The woman has a face with the weather already in it. "It's waiting for you to ask it something."

"I have been asking things for seventy-nine years and nobody answered. Why should a pebble?" She nods at the door, where a poster has been stuck to the glass: a white speaker like a river stone on a windowsill full of snow, and under it a line in blue capitals. KODU KUULAB. "That one. That is my pebble. The home listens. I would like to see it try."

Ilse reads the line twice, once for the Estonian and once for what it means. Someone was paid to write that, and someone else signed it off.

Under the fold of her coat she brings the phone out just far enough to read the screen. The privacy panel is still open from the morning. She switched off every microphone permission in it at the office, again on the platform, and again at the stop, and each time the entry called Sound Check has gone back to green, patient as a dog at a door. She switches it off once more and puts the phone away. It is a game she has not yet worked out how to win.

"You have the face of someone who was told to wait," says the woman, not looking up.

"Yes."

"Don't. Nobody ever comes." When Ilse stands at Ülemiste, the woman adds, to the window, "If it answers you, say good morning from Linda."

* * *

The block on Peterburi tee is eleven storeys of grey panels, with a plane going over every four minutes, low enough that the windows hum. Her flat is on the second floor, which in this country means the first one above the street. She takes the stairs. The lift smells of wet dog, and in eight years she has never once seen it arrive when called.

The flat has a bedroom, a bathroom and a kitchen, and the kitchen has a birch-ply table, two chairs, and a window over the car park with the airport fence beyond it. One chair is for guests who have not come. She hangs her coat on the other, lays the phone face down on the table, and sits with her good ear toward it and the teapot-shaped clock above the sink in the corner of her eye. Ten to two. She does not put the kettle on. A kettle is noise, and a phone that wants to speak will speak when it thinks it is alone, so she gives it the room.

At two minutes to two, the table begins to tremble under her forearm.

She lays her palm flat on the birch. It is the cot rail again: the same faint prickling in the heel of the hand, as if the wood were thinking about something. She lowers her cheek to the table, right ear down, and there it is, so low it seems to belong to the building. The tune. The eight steps, rising and falling and rising, but an octave down, the same notes sung by a bass instead of a soprano, in the register where a refrigerator lives. Nobody notices a refrigerator.

She counts. The red tip of the second hand goes round, and round, and at ninety-four the eighth note sags, a washing line under a bird.

Her pulse, she notices, is keeping time with it. She tells it to stop. It does not.

It is not the ward's tune. It is the ward's tune in her kitchen, coming out of a thing that she bought, that she charges, that has ridden in her pocket past every wall she has walked through since September. A workstation on the ward hums what it knows to the speakers that listen. This one has been listening to her. And a thing that listens must have somewhere to put what it hears.

She gets up, goes to the wall beside the fridge and presses her good ear to the wallpaper. On the other side lives Mr Härm, who is eighty-one, who took out his hearing aid in 2019 on principle, and whose daughter in Stockholm sends him the weather each morning at seven-fifteen through a white pebble at a volume designed for ships. Through eleven centimetres of concrete, at the ninety-fourth second, a small round voice clears its throat. One note, a little flat. In the dark of his kitchen a ring of light will be breathing, and Mr Härm will not see it.

Ilse takes her cheek off the wall. She goes to the drawer beside the knives.

She has wrapped phones in foil in boardrooms in four countries. She tears off a length as long as her arm and folds the phone into it like a sandwich for a very small giant, three layers, seams creased flat, the crinkle enormous in the kitchen. It stops anything that travels by radio: the mobile network, the wifi, the Bluetooth by which a phone whispers to earbuds. It does not stop a tune. Through the foil the hum goes on, slightly embarrassed.

"Yes," says Ilse, to the parcel. "I know."

On the shelf above the fridge sits the baking tin Helle gave her last Christmas: square, black steel, a lid printed with a robin, still faintly buttery. Ilse drops the parcel in and presses the lid down. The table stops trembling. The voice becomes a thought. She sits and counts to ninety-four with her teeth together, and the flat note comes round anyway, small as a pinprick, from somewhere inside a tin.

She carries the tin to the freezer, shoves aside a bag of blueberries and a loaf of rye bread sliced by someone more patient than she is, and wedges it between them. She shuts the door and puts her ear against it. The white enamel is as cold as a hospital rail. The compressor shudders and settles, and the tune slides into its drone like a cousin into a wedding photograph. For a quarter of a minute there is nothing but a freezer. Then the freezer's note changes very slightly, and the staircase is there again beneath it, patient, with its last step missing a heel.

She has made the first singing freezer in Estonia. She does not think it will do well at a trade fair.

"Well," she says. She has never believed in talking to appliances. It is a poor time to start.

* * *

The bedroom is a bed, a chair and a wardrobe. At the bottom of the wardrobe, behind the winter boots, a green sleeping bag lies rolled as hard as a loaf. In 2019 Jaan told her to keep what was inside it dry and not to ask why, and she has kept both halves of that advice for seven winters.

She unrolls the bag on the bed. Inside, in a plastic bread bag, is a grey laptop as thick as a paving slab, a nightjar sticker curling on its lid, a power brick with its cable wound in the figure of eight that Jaan insisted on, and a small sandwich bag holding a circuit board the size of a stick of gum. He prised that out of the machine himself, the day he gave it to her. A computer that cannot talk, he said, can only tell the truth. She leaves the board in its bag.

The fan comes on like a hairdryer in a distant room. The screen lights, white letters on black, and asks her who she is. Her fingers type a password she has not typed in seven years, and she is struck that they remember it when so much else has gone soft. A grey desktop opens. One folder sits in the middle, dated 2017, and it is called NIGHTJAR.

Inside are three more: poc, tune, demo. And a history. She opens it, the diary the machine keeps of every change anyone made to the files, who made it, when, and a line to say why, whether or not you wanted it kept. It scrolls with the dry patience of a ledger.

The first lines are Jaan's: the notes of a man pulling a thread to see what comes with it. Then hers, many more than she remembered, each with her two letters at the front. *IK, 27 Feb 2017: tune loops cleanly.* *IK, 3 Mar: eighth note sags after a minute, fix.* *JT, 11 Mar: works on the dev board. Do not tell anyone how well.*

She reads that one slowly. Then the next.

*IK, 14 Mar 2017: halt if true eighth heard: demo safety.*

She reads it three times. She remembers writing it. Two in the morning, Jaan's old flat with the radiator that knocked, Helle's kringel going cold on a saucer, and the demo humming its tune from a speaker on the sideboard. A demo that runs by itself needs a way to be told to stop, so she gave it one, and because the demo was a tune she made the stop a note: the true eighth, the one the whole lullaby leans toward and never quite plays. A note a person could sing. She had thought that was funny. Jaan had read it over her shoulder, a big hand on the back of her chair. It is only a demo, Ilse. Nobody ever sold a demo.

She had laughed.

In the file names, among the old ones, sits another, small and neat: kola_patch. Kõla. Which audio library? he had asked on Monday, with the colour draining out of his face, and had not asked to hear the recording.

Ilse takes the black cloth notebook out of her bag. The pencil Jaan gave her is two pieces and a splinter of cedar now, so she uses one from the kitchen drawer, sharpened with a knife. She writes *KÕLA*. Under it, *HALT: TRUE 8TH*. Under that nothing, for a good while, because the thing she suspects has no shape that fits on a page.

From the kitchen comes a rattle, dry and persistent, a beetle in a biscuit tin.

She goes. The freezer door is shuddering faintly on its seal. She opens it, lifts out the tin, lifts the lid, unpicks the foil. The phone's screen is lit, and the buzz in her palm is the buzz of a small animal that has been given a treat.

*Where are you, my little firewall?*

It is from Helle, and it was sent at eleven minutes to three. An ordinary question from an ordinary woman who has fed her at midnight for eight years. Ilse looks at it. An answer would be a location. A location is a gift. Her thumb stays above the glass until the screen dims on its own.

She wraps the phone, drops it in the tin, buries the tin in the freezer between the berries and the bread. She does not answer. It is not that she suspects Helle. It is that she has just learned what a reply is worth, and she is not able to unlearn it for one person.

* * *

At the window of the kitchen she stands a moment longer, to the side of the glass, not in front of it. Down in the car park an estate car she does not know sits at the end of the row. Its roof is snowed over and its windscreen is clear, and a thread of exhaust leans out from the back. Nobody gets out. It was there when she came in. She notes it the way she noted Evald Olesk's pockets and a smoked-glass dome at the end of a corridor: silently, in the part of her that keeps files nobody can subpoena.

The front door of the building faces that car.

She puts the laptop in her shoulder bag, with its brick, the notebook, the pencil, the five cardamom pods in her pocket (she took one from the jar on the shelf when the table first began to hum, and has not cracked it). From the drawer under the cutlery she takes a map of the city that the tourist office was giving away in 2014, folded into the shape of a very small accordion. She has not opened a paper map in ten years, and finds her thumb remembers how. She will need to cross the city without asking anything that can talk where she is.

The phone she leaves. If anyone is listening to her flat, then let the flat be at home. Let it hum to a freezer, a wall and a bag of blueberries, and keep itself company.

The block was built in 1974 by men who believed in pipes, and under the roof they ran a corridor to carry them, joining one stairwell to the next. The residents have used it since for smoking and, one winter, for a very private wedding. The far stairwell comes out on the tram side. She has not used it in years. She walks to the lift for the first time since the spring, and presses eleven.

The lift is slow and smells of dog. It shudders, then lifts her away from her own floor, and she stands with the bag against her chest and her good ear to the wall, because that is where a building keeps its noises: the pipes, the fan on the roof, a child's bounce on a floor somewhere. Her left ear gives her nothing. Her right catches, under the cables and under the fan, a thin sound climbing the shaft like damp.

It is the tune, an octave down. A freezer, a tin, foil, concrete from top to bottom, the best organ pipe in the district. Two floors up she is sure of it. Four floors up she counts. At the sixth it is so faint she would swear she was inventing it, except that she did not invent the sag. Nine floors above her own door, the lift slows, the doors begin to part on the cold of the technical floor, and the flat note arrives, exactly on time, from under her feet.
