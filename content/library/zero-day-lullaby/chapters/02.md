# Pebbles on a Shelf

At Pikk Street the floorboards still remember the spice trade. When the radiators come on at seven, the third floor smells of cinnamon and clove and something older and sweeter that Jaan says is nutmeg and Helle says is mice. By nine on Monday it smells of warm almonds as well, because Helle has laid a tray of kringel along the radiator under the window to take the chill off them, and of hot solder, because Ilse has slept one hour since the taxi.

"Three things," says Taavi, holding up three fingers in a mustard-coloured glove. "One. It is signed. Two. It is signed correctly. Three. It is signed correctly by a key that, according to Pesa's own documentation, does not exist."

"Say the third one slower."

"I can't say it slower, it just gets worse." He is twenty-seven and has worn gloves indoors since the day he was hired; the office has radiators, and the gloves have never been explained. He types in them with the accuracy of a man defusing a bomb. "Vendors who sign nothing, fine, that's a Tuesday. But these people signed the whole image properly and then signed a bit that isn't supposed to be there, and the signature checks out, and I just—" He turns to the screen. "I'm not mad at you. I'm mad at the situation."

"Gloves off at the table, my pet," says Helle from the radiator, without looking round. "You're dropping wool in the kringel." Helle Kuusk is fifty-four and keeps her orange reading glasses on a beaded chain and her opinions in the same place, in plain sight and swinging. She has run the office for eleven years and the firm's tempers for longer. "Ilse, my little firewall, have you eaten? You haven't eaten. Take two. They're warm. Don't look at me like that, you're thinner than a ruler."

Ilse takes one. It is soft and sweet with almond, a braided ring of yeast dough with raisins rolled into it, and she has had nothing since a fistful of dry cereal at four. "Thank you," she says, and goes back to the bench.

On the bench, under the lamp, the Pesa Mini lies on its back with its belly open. It left Harju in a freezer bag from the milk kitchen, signed over by Pille Rebane on the back of the duty rota, and the look on Evald Olesk's face as it went would have turned the milk. Inside, it is less than anyone expects. A speaker cone no wider than a biscuit. A ring of seven microphones round the rim, each the size of a sesame seed, which is how it hears you across a kitchen. A green board with a few black chips, and one chip the size of a lentil with eight legs: the one that holds everything the speaker knows. Taavi has a tiny crocodile clip fastened on its legs, and a wire from the clip to his laptop.

Pesa makes them in Tartu and sells them by the hundred thousand, white and smooth as river stones, because people will put a pebble on a shelf in a nursery or a boardroom and never ask what it is listening to. Ilse has never owned one. She has always found them rude, like a guest who sits down without taking off his coat.

The firmware is a long loaf, one grain all the way through. He has laid it beside the copy Pesa publishes, and the comparison runs down the screen: same, same, same, same, and then a slice, forty kilobytes wide, of a different crumb.

"Forty kilobytes is about a short story," Taavi says. "Sitting in a part whose whole life is turning a file called rain into the sound of rain."

"What's in it?"

"It isn't on the contents page. Nothing declares it. It sits between the audio code and the network code like a lodger nobody put on the lease." He tugs the tip of one glove finger. "And the chip's just a chip, I checked under the scope. No extra parts. No wires. Whoever did this never had to open the box. They sent it in through the front door, signed."

On the stairs a step approaches, one long and one short, the left leg waiting for the right. Jaan Tamm's hip announces the founder before the founder arrives.

He is sixty-seven and built like a door. His hands are enormous, a pair of shovels in cuffs; his vest is the green of old billiard cloth; and he has done it up wrong again, the second button in the first hole, so that the whole garment slopes to the left. Nobody has told him in six years. Helle says it is his way of letting people feel useful. Ilse suspects he leaves something obvious to notice, in case anyone is tempted to look for something else.

"Good morning, children," he says to a room with nobody in it under twenty-seven. "Helle, the radiators are singing again."

"They sing because you won't let me call the man."

"A radiator that sings is a radiator that cares." He hangs his hat on the stand and takes three more strides before he reads the strip of masking tape on the freezer bag, three words in Ilse's capitals. PESA MINI. HARJU. BAY 4.

He stops like a man who has walked into the glass door of a shop he has used for years. The colour goes out of his face from the top down until it is the colour of porridge.

Nobody else sees. Taavi is looking at his screen and Helle at the kringel. Ilse is looking at Jaan, which, in the sense this firm uses the word, is her job.

"Which audio library?" he says.

It is the only thing he asks. Not what happened, not who is hurt, not where.

"Which?" says Ilse.

"Which audio library does it link? They have two."

"The Kõla fork," Taavi says, still facing the screen. "The one they bought off the Tartu spin-out. Why?"

"Ah," says Jaan. His eyes go, very briefly, to the top shelf above the filing cabinets, where a grey archive box has NIGHTJAR printed on its end in his square black capitals, the one box up there with a clean lid, and come back. "Ah. I was only wondering."

He lowers himself into the nearest chair, left hip first, takes a kringel from the tray and does not eat it.

Taavi puts the recording on the wall screen: sixteen minutes of the ward, the ribbon from Ilse's phone made big. At that size the notes are lines, eight lines, a staircase repeated until it hurts. Ilse asks him to zoom. At ten times, the lines have a grain.

"Is that noise?" Taavi says.

"Noise is untidy." She puts a fingertip to the glass. Each note, along its length, moves in tiny steps: up a notch, hold, down a notch, hold, in a rhythm that does not breathe. "A cent is what you get when you take the gap between two neighbouring piano keys and slice it into a hundred. Most people can't hear a difference of five. These are two, three. Always two or three. Always up or down."

"Nobody's piano is that drunk or that sober."

"No piano did this. Something with a clock did." She slides to the big sag, the one that comes once every ninety-four seconds, thirty cents or so, ten times the size of the little steps, and taps it. "That's the full stop. This is the text."

"Text of what?"

"I don't know yet."

Behind her Jaan, who has been looking at the screen without appearing to, says nothing. He does not ask to hear it. In all the years she has known him Ilse has never seen him not ask to hear a sound.

The clock on the wall says ten to eleven when he stands. "It is lunch."

"It's ten fifty," Taavi says.

"Then it is an early lunch. Helle, take the boy to the place with the soup. I'm paying." He lays two twenties on the bench. Helle looks at the money, then at him, and her glasses slide down her nose. In eleven years he has never paid for soup.

"Jaan, are you ill?"

"I'm hungry for other people's lunch."

"What about the blob?" Taavi says. "We can't just leave a—"

"We'll burn that bridge when we come to it."

"Cross," says Helle. "Cross that bridge, Jaan."

"Both," he says, and holds the door.

They go, Taavi's boots and Helle's heels clattering down the stairwell, and Jaan stands at the head of the stairs with his hand on the rail until the street door slams far below.

"Don't put this in the tickets," he says, without turning round.

"Helle reads every ticket."

"I know she does." He comes back past the benches, slowly, favouring the hip. "A ticket is a letter everybody can read, Ilse. Not this one. Not yet."

She follows him to the glass corner that is his office. The desk is a battlefield tidied by a general: blotter, lamp, a dish with his keys in it. A brass tag stamped with the three lions of Tallinn. The stair key. Between them, a plain grey stick with no label. Tamm Incident Response is Pesa's retained security contractor, which is why the stick lives on his ring: an emergency signing token, good for one signature on one firmware release, in case Pesa's own signing farm ever burns down. He has carried it six years, like a spare tooth. He has never used it. When he thinks he spins the whole ring round one huge finger, a habit that has driven Helle to threaten the stair key with a file, and today it lies in its dish without a tremor.

Beside the dish the music box lies open on the blotter, a walnut box the size of a loaf of bread with a steel comb and a brass cylinder studded with pins in a loose spiral. She counts them, because she has always counted them. Seven. Where the eighth would sit, at the end of the last turn, there is a small bright bare spot.

She takes the card from her pocket and lays it on the blotter, crossed sevens up.

"You were at Harju on Thursday."

He looks at the card for some time. "I give those out to everyone. It's how I get customers."

"It was a neonatal ward."

"Babies are clients. Nobody thinks of that." The smile arrives on his mouth and goes no further. "Sit, Ilse."

She sits. From her bag she takes the notebook she has not opened in years, black cloth, a red thread for a marker, the paper soft as a handkerchief. His old rule: if it cannot go in a ticket, it goes on paper. From the stairwell Helle's laugh comes up all at once, huge, bouncing off the old brick, at something Taavi has said to a man in the street, and fades. Ilse looks for a pen. She left her pens in the taxi.

Jaan takes a pencil from his vest pocket, a carpenter's stub sharpened with a knife, and puts it in her palm and closes her fingers round it with his enormous hand.

"Paper keeps its mouth shut," he says.
