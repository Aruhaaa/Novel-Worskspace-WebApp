# The Voicemail

The tube light in Interview Room Three flickers at a rate Ilse's eye cannot ignore: nine pulses in the time it takes Inspector Lepik to unwrap a mint. Under it the room goes bright, dim, bright, and everything on the table, the cardboard folder, the paper cup, her own two hands, is briefly a photograph of itself.

"Mint?"

"No."

"Nobody takes the mint. Twenty years I have been offering." He puts it in his cheek and settles back, and the chair says something unflattering. It is Thursday, ten past ten. She slept two hours in her coat in a cash hotel on Lai Street where the lock was a postcard, and she can feel every minute of the other twenty-two behind her eyes. "Once more, from the beginning. You went home."

"Ten to two."

"Ten to two. And left again at?"

"Twenty past three."

"Twenty past three. The tram to the centre. A waitress on Vene Street says you came in at ten to five, asked for the corner, and sat there for three hours behind one coffee. Tall. White streak, here." He touches his own temple, which is bald. "She was worried about you. She wanted to know if you were all right."

"I wasn't."

"The coffee?"

"No."

"Ah." He turns a page. "At twenty-five to eight you paid. Pikk Street is four minutes from that café. The emergency call is logged at seven fifty-one."

"I stopped at the corner."

"To do what?"

"Look at his window. The lamp was lit."

"The lamp." Lepik rolls the mint across his teeth. "Our pathologist is a cautious woman, and she will not be pinned, but she likes the time between half past seven and a quarter to eight. So when you stood on the corner admiring that lamp, Miss Kantor, the man who lit it was most probably already sitting on his own landing."

The tube goes dark and comes back. Ilse looks at the folder. For fourteen hours she has been very careful not to do that sum, and he has done it for her, kindly, in the voice you use to tell someone their bus has gone.

"Your telephone," he says. "We have not got it."

"I left it at home."

"All afternoon, all evening. The network says it has been in your flat since two o'clock, four kilometres from Pikk Street, with a battery that scarcely falls. It hasn't moved. It hasn't been used. It is very well behaved."

"Yes."

"A woman goes to meet her employer on the night he dies, and leaves her telephone behind."

"I put it in the freezer."

Lepik looks at her for the length of one flicker. "The freezer."

"With the blueberries. In a tin."

"A tin."

"There's a robin on the lid."

He writes it down. She watches the pencil. It writes *robin*. "Miss Kantor. People who want an alibi leave their telephones at home. People who want to be found take them. Which are you?"

"I don't want an alibi."

"Nobody ever does. Why the freezer?"

"It was behaving oddly."

"Phones do. Mine has lately taken a view on my diary." He sighs, and the chair sighs with him. "That is not an answer."

"It's the one I have."

He lets it lie. Then he reaches under the table and sets the laptop on it between them, and lays two fingers on the lid as if taking its temperature. "Older than my daughter."

"It's from 2017."

"Whose?"

"Mine. The firm's. Jaan's, in the end."

"And he asked you to bring it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"He didn't say."

"Didn't." The word goes round his mouth with the mint. "What is on it?"

"Nothing I have read since 2019."

"Since." He taps the lid. "Is it joined to anything? The internet, the firm?"

"It can't be. He took the card out himself. It's in a bag at my flat."

"Can't." He turns the laptop on its back and copies the serial number on the underside into his notebook, one digit at a time, upside down. Then he turns it over again and slides it to her side of the table. "I have no ground for keeping it, which I regret, and no wish to, which I do not. Yet."

He opens the folder. "Tamm Incident Response. Eleven employees at its peak, four today. It owes the tax board forty-one thousand euros and the owners of the spice warehouse seventeen, and the lease is up at the end of the month. Did you know?"

"No."

"Mrs Kuusk keeps the books."

"Yes."

"She will tell me it is cash flow." He pronounces the term as if it were a minor saint. "Everything is cash flow, until it is a funeral."

Behind him stands a grey filing cabinet with a label on its bottom drawer in thick black marker: EVIDENCE. Lepik leans back with a groan, hooks the drawer open with one finger, and comes up with a sleeve of ginger biscuits. "These rooms run on them," he says, and holds one out to her.

"No."

"Nobody takes the biscuit either."

The door opens without a knock, and Helle Kuusk comes in the way weather comes in, all at once, with a smell of butter. Orange glasses on their beaded chain, a good grey coat buttoned to the chin, a paper bag in each hand. Behind her follows a lawyer so young his suit still has the creases from its box, and behind the lawyer, in mustard gloves and with his shoulders somewhere near his ears, Taavi.

"My little firewall!"

The bags go down on the table. Ilse is embraced, the whole length of her, with the cold of the street still in the wool. "I rang and rang! They told me at a desk, a desk, as if I were a person who goes to desks! Oh, my darling, you're thin. And Jaan. Oh, Jaan." Her face breaks like a plate and goes on weeping, loudly and honestly, a rain that has no intention of stopping for a lawyer. The lawyer produces a handkerchief too late. Helle wipes her glasses on her own, replaces them, and says, "I have brought kringel."

"Mrs Kuusk," says Lepik, "this is an interview."

"Then I shall be quiet in it." She sits. The lawyer says his name is Saar, that he acts for the firm, and that Miss Kantor is welcome to his services if she wishes. Ilse says she will manage. "She'll manage," Helle tells the table. "She always does. Inspector, have a kringel."

Lepik, helplessly, holds out the biscuits in trade. Helle takes one before anyone can intervene, and eats it.

"Too much ginger."

"That," says Lepik, "is from the evidence drawer."

"Is it?" She chews. "Then it's evidence of too much ginger."

The lawyer closes his eyes. Ilse does not look at Helle. She is looking at Taavi, who has not sat down, and who is not watching Helle's face. He is watching her hands. Helle's hands, which in eight years Ilse has never seen at rest, which flutter round a kringel like a conductor's, are lying on the table perfectly flat. Taavi watches them the way he watches a progress bar.

Ilse files it, and says nothing.

"Since I have you all," says Lepik, mildly, "the doors. The street door on Pikk Street and the door of the office show no mark of force. No scratch, no jemmy, no drill. Whoever came in had a key, or was let in by someone who did."

"Jaan let everybody in," Helle says. "The postman. The girl who sells honey. He was a trusting man."

"So I have been hearing. How many sets of keys to the premises?"

"Four. Jaan, me, Ilse, Taavi."

"Four." He lets that settle. "Mr Tamm's was clipped to his belt."

"The clip was empty," Ilse says.

"Yes," says Lepik softly. "I saw." He puts out a broad palm. "Humour me."

Helle's comes out of her handbag first, a ring with an orange fob and seven keys, clinking. Ilse lays hers down beside it. Taavi has to pat three pockets before he finds his and sets it on the table as carefully as a thing that might go off. Three rings, a flat grey light, and a fourth that is nowhere on the table.

"Thank you," says Lepik. "Don't leave the city, Miss Kantor."

"Where would I go?"

"People surprise me. It is the only pleasure in the job."

* * *

At the foot of the stairs on Pikk Street the constable on duty lifts the tape for them like a man holding a door at a wedding. On the third landing a rectangle of blue tape has been laid on the lino where Jaan sat, as though the wall were being papered. Ilse steps over it.

The police have taken Jaan's computer and the firm's servers, and the office feels it, a house after a burglary has taken only the televisions. Taavi walks to his desk and sits. Helle fills the kettle in the kitchenette, because what else is there, and Ilse goes into the weighing room for the quiet, with the great brass scale and its lower pan and the whiteboard where the dots are still drawn, TALLINN, SINGAPORE, LAGOS.

On the shelf under the board sits a grey plastic loaf with a cassette window and a red light blinking: 1.

It has been blinking since yesterday afternoon, in a glass room that two policemen walked through and took for a dead appliance. It is not a computer. It is the firm's old answering machine, on the firm's old number, which a few clients who distrust the modern world still ring; Jaan would not let anyone throw it out. A message left on it goes nowhere but a tape. It has no ears of its own and tells nobody.

She presses play.

The tape hisses. A woman's voice, recorded in another century, announces, "Wednesday. Sixteen. Twelve." A click. A cough, and a long breath out through a big nose, and a chair taking weight. Then Jaan.

"Ilse. It is Jaan. I am speaking to a machine that cannot repeat what I say to anyone, which is a quality I have come to admire in my old age." A pause; the radiator ticks behind him. "It is a little after four. If you are hearing this you have thought of the old telephone before the new one, and I was right about you, which is a comfort. Come tonight. Do not hurry. The stairs are patient." Another pause, shorter. "And Ilse. The music box. Count the pins again."

The tape hisses on to its end. The machine clicks, and falls silent.

She stands very still. On Monday she counted seven pins on his blotter, in a spiral, and a bare bright spot where the eighth would sit. Count the pins again. As if the number might have changed in her absence.

She goes out into the open office to tell Taavi. His chair is empty and turning slowly. She lays her hand on the seat: warm. His screen still glows, a progress bar at ninety per cent. His mustard gloves are not on the desk. His phone is, in a biscuit tin with the lid on, where it can think about its behaviour.

Helle comes in with a tray, three cups and a plate. "Where's my pet?"

"Gone."

"Gone where?"

"I don't know."

"He does that when he's frightened." Helle sets down the tray with great care. "He goes and eats something." Her glasses hang on their chain and do not swing. She is looking, Ilse sees, at Taavi's chair, and not at anything else in the room.

Ilse goes back into the weighing room and shuts the glass door. Four rings, three of them on a table that morning. The fourth is in the pocket of the man who unscrewed the bulbs. The doors showed no mark, and doors are not opened by being wished at. A key walks through them as easily as a draught. Someone with the firm's key had stood on that landing, or had been let in by someone who had it. And one of the other three was in a young man's pocket, and the young man has left his phone in a tin and walked out in the middle of a dead man's voice.

She plays the message again. The first time for the words. She plays it a third time with her eyes shut, for the man: the cough, the chair, the patience, a voice with no hurry in it because the hurry was already over. The tape runs out. The machine clicks.

She presses eject and slides the cassette into the pocket of her coat, with the pods. Then she takes one of them out, a small green husk with a seam down its side, and sets it between her back teeth, and cracks it.
