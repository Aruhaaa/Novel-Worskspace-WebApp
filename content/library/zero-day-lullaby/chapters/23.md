# The Commits

The phone in the freezer is still humming.

A man in a paper suit has opened the door and is holding the tin at arm's length, the way he might hold a bird that has flown into a window. It is Helle's baking tin with the robin on the lid, and the hum comes out of it in a thin, patient line, an octave under the tune, a refrigerator trying to remember a song. He looks at his colleague. His colleague, who wears blue plastic covers over her shoes so that she will leave nothing of herself in Ilse's kitchen, which strikes Ilse as a little rich, writes something on a form. The tin goes into a bag the size of a pillowcase. The robin looks out through the plastic.

Ilse sits at her own kitchen table, in her own coat, and watches it go. She came for the wireless card. She told Taavi so at the kerb, in a whisper, and Taavi said it was the worst reason in the world, which it was. The card lives in a bag in the toe of a winter boot at the back of her wardrobe, and the Nightjar laptop is a paperweight without it, and whatever she is going to do with Jaan's token she cannot do on a machine that has never heard of a network. "Five minutes," she said. Then the lift opened on the second floor, and on her doormat a man in a paper suit was stooping to put on his overshoes.

Now a technician draws a small bag from the boot. In it is a card the size of a stamp. Ilse watches her reason for being here go into evidence.

"Ms Kantor." The woman across the table has not stood. She is fifty or so, in a long navy coat she has not taken off, with grey hair cut by someone who respects it and an umbrella, furled, laid across her knees like a rifle. "Kaie Lõhmus, Prosecutor's Office. I have a warrant with your name in it, spelled correctly. We have been here since half past four. Your freezer has a very loud telephone."

"It's a long story."

"I have all evening," says Lõhmus, in the voice of someone who does not. "Unlawful access to a computer system. Preparation of an attack upon one. I am obliged to read you the article, so I shall read you the article."

She reads it. It takes a minute. Around them the paper suits go about the flat with the grave speed of people laying a table for a dinner no one will eat: two laptops out of the wardrobe, a third from under the bed, a stack of drives, the router, a man on a chair unscrewing the smoke alarm. Somebody has opened the baking drawer and is looking into it with great suspicion.

"Pockets, please," says Lõhmus. "The coat."

Ilse puts the notebook on the oilcloth. Black cloth, a red thread marking the place. Beside it, from the other pocket, goes the twist of foil. The technician lifts it to the light with a gloved finger and thumb.

"Item fourteen. Three seeds, or pods. In foil."

"Cardamom," says Ilse.

"Why in foil?" asks Lõhmus.

"I was told phones can hear me crack them."

The technician writes it down. Ilse watches the pen go. *Reason given for foil: to prevent audio detection.* It sounds guilty. It is true, which is the same thing. Lõhmus's mouth does something that on a less tired face would have been a smile, and then is not.

She slides a tablet across the oilcloth. On it is the plain grey page of a leak site called Daylight, which went up at twenty to six and has been copied to forty mirrors since. A folder tree. A file called kola_patch. A list of commits in small clean type, and on every line her own name, and the year she was twenty-eight.

> kola_patch, 14 March 2017. IK: halt if true eighth heard: demo safety. JT: Do not tell anyone how well.

Ilse reads it twice, though she could recite it. She has seen those two lines in exactly one other place in the world, on a grey laptop with a nightjar sticker, in a rucksack that is at this moment on a young man's back across the road. So the leak has not come from her. It has come from the copy that somebody bought.

"You wrote that," says Lõhmus. "Demo safety. A demo of what, Ms Kantor? Something that listens to babies?"

"A demo of a bug I wanted fixed."

"There is more." Lõhmus turns the tablet. "At eighteen hundred, the Secretariat of the Standing Committee on Lawful Access issued a statement from Helsinki to six news agencies."

> The Committee has identified Ilse Kantor of Tallinn as the principal author of the software known as the Lullaby, and Jaan Tamm, deceased, as her associate. The Committee is cooperating fully with the Estonian authorities and will make no further comment.

Associate. Jaan would have hated the word. He would have tried it aloud, like a man pressing a bruise, and then said something about a stitch in time that meant the opposite.

"I tell you the rest so that you understand the shape of it, not to be unkind," says Lõhmus. "Mr Abara's company accounts were frozen in Lagos at four this afternoon, at the request of a body in Helsinki. Mrs Rebane's director has filed a complaint, and Mrs Rebane is to present herself at Kesklinn at nine tomorrow. It has been a busy day for your acquaintance."

"And then?"

"And then, Ms Kantor, you come with me." Lõhmus stands, and with a certain reluctance puts the umbrella under her arm. "You are detained on suspicion. You may have a lawyer. I would suggest the lawyer."

They take her down by the stairs, a paper suit on either side, because the lift is full of evidence bags. A warm wind has brought rain, the sort that falls as snow and lands as water, and in the courtyard the lamps stand in wet halos. Across the road, at the bus stop, with its side door open and its lights off, is the white van. Beside it stands Taavi.

He has done the one thing she asked him not to. He has taken out a phone, a cheap new one in its jacket of foil, and he is holding it up in both mustard gloves with the blue light on his chin, filming her walk to the car like a boy at a concert. His mouth is open. On his back is the rucksack with the grey laptop in it, and a music box in a bread bag, and a napkin with a stick of plastic inside, and that is the only thing on the entire street that matters.

At the far end of the block, under the last lamp, with its engine ticking and its lights out, waits a black Audi. She knows the plate by now: the blue strip, the two letters of a ministry.

Ilse shakes her head. It is a tiny movement. Taavi does not see.

A man steps out of the Audi's shadow. Toast-coloured leather, grey hair, a hat in his hand. He crosses the road in a dozen unhurried steps, and none of the paper suits looks up, because a raid makes the street invisible. He comes at Taavi from the left and takes the phone out of his gloves the way one takes a toy from a child in a restaurant: gently, and with no possibility of argument. He lays it on the wet pavement. He considers it. Then, with all the ceremony of a man laying a table, he puts one boot on it.

She sees the tread in the lamplight. A chevron. Size forty-four. She does not hear the glass go. She sees the blue light on Taavi's chin go out.

"Sorry," says Sarapuu. She reads it off his mouth. He says it to the phone, or to Taavi, or to her.

Taavi runs. He does not look at the man, or at her. He runs the way an animal runs when the part of it that thinks has been switched off, straight for the van, the rucksack bumping, and Ilse, without any decision at all, says "Go" aloud, quietly, as one tells a dog to sit. "Go, Taavi."

"Who are you talking to?" asks Lõhmus.

"A friend who has just lost his phone."

The van door slams. The van, which has no engine to start, begins to move, and slides away from the kerb into the rain with its lights out. The man in the leather jacket does not follow it. He bends, picks the SIM card from among the pieces, puts it in his pocket, and walks back to the Audi, which lights its lamps as he gets in, and goes.

Lõhmus has turned to watch it. "Who was that?"

"A locksmith."

"Should I be concerned?"

"Yes," says Ilse.

Lõhmus looks at the tail-lights going away down Peterburi tee, and Ilse sees her read the plate, and sees something in the tired exact face rearrange itself and then settle, carefully, back into place. In this country there are only so many ministries. The prosecutor says nothing at all. She opens the door of a grey Škoda and says, "Priit will drive you. He does not talk," and shuts it.

Priit does not talk. He is a broad young man in a rain jacket with the hood down, and he drives with both hands on the wheel at exactly the limit, and the radio is off. The rain comes harder on the way to Kesklinn, and the wipers go across the windscreen in their slow arcs, left, right, left. Ilse watches them. Peterburi tee goes by in orange smears, then Ülemiste, then the ring road, then the dark glass of the offices. She has no phone, no notebook, no pods. Her hands have nothing to do. They are, she finds, in her lap, counting.

A double stroke of the wipers takes a second and a half. Sixty-three of them make ninety-four seconds. She counts sixty-three and starts again. Behind her breastbone another count keeps time with it, the one she has carried since the rail trembled against her palm. In the front seat Priit drives. The rain comes. Nobody else in the car is counting. Perhaps nobody else in the country knows what the number is for.

The custody sergeant, a woman with reading glasses on a cord, takes her belt, her bootlaces and the drawstring from her hood, and writes them in a book.

"What's that?" Ilse says, looking up at the corner of the ceiling above the desk, where a white stone sits in a wire cage.

"Building management. It does the lights and the heating. It doesn't do anything else." The sergeant caps her pen. "Don't mind it."

The cell is two metres by three: a bench with a mattress as thick as a book, a steel basin, white tile, a blanket the colour of a sticking plaster. The door closes with a sound like a drawer. Ilse sits on the bench, and looks up.

In the ceiling, in a wire cage like a lamp, is another stone. White, fist-sized, polite. A ring of light runs round its base.

As she watches, the ring turns from white to blue.
