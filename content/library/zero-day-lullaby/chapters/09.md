# The Drop Cabin

Marit Soini is turning the heel of the second sock when the man comes out of the warehouse door.

She has been in the hired car since four. It sits thirty metres up Pikk Street with its nose to the kerb and its engine off for the cold, and a flask of tea between her thighs. The car smells of the last driver's dog. Snow comes down through the lamplight one flake at a time, each taking its time, as if it were being paid by the hour. Over the afternoon she has watched the door the way she once watched a grey sea for a smaller grey thing in it: a woman in orange spectacles carrying a tin under her arm, going home at six; a thin young man in mustard gloves, walking like someone late for something he dreads; and at five past seven a man in a leather jacket with a flat canvas bag, who rang the bell, waited, and was let in.

She did not think about him. Locksmiths ring bells. The firm on the third floor has a great deal of locking to do.

Thirty-six minutes later he comes out and draws the door to behind him, and does not lock it. A locksmith who does not lock. That is the first thing. The second is the bag. It hung from his hand when he went in as a flat bag hangs. It swings now like something is in it, and he carries it a little out from his leg, the way you carry a pail that is full to the brim.

Marit has understood the weight before she understands the rest, as you read a sea from how a boat sits in it. She puts the needles down. Her hands stay where they are on the wheel. She had been told to expect a search. At the very worst she had expected paper. She had not expected thirty-six minutes, and she cannot get them back.

He walks up Pikk Street without looking round. It is the walk of a man who has never been followed, because it has never occurred to anyone to try.

* * *

She follows on foot. A woman of fifty-two in a waxed coat, with a canvas knitting bag over her arm, is the best disguise in the north. She keeps a house and a half behind him, and she does not need to see him, because the snow does the work: the print of a boot in the new fall, a stack of chevrons, V upon V, climbing the sole and pointing the way he goes, like the track of a gull. Under the Great Coastal Gate, past the fat tower, out along the dark side of the old wall, and into Kalamaja.

It is a district of wooden houses, tall and thin and painted the colours of old sweets, standing in lanes that are not wide enough for a conversation. Windows are lit. Somewhere a person is frying onions. The man walks into a lane that ends in a snowbank and a white van, tight against the bank as if tucked in, and Marit stops at the corner and becomes a woman who is looking for a dropped glove.

The van has lettering on its flank. SARAPUU & POEG, LUKSEPAD, 24 H. And a mobile number, and under it, the plate: 913 KPL.

Sarapuu and son. Locksmiths, all hours. She says the name once in her head. A son, then, somewhere. She has never in her life had cause to find that sad until this moment, with the lane quiet and the onions frying and the lamp on the corner making a small yellow room out of the snow.

The man gets into the cab and turns on the dome light. She sees him now, as well as she'll ever see him: neat grey hair, a soft jacket, black wool gloves with the fingertips cut off. He takes a rag from the door pocket and cleans the gloves, finger by finger, as carefully as if he were polishing a pair of spectacles. Then he cleans the steering wheel. Then he lays the bag on the passenger seat, and, to her astonishment, passes the belt across it and clicks it home.

The van starts. The headlamps wake the snowbank. It goes out of the lane without hurry and turns left and is gone, and she is standing at the corner with a glove in her hand that she never dropped, and the number in her head like a pebble in a boot.

Behind her, back up the hill, a siren winds up and stops. Then another. Then a third, nearer.

Marit does not look round. She knows what they have found. The car is on Pikk Street, a hundred metres from three police cars, hired in a name that is not hers. She will fetch it tomorrow, or never.

* * *

The harbour at night is orange: sodium light on ice, on black water, on the white flank of the Nightboat, which lies at the quay with its funnel painted with a blue wave and its gangway down, taking in the first of the evening's lorry drivers and pensioners and lovers. A tugboat sounds its horn, two notes, a fall and a rise, like an old man clearing his throat to begin a story. The cold is so clean it has an edge.

"Seven-one-one-eight," says Kerli, behind the desk, with the sorrow of a woman for whom a cabin has gone unslept. Her hair is hennaed the colour of a fox. "Again no sailing?"

"No."

"Seven nights paid for, two gone, and not a hair on the pillow. That is the most loved cabin in the fleet, and the mattress is beginning to feel unwanted."

"The telephone," Marit says. "Is it free?"

"For you, two euros a minute, and I won't listen." Kerli slides a grey handset across the counter, coiled cord, a laminated card taped beside it of dialling codes from before the euro. "I will, of course. It's a quiet night."

Marit dials a number she learned by heart because she was told never to write it down. It rings in a hall somewhere across the water. A porter answers in Finnish, and she says a name, and she waits.

"Yes," says Ülo Hint.

"It is Soini."

"Mrs Soini. A ship's telephone. How enterprising of you."

"A man is dead on Pikk Street. The older one."

The line hums. Behind it she hears a clock, and the wood of a room with high ceilings. "I see," says Hint.

"Not mine."

"No. I should hope not."

"Yours?"

"The Committee engages you for quiet, Mrs Soini. I have never in my life found a corpse quiet." The voice is as gentle and exact as a surgeon's. "I am sorry to hear of Mr Tamm. I am sorry, in a general way, for any man."

"Then who?"

"That is a question for a different party."

"There is a different party."

"I did not say so."

"You did," says Marit, and waits.

Hint is quiet so long that she can hear Kerli, at the other end of the counter, turn a page. "I will tell you what I can," he says finally. "The woman, Kantor. She lives until the decoder is in the Committee's hand. She is to be watched, and kept, and her habits noted. If she is harmed, you will have harmed the only door that opens. And no more telephones. They are loquacious."

The line clicks, and goes.

Marit lays the handset down on its cradle like a thing that might wake. "Four minutes," says Kerli. "Eight euros. There is a sandwich, if you like, left from the lunch."

"No."

"Sad, with the herring."

"Another time," says Marit, and goes out on deck.

* * *

She does not know what she wants from the cold. The wind off the gulf is steady, and the ice at the edges of the basin is a grey plate that cracks and knits and cracks again. She leans on the rail, and for a minute she is on a black sea with a boat hook in her hands, listening for a voice.

At the foot of the gangway, under the lamp, a man is standing alone. She sees the hands first. The face is a hat brim and a cardigan collar above the lapels of an overcoat, and from her height and in that light it could belong to anyone. But the hands are lit like something laid on a plate. They take a mandarin from a pocket and begin to peel it, in a slow spiral that does not hurry and does not break. The thumbs know where they are going. The peel falls away from the fruit in a single orange ribbon, longer and longer, and he lets it hang, turning, in the lamplight, as if to see through it.

They are soft hands, with clean nails. Hands that would feed pigeons in a square in the morning, from a paper bag, and sign a paper in the afternoon with the same unhurried tenderness. She has not met a man like this. She has met his hands in other men, in a corridor, at the end of a table, on the wheel of a car that waited. They are never in a hurry because the hurry is done by other people.

He looks up. He sees her at the rail, forty feet above him, a woman in a waxed coat with a knitting bag, and he nods. He does not smile. He nods the way a man nods over a garden fence to somebody he has lived next to for years. Then he lays the whole peel on the bollard like a ribbon on a gift, and goes off down the quay toward a dark car whose driver has already got out to open the door.

Marit watches the peel until it lies still. Then she goes below.

* * *

Cabin 7118 is inside, on deck seven, by the stairs. It has two bunks, no window, a basin the size of a hat, and a vent grille above the lower bunk held with two screws that a ten-cent coin will turn. In the gap behind the grille sits a flat tobacco tin, and in the tin the half of Hint's fee that she would not carry. She does not count it. She leaves it, replaces the grille, and sits on the lower bunk with her bag on her knee.

The engines wake somewhere under the deck. It is a long way away, and then it is not, a thrum in the steel that she feels in her teeth and the bones of her feet before it reaches her ears. In an hour the ship will sail without her. She will go ashore at half past ten, as she did last night, past Kerli, who will not ask.

She takes out the sock and begins to count.

She did this once on a deck. After a night's work she would count the heads. Not the saved, the lost: the ones the sea took while her hands were full of another. You go down the list in the dark by feel, and each one has a face, and the faces do not change however many times you count.

These are not the sea's.

A customs officer in Klaipėda, who took a farm and a signature. A bank clerk in Daugavpils, who took a ticket to Montreal and a different name. A lawyer in Gdynia, a printer's widow in Tartu, a young man in Turku with a stammer who signed with the wrong hand. Seven more like them, with envelopes, and weather, and the station clock stopped at the same hour. In nine cases the paper was enough. In the tenth, a man in Narva took the paper and then the stairs. The eleventh she does not know. A woman in Gdańsk who never answered the door.

Eleven, she thinks. She keeps the strip with their names in the lining of this bag, in a pocket sewn for the purpose, and she has never once taken it out to read it.

The card from Hint said Kantor, Tamm, and a nurse. The word *close* was on the envelope, as it always is, in his pale hand. Nobody had told her to close an old man on a landing. Whoever went up those stairs tonight was working from some other list, and took no pleasure in it, and afterwards cleaned his gloves.

She sets down the needles. The cabin is cream-painted steel, and has nothing in it to look at, and the wall faces her like a patient face.

"Nine one three," says Marit, aloud. "K P L."

It is odd how a number sounds, said in a closed room. A thing in her head can be put away. A thing said aloud has a place in the room, and she is in the room too. She could write it on a slip, and put it behind the grille, and by morning Hint would have it, and by evening the Committee. A man who cleans his gloves would be easy for them to find.

She does not write it. She says it again, softly, once, so it will not be lonely. She has not decided what she means by that. She knows only that the plate now exists out loud, and that she has let a lane in Kalamaja keep its snowbank and its van, and that she is, for the moment, a woman who has let Sarapuu go.

Under the deck the engines settle to a long steady pulse. She looks down at the sock in her lap. Three rows under the needle a stitch has dropped and begun to run, a small ladder in the grey. She finds the crochet hook in her bag, and picks up the stitch, and takes it up row by row.
