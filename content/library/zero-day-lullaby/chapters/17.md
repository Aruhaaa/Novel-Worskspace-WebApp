# Mind the Step

From the third row of the car park the hospital is a ship at anchor, all its lit windows stacked in the dark, and Marit knows which ones she is watching.

The hired Skoda is a white estate with a child's seat bolted in the back and a pine-tree air freshener that has been dead for years. She has parked it between two vans, nose out, with the engine off and the heater on its last warm breath. Snow lies on the roof, a hand deep, and she has not brushed it off, because a car with snow on it has been sitting since the morning, and nobody looks twice at a car that belongs there. It is twenty to ten on Saturday night. She has been in it since eight. In her lap are four needles and the foot of a grey sock, and the thing she has not been doing is knitting.

A tram comes in at the stop on the corner with its bell going, lit like a lantern, and lets people out. A porter first, so tall that he has to fold himself through the door, in a donkey jacket and a woollen hat with a bobble. After him, round as a muffin, in a fur-trimmed parka with a bun on top and a pencil through the bun, comes the woman whose name was on Hint's paper.

Marit has not seen her before. She had a picture in her head built out of other people's words, and in the picture the nurse was frightened. The nurse is laughing. It comes across the car park in the dry cold like a bell, a big, helpless, delighted laugh, with her head thrown back and her hand on the porter's sleeve. She has to stop, and bend, and hold her knees. The porter waits with enormous patience, a man who has done this for eleven years and would do it for eleven more. They go on toward the night door together, her head coming up to his elbow, her laugh still unwinding.

The man in the doorway is not laughing.

He steps out of the dark under the bus shelter as the pair go by. He is tidy. A leather jacket the colour of toast. Grey hair, brushed. He falls in behind them a house and a half back, on the far side of the same pavement, matching his stride to the porter's long one, and from the Skoda she can see the way he walks, upright and unhurried. It is the walk of a man who has never been followed, because no one has thought to.

Then he looks at the car park, at the snow-roofed estate between the two vans, a lump among lumps. He pulls a small notebook from his breast pocket. He writes in it, with a pencil, three or four words. He is putting down her plate. He takes off a black glove to do it, because you cannot write well in fingerless wool at minus eleven, and when he has done he puts the notebook away, and the glove with it, and the glove misses the pocket.

It falls without a sound. It lies in the snow beside the shelter, black on white, a small crumpled hand.

He does not look back. The night door swings and takes the nurse, and the porter, and a moment later, the man.

Marit sits for the space of ten breaths. Then she opens the door of the Skoda and gets out, and puts on the knitted cap, and walks across the car park in the tracks that other people have made. She picks up the glove. It is warm, which she did not expect. It smells of clean machine oil, and at the thumb it has been darned, in grey, by a patient hand, with stitches so small and even that she turns it to the light to admire them.

She puts it in her pocket. In the snow beside the shelter, pointing toward the door, is a track she has seen before, in Pikk Street, and again in a lane in Kalamaja: a stack of chevrons, V upon V.

* * *

At that hour the lodge is a dog and the dog is asleep.

The man in it has his chin on his chest and a saucer of sunflower husks at his elbow, and Marit goes by the window with a mop bucket on one hip and a grey housekeeping tabard from a peg in the passage, the best disguise in the north after a knitting bag, and nobody sees her. The stairs behind the loading bay are concrete. They smell of cold, of old floor wax, of a dozen years of shoes, and on the third landing, where the stair turns to the neonatal wing, of oranges.

She stops. The smell is so strong and so out of place that for a moment she thinks it is in her head, a thing she has carried up from the quay at Tallinn. Then she sees it. On the radiator under the window, between the pipe and the wall, where the heat has been at it for hours, lies a long curl of dried mandarin peel. It is a single piece. A spiral, unbroken, brittle as paper, hard as a shaving of horn. Someone has sat here this afternoon and eaten a mandarin and laid the skin on the warm iron to dry, as another man might leave a coat.

She looks at it for some while. It is, she thinks, the quietest signature she has ever seen.

"Good evening," says the man behind her. "Mind the step."

She does not turn. She has heard the boot on the stair a half-second before the voice, a heel and then a sole, easy and unhurried. It is the sound a man makes who wishes you to hear him come, and she understands that he has known since the car park exactly whose knitted cap that was.

"You dropped something," says Marit.

"Ah." A pause, courteous. "Did I?"

She takes her time. She sets down the bucket, and turns, and brings the glove out of her pocket and holds it up on her open hand, and he looks at it with real pleasure. He is at the foot of the last flight, one hand on the rail. The other is bare. His eyes are mild, the colour of tea, with a deep tired crease at each corner.

"That is kind," he says, "that is very kind. My wife darned it." He starts up the flight. "I'd be grateful. They are so difficult to find, the good ones."

He holds out the bare hand for the glove. It is a locksmith's hand, broad, scarred across the knuckles, the nails kept short and clean. Marit reaches for the wrist.

She has done it a hundred times, on a deck, in a swell, to a man who was drowning and did not wish to be helped. She takes the wrist in her left hand and turns it, palm up, and the needle is already in her right, a steel double-pointed needle, thin as a pin, three inches long, and the point rests on the soft blue place below the base of the thumb where the pulse is. She does not press. She does not need to. He feels it, as you feel the first cold flake of snow on the back of the neck. He goes entirely still.

"Mind the step," says Marit.

For a time neither of them moves. Below them, the stair light clicks off and, in the dark, comes on again at a slow thump of his heel.

"I see," he says, softly. "Yes. I see that I've been rude." He doesn't pull. His breath goes in and out, steady, with the faint sweetness of cough sweets. "You are the lady in the lane. Wednesday night, in Kalamaja. You were looking for a glove. I remember thinking, that one has never lost a glove in her life."

"You saw me."

"I see most people. It's the trade." There is no sarcasm in it. "You let me go. I have wondered why. May I ask who you are?"

"No."

"No. Quite right." The tea-coloured eyes go to the needle, and back. "You will want to know why I'm here. I'll say, to save the stair. I am asked to watch the nurse. I am asked to be seen watching her. That is all. I'm told it's a kind of fence, a man who can be seen. Nobody is meant to be hurt."

"A man was hurt. On Wednesday."

His face does not close. It does something stranger. It becomes more open, as a house does when the last lamp is put out. "Yes," he says. "I closed his eyes. It seemed the least I could do."

She holds the wrist. She thinks of a landing in Pikk Street where an old man sat with his palms up. She thinks of a list in the lining of a bag.

"This nurse belongs to the Committee," she says. "She is mine until Monday. If I see you on her stairs again, or her pavement, or her tram, or in a door she goes through, I shall not ask you to mind anything."

"I don't take instructions from the Committee, Mrs..."

"Soini."

"Mrs Soini." He says it as if tasting it. "But I take your point, and I'm obliged. It is not a long way to Monday." He looks down at the needle on his pulse and gives, for the first time, a thin, rueful smile.

She lifts the needle. He rubs the wrist, once, and then takes the glove from her palm and draws it on, finger by finger, as carefully as a surgeon, and goes down the stairs, and she hears him all the way down, not hurrying, one flight, two, until the street door at the bottom opens on the cold and shuts again.

She stands on the landing with the peel at her back. Her hand, which has not been unsteady in thirty years, has a very small tremor in it, and she looks at it with interest, as if it had been left to her by a stranger.

* * *

There is one pay telephone left in the Harju Children's Hospital, in the entrance hall between a dead fountain and a machine that sells hot chocolate in the shape of an apology. It takes coins. It has been used this winter, so the lodge man will tell you if you ask him, by three fathers, a lawyer, and once a bishop.

She dials the number she learned by heart. The Helsinki porter answers in the voice of a man at the bottom of a lift. She says a name, and waits, and listens to the high room breathe.

"Mrs Soini," says Hint. "I did say no telephones."

"It is a hospital."

"That is not a recommendation."

"I have the nurse." She says it flatly, as she once said the position of a boat. "Rebane. On the ward tonight, and tomorrow night, and then nothing until she goes to ground. A man of the other party is watching her. I have seen him. I have spoken to him."

"Spoken."

"Briefly. He will not be a difficulty."

The line is silent. She can hear the clock again, and a second sound behind it, a kind of dry whisper, like one hand turning a page against another. "And the closing?" says Hint.

"Monday."

"The Committee asked for the weekend."

"The Committee asked for quiet. A hospital on a Saturday night is full of lamps, and a woman who has been dead in a corridor is a headline by eight. Monday. I shall bring her in alive, and you may ask what you like. A dead nurse will not tell you where the decoder is. She has been in the room with it for a hundred nights."

It is a good lie. It is the best she has ever told. It has a shape that Hint will recognise, a reason and a delay and a cost, and as she says it she hears herself pronounce the word *alive* as if it were a thing she could return.

"Monday, then," says Hint, after a time that is longer than she likes. "You are always so reasonable, Mrs Soini. It is why we engage you. Good night."

The line clicks and goes. She sets the receiver on its hook with the care of a woman putting a sleeper back to bed.

* * *

The linen room opens off the service corridor on the third floor, a long narrow cupboard with a door that does not close and shelves from floor to ceiling heaped with folded white things. It smells of bleach and steam and warm cotton. At the far end there is a hatch in the wall, the sort that was once used to pass trays, with a wooden shutter pushed up, and through it she can see a slice of Bay Two: a pool of green light, an incubator, a chair, and the back of a round woman with a pencil in her bun.

She did not mean to stay. She meant to look, and learn the corridors, and go. But the nurse is talking, and there is nothing in Marit's life that has ever kept her from listening to a person who is talking to a child.

"No, dear, listen. Listen to me. It isn't a difficult game. There are two teams and one ball and the thing is that nobody likes anybody." Pille has a baby in the crook of her arm, a scrap in a striped hat, one hand curled round her thumb. The baby's face is the size of a plum. He is, Marit judges, about a month early and a month old. "The Estonians are the ones in blue. They have played since the Flood and they have lost since the Flood. Their goalkeeper is a man called Aleksandr, he is very handsome, and he saves everything except penalties and his mother's feelings. Now, tonight. Tonight it is the Finns."

A small sound from the striped hat.

"I know. The Finns. Don't look at me like that, it's not their fault, they can't help it, they're tall." She rocks, once, twice. "Your auntie Pille has a gentleman, did you know? He follows her tram. Ever so polite, very clean boots, he never says a word. I have decided he is a suitor. Goodness, you've gone to sleep. Bad referee. Well, I'm not to be held to it. I'll tell you the score in the morning."

She sets him in the cot, tucks the blanket over, and with two fingers draws the hat a quarter of an inch lower on the small head. Then she stands there for longer than the task needs, her hand on the rail, looking down.

Marit has her own hand on the linen shelf, and finds she has pressed a swaddle flat.

The nurse knows. She has seen the man on the pavement, and made a suitor of him for the sake of a child who cannot understand, and come in to work all the same. Marit has met that kind of courage on a deck, in people who were afraid of the water and went out on it for a living. It does not look like anything. It looks like a woman telling a baby about football.

She thinks of a night off Porkkala with a boat hook in her hands, and of the strip of eleven names in the lining of her bag, and of a man in a tidy jacket saying *I closed his eyes*. For nineteen years the last pair of hands was the one thing she was good at. Nobody ever told her that hands can also be kept in the lap.

From her coat she takes the phone. It is a plain grey handset that Hint's man gave her in Helsinki, to be used in case of fire, and to be kept charged in every other case. Its small green eye has told Hint's people where she sleeps since Tuesday. She turns it over. She pries off the back with her thumbnail, and the battery slides out into her palm, a little flat thing with a dull silver face, as warm as a pebble.

On the shelf beside her, among the stacks of white, is a row of knitted boots, no bigger than egg-cups, in pale wool, waiting for some cold foot. She knows the pattern. The Thursday knitters did these. She lays the battery in one of them, and sets the phone in another, and pushes both to the back of the shelf, where they are no one's business but the laundry's. Then she takes the glove out of her pocket, looks at the neat grey darn at the thumb, and puts it away again.

Through the hatch the nurse is writing something at the desk, and humming, softly, without noticing, a tune that goes up in eight steps.

Marit does not hum it. She goes to the end of the linen room, and stands where the light from the hatch lies in a square on the floor, and watches the ward lights go down, one bay at a time, as the night shift dims the rooms for the small hours. First Bay One. Then Bay Three. Then the long corridor, its white going to grey. She watches the dark come on the way she once watched a grey sea close over a harbour, steadily, without malice. It is nearly one o'clock. By Hint's rules the courier rings at one with the hours and the doors, and by two the Committee has a map of this ward's night. The pay telephone is forty steps from where she stands.

She does not report.
