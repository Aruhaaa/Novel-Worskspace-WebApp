# The Canary

"Anni Põld," Pille writes in the agency book, in her upright capitals, "locum, neonatal, on call from Mustamäe." She reads it back, moving her lips, and puts a dot over the i with a little flourish. "There. You're a real person now. You've a mother in Rakvere and a bad knee."

"Which knee?"

"The one you're not standing on."

The night door of Harju is a steel one with a push-bar and a camera in a cage, and the porter holding it open is so tall that Ilse has to put her head back to meet his eye. Rein looks her up and down, from the paper cap that hides her white streak to the blue gown that Pille found for her in the sluice room, the mask hanging under her chin, the clogs two sizes too wide. "She said you were taller," he says.

"I'm tall."

"She said taller." He seems to find it a puzzle, and to put it away for later. "I never saw you. I'm a porter. I see trolleys. I tell anybody anything, but nobody ever asks me the right thing."

"What's the right thing?"

"I'll let you know," says Rein, and leans on the bar, and the door shuts behind them with a sound like a held breath let go.

It is ten to three on Sunday morning. They go up by the service stair with its smell of cold concrete, and at the third-floor landing Ilse stops for a second beside a radiator, where something dry and pale is curled against the pipe. It smells of orange. She does not know why it should make the back of her neck prickle, and moves on.

* * *

The ward at night is not quiet. It is a place that has learned to talk to itself in a very small voice, and Ilse, who has always listened to the world with one ear and the floor, is rapidly learning its grammar.

The beeps come from her right: she knows that by the soft edge of them, a pulse oximeter in Bay One, a drip pump in Two with its slow, dutiful chirp. She steers by them, as a ship steers by a lighthouse, turning her good ear to the sound and walking toward the place where the sound is least. Under the beeps, in the soles of her borrowed clogs, runs a deeper hum from the building itself, a pump in the plant room, a lift coming up from the ground, the long pulse of the ventilation. Where the floor changes under her, from vinyl to the rubber mat outside Bay Three, her step softens and her whole body knows where it is. The left side of her head is a grey wall. Everything real comes in on the right.

"Walk like you've been here for years," Pille murmurs. "Look bored. Look a bit cross. Locums always look cross."

They pass the glass-walled milk kitchen. They pass a trolley with a tray of syringes capped in blue. They pass the tartan owl on its shelf, with its single button eye, and the ranks of the others beside it, seventy-one of them in all, knitted, on the shelves above every cot, watching the babies with their stitched and slightly lopsided faces. On each shelf beside them sits a white stone the size of a fist, and in each stone a ring of light is breathing, white to pale and white again. Thirteen. One shelf in Bay Four holds only a pale circle where dust has not settled.

At the end of the corridor a door stands ajar, and from behind it comes the soft, dry, quick click of two needles. Ilse turns her head.

"The linen room," says Pille, without looking. "The pipes tick. It's very old, the plumbing. We think it's haunted. We think it's a woman who died in 1962 of knitting." She opens the glass door of the charting room and shoves Ilse into the light. "In you go, dear. Sit. Don't touch anything and look like you're allowed."

* * *

The records computer stands on a desk by the window, a grey tower with a flat screen, an old keyboard with a worn space bar, and on top of it, held down by a stack of patient folders, a knitted owl in a pink hat. It looks like nothing. It looks like the machine in any hospital that has been there since before the nurse. Ilse stands behind Pille's shoulder and looks at it for some while, a thing that has been singing at three o'clock for a hundred and twenty-three nights.

Pille logs in with a green lanyard and a six-digit number. The screen fills with grey boxes. She finds a tab marked *Events*, and there, as she once photographed it, is the line: *Audio endpoint, active.* Beside it is something Ilse had not noticed, a small grey counter, *queued 17, sent 0*.

"It keeps a queue," says Pille. "Look. Seventeen records. It counts them out like a woman with a purse."

"Seventeen."

"Tonight's lot. All the children of people who sign." Pille cracks her knuckles. "And now yours. Go on, dear. Tell me who she is."

"A girl," says Ilse.

"Name?"

She had thought she would say it flatly, as one reads a figure out of a table. "Lumi."

"Lumi." Pille types. "Surname?"

"Tamm-Kask."

The fingers stop. They stay above the keys, at a little distance, as a pianist's do after the last note. The nurse does not turn her head. In the dark edge of the screen, in the reflection of her face, Ilse watches her mouth work.

"Kringel man," says Pille, very quietly. "With a hyphen. He'd have said it was an extravagance."

"He'd have said *double the surname, half the baby*."

"He'd have said it wrong." Pille laughs, and it comes out wet, and she wipes her face with the back of the wrist that holds the pencil. "Right. Right, we're being professionals. Weight?"

"1,611 grams."

"Nice and small. A little madam. Born?"

"02:14."

"Poor lamb, forty minutes old. Gestation, thirty-three weeks, we'll say." She works down the form, field by field, with a nurse's speed. At the bottom there is a small grey box with a label Ilse has to bend to read, *Enhanced confidentiality (protected family)*, and a second field for the guardian's post. "Nobody ticks this unless there's a minister in the family, dear. In nineteen years I have ticked it four times. It's meant to keep the records quiet." She ticks it. In the guardian box she types, with a flourish, *Minister for Maritime Affairs, Republic of Latvia*. "Such a modest title. Nobody will remember a minister of fishing."

She presses save. The screen thinks. The counter says, *queued 18, sent 0.*

"There," says Pille softly. "She's born."

* * *

At three o'clock the speakers begin to breathe.

Ilse feels it before she hears it. She is sitting on a footstool in the charting-room doorway, with a spare cot parked beside her for the screen, her recorder in her lap under a fold of the gown, and her right hand on the cot's cold steel rail. And the rail trembles. It is the faintest thing, a shiver, the shiver of a glass on a table when a lorry goes by in the street. She would not feel it if she were not waiting for it. Then, from the shelf above Bay One, from the shelf in the corridor, from all thirteen, the first note comes.

It is quieter than on that first night. The IT head turned it down on Monday and nobody has dared turn it up. But it is there, round and soft and slightly sorrowful, a hum with a face, and then the second note, and the third, a staircase going up in the dark. By the fifth the whole ward has joined in. The sound comes out of the shelves and the walls and the owls' stitched faces, and the little green lights of the incubators go on shining under it, unbothered, a row of calm lamps. Ilse's palm sits flat on the rail and reads it like Braille.

She does not crack the cardamom. She turns the recorder face up in her lap. On its screen, ten euros from a man with a past, a ribbon of colour crawls left to right, a bright line, a dark gap, the line again. Eight notes. Eight notes. She does not need the numbers. She has the shape. In her wrist, quietly, her own pulse is running at what she recognises, with a small cold shock, as ninety-four.

"Is it all right?" whispers Pille from the desk. She has taken a chart and is pretending to read it. "Is it, you know? Behaving?"

"It's reading."

"To whom?"

"I don't know yet."

On the screen, at the foot of the Events tab, the counter ticks. *Queued 18, sent 1.* Then, a frame later, *sent 2.* The tune goes round and round the ward like a child on a ring road, and with every hundred seconds or so, with a patience that makes Ilse want to take hold of something, a note sags. Flat, and tired, and then the proper tune again. A full stop. A carriage return. Another line leaves the building, another small stranger in a blanket with a name.

Thirty-three minutes pass, and she feels each of them in the rail. Pille tops up a bottle. A pump alarms in Bay Two, a long rising complaint, and the nurse goes quickly and silences it and comes back with her hand over her chest, saying nothing. Twice a trolley goes by in the corridor, pushed by a porter who looks only at the floor. Somewhere in the walls a pipe ticks like needles.

At twenty-six minutes to four, the counter reads *queued 18, sent 17.*

Pille puts down her chart. She comes and stands in the doorway with her hand on the frame, round and silent in the green light. The tune has gone on again, and the speakers breathe, and Ilse counts, because it is what her heart is doing anyway. The notes climb. The seventh. And then the eighth drops, as it always drops, a half-step of tired air, a note with its shoulders down.

On the recorder's ribbon a line begins that she has never seen. It is thin and bright, a little different in its rhythm, as a voice is different when it says a name you know. She watches it cross the screen in eight-note steps, and the counter, without any fuss, turns its small grey face over.

*Sent 18.*

"That's her," says Pille.

"That's her."

Lumi Tamm-Kask, born at 02:14, one thousand six hundred and eleven grams, a minister of fishing's only child, leaves the building as a tune. She goes out through the white stone on the shelf above the cot, up through the ceiling of the ward and over the roof, through the cold, along a cable, to a rack in the south, and on. Ilse has imagined it for six days, and it is smaller than she thought. It is not a flash. It is eleven seconds of a lullaby, slightly flat, going out into the dark in the ordinary way, while somewhere an unseen reader writes her down.

She says, aloud, to nobody, "Go on, then."

The lights go out.

* * *

It is not a flicker. The whole third floor drops into black at once, ceiling, corridor, desk lamp, screen, and the white rings on the thirteen stones go out like thirteen eyes shut. The hum stops dead in the middle of a note. The rail under her hand stops shaking.

Ilse does not move. In the dark her good ear opens like a hand. She hears Bay Two's pump, still going, on its own battery. She hears a baby turn over in a cot with a small wet sound. She hears her own pulse, and she counts it, one, two, three, and under the count a long way off a lift cable shudders and lets go.

A hand finds her sleeve. It is round, strong, smells of chlorhexidine. "Battery, dear," whispers Pille, so close that Ilse feels the words. "Every incubator in the building's on battery. They don't even blink. Look." And there they are, in the black, the green lights, a long double row of them going away down the ward, calm as a runway. "Nothing happens to my babies. Nothing. I've been doing this nineteen years."

"I know."

"That's not us." The hand tightens. "That's not the ward, Ilse. That's the building. Somebody's turned off the building."

*Three.* The lights come back with a heavy thud, ceiling by ceiling, and everything in the world has been put one inch to the left. The hum has not begun again. The thirteen rings are dark, and then, one by one, they come on, white, breathing, like animals who have slept. The screen on the desk wakes, with a small stupid chime.

And at the far end of the corridor, under the smoked-glass dome, stands a man.

He is a long way off. At that distance he is a neat shape, a leather jacket the colour of toast, grey hair, a pair of hands held a little in front of him like a man who has come to the wrong door at a party. He has taken off his hat. On both hands are black wool gloves with the fingertips cut away. He stands very still on the vinyl. From where Ilse sits, in the doorway with the cold rail under her fist, he seems to be looking, with great politeness, at her.

He lifts one hand and gives a small apologetic wave.

"Oh," says Pille, in a voice with the bottom gone out of it. "That's my suitor."

Ilse is on her feet. The recorder goes into the front of her gown. And Pille, without another word, has dashed into the equipment store at the end of the charting room and come out again with her arms full: a coil of black power cable, two grey adaptors, a spare charger in a plastic bag, all of it bundled together in a white cellular blanket, folded round and round and tucked at the end into a small neat head. She pushes it into Ilse's arms. It is heavy and awkward and rustles. In the gloom it looks, exactly, like a baby wrapped for the night.

"Nobody stops a woman running with a baby," says Pille. "They get out of her way. Hold it by the head. Don't drop it, it's very expensive." Her face is white, the pencil sticking out of her bun like a flag. Down the corridor, a heel and then a sole come toward them on the vinyl, unhurried, light, and at the foot of the ward the dome watches. "Run with the baby."
