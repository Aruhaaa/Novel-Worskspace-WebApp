# What the Tune Carries

Ilse writes the decoder the way other people write letters of complaint: angrily at first, then in a tidier hand.

By seven on Monday evening the third floor has emptied. Helle has gone home, leaving a tin of kringel on every desk like a woman marking territory. Jaan left at half past five with his hat on crooked and a paper bag from the baker, and the last they saw of him he was standing at the foot of the stairs looking up, as though the building had said something he wanted to hear again. Taavi has not gone anywhere.

"I'm a stakeholder," he says, and orders noodles.

The method is a guess, then another guess, then a refusal to be embarrassed by the first two. On the wall screen the sixteen minutes of the ward scroll past as a ribbon, staircase after staircase, three seconds a climb. Ilse chops the ribbon into staircases and measures each step against where a piano would put it. Nearly all of them miss by a hair, sharp or flat, by two or three cents, and she gives the two directions the names a programmer gives anything with two states. Sharp is one. Flat is zero.

The first guess is that every note carries a bit. By nine o'clock she has proved it wrong, and the decoder prints a rain of little boxes and question marks.

The noodles come at ten past: two cartons from the Vietnamese place on Viru Street, in a paper bag gone transparent with steam. Taavi eats standing at her shoulder, chopsticks in a gloved fist, reading the screen as it throws errors. When it prints a whole line of nothing but question marks he says, "Sorry," to the monitor. "Sorry, sorry. I know. You didn't ask for any of this."

"It's a screen."

"It's a screen with feelings."

"Hold it still." She chews. The second guess comes in with the broth, and she says it aloud, because that is the only way she can tell a guess from a hope. "Seven notes carry. The eighth counts."

"Counts what?"

"The other seven. Seven bits make a letter. If there is an odd number of ones in a staircase, the eighth note leans sharp; if even, flat. A cashier counting the till twice before she shuts. When the till and the count disagree, I throw the letter out and leave a gap."

"And then?"

"And then I find out what the letters say."

By one in the morning the letters are right and wrong in a pattern, which is the worst kind of wrong. The first minute of the recording decodes clean, the second dirty, the third like a ransom note. Taavi, folded into the beanbag under the window with a glove over his eyes, offers from the dark that this might be a Tuesday problem. It is, as of midnight. She is too tired to laugh and too well brought up not to notice that it was funny.

At half past two she sees what the big sag is for. She had taken it for punctuation. It is a clock. The recording drifts, sixteen minutes of cheap speaker electronics and fridge compressors, so that every staircase arrives a few thousandths of a second later than her decoder expects, and by the third minute her cuts are falling between the steps. Every ninety-four seconds the sender drops one note by thirty cents and the reader knows where it is. The flat note is the one thing in the tune that carries nothing. It is there so the reader never loses its place.

She teaches the decoder to wait for it. "That," she says to the empty room, "is why you keep the appointment."

At ten to four the tubes in the ceiling have been humming at fifty cycles for twenty hours, and she can no longer tell which hum is hers. She walks the room and flips the switches off bank by bank until the only light is the screen. She turns the laptop so that its small speaker faces the wall, because a sound thrown at a wall comes back softer, and she wants it soft. She wants to hear it the way the ward hears it.

She sits with her right ear to the machine and her deaf side to the window, where Old Town lies under snow and nothing moves on Pikk Street but a single taxi with its lamp off. She sets the decoder to print as it plays, and starts the recording.

Sixteen minutes of the ward come out of the laptop, small and close: eight notes, eight notes. A lullaby with no baby in it. She was taught to listen at six, in a hospital, when the left side of the world was taken away and she was told to make the most of the right, and she listens now with her whole head, the way you listen for a car in a snowstorm.

For the first minute the screen gives her boxes. Then, at two minutes to four by the clock in the corner, the boxes thin out and letters stand up in them.

F. I. A space. S. A. L. M.

One letter to a staircase, so slowly that she reads each before the next arrives, like watching a stranger write on a window in frost. I. V. A. A. R. A. The tune goes round, soft and tidy and nobody's, and under it a name is being spelled by something that does not know it is a lullaby.

FI SALMIVAARA VENLA 15.12 1840G

She knows the surname before the last A lands. FI is Finland. Venla is the first name. A birth date, day first, then a dot, then the month, the way Finns write it: the fifteenth of December, two weeks ago. A weight in grams. One thousand eight hundred and forty, not much more than two bags of sugar, a child born too soon.

And she knows Salmivaara, because a man comes with the name: short grey beard, heavy eyes, the Finnish Minister of Transport and Communications, on the evening news last week at a harbour with a drum of fibre cable the thickness of a horse behind him, telling the cameras with a tired laugh that he had not slept for a fortnight because of his new daughter. The reporters had laughed with him. Ilse had been eating toast.

The tune goes round. A second line has already begun to spell itself under the first. She does not read it. She puts out a hand and finds it lying flat on the desk, a habit she has when the floor goes.

"Taavi."

"I wasn't sleeping," says the beanbag. "I was resting my eyes in bullet points."

"Read this."

He crosses the dark in his socks, the glove still stuck to one cheek, and reads over her shoulder. His lips move. His face does something she has seen only on people watching a lift cable go. "That's the— That's the minister. That's the Transport one. Oh no. Oh no, no, no."

"Keep reading."

The decoder finishes its sixteen minutes and the screen holds eleven lines. Two have gaps where the cashier's count failed. The rest are clean. Taavi reads them aloud with his gloved finger under each, like a child at school. "FI again. LV. SE. PL. DK. LT." He sits back. "Latvia, Sweden, Poland, Denmark, Lithuania. That's a Baltic cruise with a very strange guest list."

He types faster in gloves than any man should be allowed. Six government websites come up one after another, and under each surname he finds it, on a cabinet page or a ministry's staff list: a Latvian state secretary, a Swedish minister, a Polish deputy minister, a Danish minister of state, a Lithuanian vice-minister. Six countries. Six small weights in grams.

"These aren't just babies, Ilse. These are the ministers' babies."

"The babies of people who sign things."

"But why would anyone steal a weight? A birthday?"

"Nobody steals a weight for the weight." She is looking at the surnames, lined up like a seating plan. "Where do they come from? Not from Harju. The Salmivaara child is in Helsinki."

Taavi is already searching. "KindWard," he says. "The Baltic Paediatric Alliance. One record for every child in every Alliance hospital, so a baby can be moved between wards and nobody has to weigh her again. Every ward keeps copies. Harju's got everybody's." He reads on and goes quiet. "It's a sealed network. It's not supposed to touch anything."

"It doesn't. It has no road out, not to the internet, not even to the building it sits in. The speakers live on the other network, the one that runs the lights and the heating. Two houses, and no street between them." She turns the page in her head. "Something inside the sealed house made a road out of sound. And it didn't walk there. Someone put it there. Someone with hands on the machine. An insider. A mole, maybe."

Taavi flinches. It is small, like a cat that has heard foil. "Don't say mole."

"It's a word."

"It's a word that ends careers." He tugs his cuffs down over his wrists.

"And then?" says Ilse.

"And then nothing. I'm thinking about noodles."

She lets it go. She puts it in the place where she keeps things, next to the camera dome and the pockets of Evald Olesk.

"Where does it go?" she says. "After the speakers."

Taavi is back inside the Mini before she finishes. The little speaker keeps a diary. Every time it talks to the building-management network, the one that decides whether the corridor lights are on and whether the radiator in Bay Two is warm, it writes down a line in a ring of memory nobody meant anyone to read. Address, time, size. He scrolls. Every night at twelve minutes past three it speaks to the same address, for a minute and a half, for two kilobytes. He runs the address through one registry and then another, and reads off the answer like a man reading a bad tooth from an X-ray. "Singapore. Tanjong Vault. It's a data centre. Colocation, you rent a locker for your server, like at the swimming pool. And the name of the locker—" He leans in. "Rack 14-B."

"A rack," says Ilse. "One."

"One rack in Singapore for fourteen speakers in a children's ward in Tallinn." He drags the scroll bar with a gloved fingertip and counts under his breath, in Estonian, then in English, loses count, and starts again. "A hundred and seventeen."

"Nights?"

"Lines. One per night. The first one's the fourth of September."

The fourth of September. Through the rest of the autumn and the first snow, through every night shift Pille Rebane has worked, the ward has sung into the dark at twelve minutes past three, and nobody heard, because it was too quiet to be a sound. Until Thursday, if Evald Olesk can be believed. Until a man with crossed sevens sat in the parents' lounge for an hour looking up at the shelves.

She opens the notebook. With the pencil Jaan gave her she writes WHY LOUD NOW? and, beneath it, THURSDAY, and does not write the next thing that comes to her.

"So we call the police," Taavi says.

She has the pencil in one hand and the phone beside it. It is twenty to six. Outside the window nothing has changed; dawn in Tallinn in December is a rumour that arrives after nine. There is a number in Estonia for everything: a number for a lift stuck between floors, a number for a heron in a stairwell. There is no number for a song that is reading the children of six governments. The Estonian police could take a statement about a hospital ward. The data belongs to Finland, to Latvia, to four others, and it has gone to a rack in Singapore, and whoever put it there has the patience of a hundred and seventeen nights. Which police? Whose? And if the ones who ought to hear this are among the ones who already know...

"I don't know which," she says.

"Which what?"

"Which police."

Taavi looks at her, and then at the surnames, and for once he says nothing at all.

She turns to a clean page and writes it in capitals, the way Jaan writes: SIX COUNTRIES. She underlines it. She underlines it again, harder, and a third time until the lead gives out, and then she goes on until the wood does, and the pencil snaps in her hand and a splinter of cedar lands on the page like a full stop.
