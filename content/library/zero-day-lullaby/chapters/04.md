# The Courier

The toe is the part of a sock you cannot hurry, and Marit Soini is down to the last twelve stitches of one when a man she does not care for crosses the terminal floor toward her.

She does not look up. The needles go on at the pace of her pulse, which is slow. The wool is grey and undyed, from a farm in Savo, the grey of a seal's back. She holds the working needle between her thumb and three fingers because of the fourth: the little finger of her right hand ends at the first joint, cut clean by a mooring line off Porkkala in 2008, and it has never since got in the way of anything she meant to do.

Katajanokka terminal at half past eight on a Tuesday is a hall of grey carpet and blue light beside a harbour frozen to the breakwater. The café sells cinnamon buns the size of a baby's head and coffee in cardboard. Finns in anoraks queue for the Tallinn ferry with the solemn faces of pilgrims, pushing empty shopping trolleys, men who intend to come home heavy with beer.

Ülo Hint sits down opposite her without being asked. He is a thin man in a good grey overcoat the exact colour of the carpet, so that he seems to have risen out of the floor, and he sets a paper cup on the table which he will not drink. His hands are pale and scrubbed. He is secretary to a Committee whose members he has never once named to her, and in eleven years he has paid her in cash, in notes with the corners softened by other hands.

"Mrs Soini."

"Yes."

"You had a pleasant crossing?"

"I live here."

"Of course." His English has every article in place, like a table laid for guests who will not come. He puts an envelope down beside the sugar bowl: cream paper, the flap taped, a signature drawn across the tape. He does not own a telephone. He says they are loquacious. It is the one opinion of his that she shares. "You will want to know the arrangement."

"Yes."

"A matter has arisen. An instrument, which our—which was never intended to be heard, has been heard." He turns the cup a quarter turn on its ring of damp. "In a children's hospital in Tallinn, by a nurse, in the first instance. By a small firm of responders, in the second. It lives in domestic speakers. Of the Pesa make. You will have seen them."

Marit looks up. Above the café counter hangs a banner as long as a bus, a white stone on a field of blue. PESA. Under it, in smaller letters: OFFICIAL SOUND PARTNER, GULF CABLE ACCORD SUMMIT, HELSINKI, and a date seven days away. A stone that listens. She looks down again and turns the needle.

"Heard," she says.

"In a manner of speaking."

"What does it do?"

"It carries." He says it as though it were a figure of speech. "The Committee would like to know who carries it. Who built the means of reading it, and who holds that now. A decoder, if you prefer. It would be convenient if the decoder came to the Committee and not to a newspaper. And it would be convenient if what remains were closed."

"What remains."

"Ends. Loose ones."

She has been told to close things for eleven years of Tuesdays. A person can close a door, an account, a mouth. Hint has never once said which. She slits the tape with her thumbnail. Inside is a stiff white card with three lines in a clerk's upright hand: Kantor, Ilse, of Tamm Incident Response, Pikk Street, Tallinn. Tamm, Jaan, the same. And the night nurse of the neonatal ward at Harju, name to follow.

"The old man." She touches the second line with the bitten end of a needle. "Why him?"

"He called at the ward on Thursday. The camera has him." Hint straightens the cup. "He was a colleague, once, of colleagues of ours. A long time ago, in a different line of work."

"Once."

"It is of no present concern."

"And her."

"Miss Kantor was summoned by the nurse. She is the one reading it."

"Alive?"

"The Committee prefers quiet." Hint lifts his eyes to hers, pale and attentive, with nothing in them she could take hold of. "Quiet is generally achievable by several means."

A second envelope, thick, goes down beside the first. Half now, half on closing. Marit counts it through the paper with her thumb, the way a harbour master once taught her to count a catch without the fish knowing, and finds it right. Hint is never wrong about money. It is the only place he allows himself to be exact.

"You will begin in Tallinn?"

"Yes."

"You will report?"

"Not yet."

"Naturally." He rises, and the carpet takes him back.

* * *

The ten-thirty follows the icebreaker out, a red smudge ahead in the white, and the sea closes behind them in a path of black water that scabs over before the ship has finished passing. Marit takes a window seat in the big lounge and spreads the sock on her knee. Around her the Finns settle to the business of pea soup and pancakes with jam, and the duty-free shop opens its doors like a shrine.

An old man sits across the aisle, eighty at least, in a tweed cap and a coat gone shiny at the cuffs, with a thermos the size of a fire extinguisher. He pours a cup, hesitates, and holds it across to her.

"My wife packs for six," he says. "We are two. We are always two."

"Kiitos." The coffee is weak and hot and tastes of a kitchen she has never been in.

"Do you know the ice? Which side is better? For looking."

She does not turn her head. Out past the second buoy the sea is going from black to pewter to the dull white of old teeth. "Right," she says. "After the lighthouse. The wind has been north-east since Saturday. It piles the floes against the east side of the channel. It will be thickest there, and loud. You will hear it before you see it. Keep your coffee in your hand."

"Thank you," says the old man, and moves to the right-hand windows with his cap and his thermos, and does not ask how she knows. In Finland you do not ask a woman with a grey braid how she knows the ice.

Nineteen years she knew it for a living. Brash and pancake, grey ice and white, the rafted sheets that climb on one another like cattle at a gate. In February 2011, off Porkkala, a woman in a man's camel overcoat walked out across ice that was only pretending, toward a ship that had no business being there. The ice gave up at forty metres. Marit, on the bow of the cutter with a boathook and a line, took her out of the black water by the collar, and the coat weighed as much as a second person, the wool drinking the Gulf. The woman coughed up a litre of it and said, in a voice dragged over gravel and then smoked, "Slowly. Again."

Marit had said something quick and Finnish. So she said it again, slowly. Hold. The. Line. And the reporter held. The name came later, off a hospital whiteboard: Anneli Raud. Every December since, a postcard arrives from Tallinn with no message on it but a pencil drawing of a boathook.

Somewhere after the lighthouse the ice begins to talk, a long low scrape along the hull like a shout held in one breath. At the right-hand windows the old man flinches, then smiles at the glass. Marit goes back to the card.

Hint said our. He mended it to which, fast, the way a man steps over a crack in a pavement, but she heard the step. She turns it over as she turns a heel, a stitch at a time. A Committee that sends a courier to a children's hospital instead of a policeman is not afraid of a thief. It is afraid of an audience. A decoder is the thing that tells an audience what a song has been saying. If the Committee wants the decoder before the newspapers do, then what the song has been saying is something it would rather not hear read aloud; and if the word on Hint's tongue was our, then what the song carries is theirs, or was paid for by them, or was built with their blessing. Their own tool is in danger of being found out.

She does not ask why. Asking was the first habit she lost, and she has not missed it.

Down the lounge a child is shrieking with joy at the ice. The old man, his thermos between his knees, is telling his wife on the telephone that the lady was right, the ice is on the right, you would not believe it, it is like the whole sea has been broken up and put back wrong.

* * *

Tallinn comes up out of the white at half past one, a toy town of copper spires and tiled roofs under a low orange sun that will be gone by three. The terminal is a long glass shed. At the cabin sales desk, a clerk with hennaed hair and a name badge that says KERLI looks at Marit's grey braid, her old waxed coat and her bag of wool, and decides she is somebody's aunt.

"Seven nights," Marit says. "The twenty-three hundred to Helsinki. Back each morning. Cabin 7118."

"An inside cabin? There are nicer ones. With a window. For a bit more."

"This one."

"It's by the stairs," Kerli warns. "You'll hear people."

"Yes."

"Cash?"

"Cash."

Kerli counts the notes twice and prints the confirmation on a slip of paper. A cabin on a ship that crosses the border twice a day is a post office with no postman. Behind the vent grille above the lower bunk in 7118 there is a gap the width of an envelope, and Marit has known it since a winter she does not discuss. Whatever she needs to leave, or to find, will wait there in the dark until she comes.

She sits on a bench by the terminal's tall window, looking out at the ice and the cranes standing in it, and knits the last three rows. Then she threads the darning needle and grafts the toe shut, stitch against stitch, so that no seam is left for a foot to find. It takes eleven minutes. She does not hurry it.

When it is done she holds the sock up to the light. A grey tube with a closed end, a thing that holds a foot and asks nothing. Hint's card is in her pocket with the names on it. A woman who cannot hear on one side, though Hint did not tell her that. An old man whose vest, the camera says, was buttoned wrong. A nurse not yet named.

She bites the wool through. She tucks the loose end into her coat at the top buttonhole, the way another woman might press wax to a letter, and the sock goes in her pocket, finished.
