# The Last Pin

The thaw comes on a Thursday. Water runs everywhere in Tallinn. It comes off the eaves in Pelgulinna in long bright ropes, and drums on the lids of bins, and races ankle-deep along the gutters toward the drains with a sound like a very small crowd applauding, and Ilse, who has spent three weeks learning what her right ear can do, hears all of it. She walks to the hospital. A tram would be a room, and she has the time. The prosecutor has given her little else.

Kaie Lõhmus read her the article again on the Monday after the summit, in a room with a window, with the umbrella across her knees. One count. Unauthorised access to a computer system, by means of a release signed on a Tuesday afternoon in Helsinki, in the presence of five hundred witnesses and forty cameras. "It is the law, and it is written down," said Lõhmus. "The rest of it is also written down now. In a good many places." Trial in March. A passport in a drawer. Nine o'clock on Fridays at Kesklinn, where Lepik gives her a biscuit from the drawer marked Evidence and tells her nothing, loudly.

Other things she knows by heart, because Taavi reads them aloud to her off the news. Ülo Hint resigned by letter, in pencil, on the Wednesday, and the Committee has paused itself, which is what committees do instead of dying. In Singapore Dr Ong has sworn to what she saw before a judge who writes with a fountain pen. Chidi is on every channel he owns. In a Finnish cell a man called Sarapuu is said to have asked for a duster. Helle is in Haapsalu with her sister and has been seen at a bakery counter, buying nothing, reading the labels. And of the man with the mandarin there is no word, because the country he serves has never been named, and says nothing, which is what it does.

The doors of the third floor open on quiet. It is not the quiet of a Monday night in December. It is the quiet of a room from which something has been taken. Ilse stands at the end of the corridor with her good ear to the ward and her deaf side to the glass, as she stood once, and waits for a number she cannot hear, and there is no number. The shelves are bare. On each one, in the dust, a pale circle the size of a fist shows where a stone sat. The knitted owls remain. All seventy-one are still in their rows, with their wonky ears and their button eyes, looking at the empty circles with an air of having expected this.

"I keep checking the shelves," says Pille, behind her. "Three weeks. I go round at three in the morning with my head on one side, listening for it, like a woman who has stopped smoking and keeps patting her pocket. Dead is very quiet, dear. This is just empty." She comes up on Ilse's right, where she has learned to stand. "Look who's escaped."

She is in the green fleece, with the pencil through the bun and her hands smelling of chlorhexidine, and she has put on a stone in the weeks since the Nightboat, or lost one, Ilse cannot tell which. The Director who suspended her has gone to spend time with a solicitor. The Director who has replaced her reinstated Pille on a Tuesday morning by sending a bunch of tulips with a card that said *sorry* in capital letters and was signed twice. Evald Olesk is on leave, in a sense that will take some time to explain. Pille takes Ilse's elbow and steers her down the corridor to the milk kitchen as if returning a patient.

"I've got something. Shut your eyes."

"I can't hear with my eyes shut."

"Then open them and be quick."

It is a Kopli bakery bag. Someone has crossed out EVIDENCE across the side in thick pencil and written under it, in the same hand, PRESENT. Inside is a box of pale birch the size of a loaf of rye, with a darker owl inlaid on the lid, and when Ilse lifts the lid she sees steel and brass: a comb, a cylinder, and a spiral of pins. She counts without meaning to. Eight.

"A Mr Pihlak, in Pärnu," says Pille. "Nine days. Taavi gave him the recording from the hall, and he tuned the last tooth against it with a fork. I thought it was time Pärnu did something for me." She sniffs. "Tarmo's sofa is there."

Ilse winds the key. The cylinder turns and the comb takes up the tune, one, two, three, bright and small in the milk-kitchen air, up through seven, and then the eighth, round and low, a note with its feet on the floor. It does not sag. She holds the box to her right ear and listens to it all the way down, and when it has run out she goes on holding it.

"Hold this," says Pille, and puts a baby in her arms.

She does it the way she does everything, quickly and without warning, from the Bay Four crib that has no shelf above it. Swaddled tight, in a striped hat. A girl, nine days old, nineteen hundred and eighty grams, with a mouth like a rosebud under a hand. Ilse has not held a baby since she was a child herself. She stands with her elbows wrong and her heart going, and the baby gives a small damp sigh against the wool of her sleeve, and Ilse hears herself humming.

She hasn't been asked to. It is only the tune, low, in the back of the throat, the way a person hums over a sink. One. Two. Three. All eight. The baby's fist opens against her wrist and does not close.

"There," says Pille, very softly. "That's the end of that."

Taavi is waiting in the lobby when she comes down, with a Thermos and a face that has been up since five. "Soup," he says. "Pea. For Pille's night. I thought, soup, it's the oldest answer." He sits on the radiator and the Thermos steams between his gloves. "Bullet points. One, I sleep with my phone in the bathroom now, under a towel, with the tap dripping. It's not that I think it's listening. It's that I think it might be thinking about it. Two, Raud rang."

"And?"

"She says your Courier has a job." He has never once, in three weeks, said Marit's name. "Mending nets, on Kallavesi, for a man with a smokehouse. No stairs, she said. She sent Pille socks. Grey, with a note, three words, I wasn't told which."

Ilse says nothing. In her coat pocket, behind the foil twist that the prosecutor's office returned to her in a clear bag with a form, lies a postcard Raud sent over on the Monday. A pencil boathook on the front. Blank on the back. She turns it over with her thumb. One does not know, she thinks, how to write to a woman who kept a list of eleven names, and lost the end of a finger for the twelfth. She has not decided. She has until March.

At four the parents come for Ott.

He goes down the corridor in a car seat the size of a bread bin, held in front of his father with both hands like a crown, a tall man in a jacket that still has the shop fold in it. Two thousand four hundred and ten grams. Off everything. His mother walks behind with the bag, and in the seat beside him, with its ears at two different angles, sits the grey owl with the wonky ears that went to Helsinki and came back.

"Right," says Pille, planted in the middle of the corridor, with her arms folded across her chest like a woman barring a door. "Instructions. Feed him. Burp him. Keep him away from any woman with a bag of rusks. If anyone offers to fix your lock, say thank you and use the window." The father laughs, uncertain. The mother takes her hand. "And you'll write. A card. A photograph. Nothing with a speaker in it."

"We will," says the mother.

"And he's to have his jabs on time, he's a good boy, he's the best boy on the ward, he's—" Pille's chin goes. She says, "Oh, dear," in quite a different voice, and puts the back of her wrist against her mouth, and the tears come at once and in a rush, like a tap that has been stiff for years. The mother puts an arm around her. Ott, in his seat, regards the ceiling. Nobody in the corridor says anything for a while. Ilse stands with the box in her pocket and her good ear to the whole of it.

Then the lift doors close on the three of them, and the corridor is empty.

She goes to the window at the end of the ward. It faces west, over the roofs of Pelgulinna, and the thaw has turned them all to a dark wet shine, and the gutters pour, and out beyond the last block, small and silver under a low sun, the sea has lost its ice at the edges. She stands with her hands on the sill. She hears the water, and a gull, and her own breath, and in all of it, for the first time in months, no machine.

It does not feel as she had thought. It feels like the quiet after a long note. She takes a pod from her pocket, the first she has dared to crack since Monday week, and cracks it in her teeth, aloud, with nobody and nothing to hear. The seed bursts bright and bitter. Somewhere there is a listener who has not been named. A third kind of reader, who came in by the name of an old street, who watched a child leave a building, who answered a hall with a line of lower-case text and then went quiet. They may be listening still. They may be listening now, through the glass, to a woman at a window eating a spice.

"Kurat," says Ilse, mildly, to the sea.

And a note sounds on her right.

It is not loud. It comes from the corridor behind the lift lobby, a hall she cannot see, round the corner: a woman's voice, low, a hum over a baby's head as the lift waits. Ott's mother. One. Two. Three. The tune is the one that every kitchen in Europe has learned this month, off every screen: seven notes, up the stair, and then the stair stops. The voice reaches the seventh, and hangs, and does not know the rest.

Ilse puts down the pod. She turns her right ear to the corridor and opens her mouth, and supplies the eighth note, aloud, low and round, and lets it ring.
