# First Ferry

By the time the ferry clears the breakwater, Helle's ledger has been in the lining of Ilse's coat for two hours. She sewed it in on the floor of the van at half past five, by a bike lamp that Taavi held in his teeth, with a needle from his glove-repair tin and a length of mustard thread: four sheets of green ink, folded small and flat, lying along her ribs so that every breath she takes is measured against them. The scans are on the grey laptop in the rucksack, fed through Madis's invoice scanner at seventeen seconds a sheet while the shop heater clicked. The paper itself goes where she goes. A heavy coat is a very good lie.

She has bought the ticket in her own name. That was the van's decision, taken somewhere between the sewing and the noodles: a false name needs a false card, and the only man in Tallinn who sells them does not answer before nine. KANTOR, I., one adult, window. The girl at the counter types it without looking at her face, and the system that could tell the whole Gulf where Ilse is for the next two and a half hours is, at half past seven, still asleep. At nine Inspector Lepik will look at an empty chair. In the room across the corridor, at five past, he will look at another, Pille's. She hopes he finds a biscuit.

Beyond the bow window the harbour ice is grey, young and two hands thick, and the ferry leans into it and it breaks in plates that tilt and slide and roll along the hull, each one crackling as it goes, like someone walking on a very large bag of crisps. Ilse stands with her right ear to the glass and her left to the room, and feels the whole building shudder in her palm.

"Bullet points," says Taavi, arriving with two coffees and a paper bag. He has not slept, and it has made him formal. "One, it is a Finnish bun. Two, the Finns put cardamom in it, I asked the girl, she looked at me like I was a tourist. Three, I thought you should be in the vicinity of a pod."

It is plaited and glazed and the size of a fist. She picks three pods from the crumb with a fingernail, green and papery and a little sticky, and puts them in her pocket, and the pocket is full of something for the first time since Monday. She does not crack one. There is no telephone within ten metres of her. She does not crack one anyway.

"Say it," she says.

"I keep saying the plan in my head and it sounds like a bad idea in a good accent."

"And then?"

"And then I stop, because the alternative is Helle's pastry on a policeman's table and Jaan on a stair." He pushes his gloves further up his wrists. "I'm in. I only wanted it on the record that I'm frightened."

"Noted," says Ilse, and he looks at her, and almost laughs.

Helsinki at a quarter past ten is blue snow and a low gold sun and trams ringing their bells along the Esplanade. The Hotel Pohjola stands on a corner with a doorman in a long grey coat and a lobby that smells of coffee and floor polish, and its breakfast room is a long white hall in which two hundred people in lanyards are eating rye bread with the concentration of those who must defend a treaty by three. The lanyards say GULF CABLE ACCORD in letters you could read from the door. The clatter of cups comes at Ilse from the left as one grey wall, which her left ear hands straight to the dark, and she crosses the room by faces.

Dr Ong sits at a table by the window in her grey cardigan, with a steel flask the size of a baby's leg and a glass mug, and a lanyard at her chest that reads TANJONG VAULT, DELEGATE. She does not stand. She lifts the mug an inch.

"You flew," says Ilse.

"Finnair, ten to six. The seat was acceptable." Ong pours hot water for Taavi without being asked, which is, Ilse understands, an honour. "Tanjong Vault holds a seat in Hall A for the data sovereignty session. It has been empty since August, because my director dislikes ministers. He believes I am at a cooling-systems fair in Frankfurt." She slides two blue paper wristbands across the cloth. "Technical staff. A delegate may bring two, when the vendor audit clause applies. The names are written at the registration desk, in marker, by a volunteer who has looked at four hundred faces since eight o'clock and has stopped seeing any of them."

"Which clause?"

"Fourteen. A delegate may inspect any connected device in the hall." The mug does not move. "I intend to inspect sixty."

At ten past eleven the laptop rings, and Chidi arrives in a small square, shouting.

"Can you hear me? I can't hear me! This is a bad line! It is the line, not me!" He stands on a Lagos pavement in the thin early light, in a lime-green shirt, with a shop shutter behind him and a motorcycle taxi cutting the picture in half. Somewhere off-camera a goat offers its view. "You hear that? Three phones. Since yesterday afternoon, one number, same number, ringing. I have not picked up. My mother says pick up, my lawyer says don't, the goat says nothing. I put them in a bucket, you hear me? A bucket of ringing. I am walking round Lagos like a church bell in a bucket."

"Don't answer," says Ilse.

"I am many things, but I am not a fool in a bucket." He pushes his face at the camera and drops his voice, which in Chidi means that it is merely loud. "Listen. Kobo has until Friday. After Friday I am a man with a nice shirt and a lawyer. You do what you do, you give me the minute. I have four hundred and eleven thousand people who think I sell football shirts. On your minute, I sell something else."

"Noted," says Ong.

"She said it! Mei Lin said it!" He points at the sky, as if for a witness. "Play to injury time, you hear me? The ninety-fourth minute is where it is won." The square goes grey with a flicker of goat.

At half past eleven every lanyard in the breakfast room turns its head, because a woman in a felt hat with reindeer ears has come in carrying a baby bag with a duck on it. She stops in the doorway and takes in the two hundred and the tablecloths and the flag of the Accord on its pole, and says to the nearest waiter, in clear carrying English, "Dear, I'm told there's rye bread."

She has the look of a person who has been dragged through a hedge by the ears and then sat on by the hedge. Ilse is on her feet before she has decided to be. She is not a woman who hugs. She gets her right hand onto Pille's shoulder and leaves it there, and Pille, who is, hugs her round the middle with the whole bag, so that the owl is pressed against the ledger in Ilse's lining and a rusk breaks.

"A girl with a car," says Pille into her coat. "Raud's. I said the number. She said, Slowly. I said it again. I've said everything again, I'm very good at it now."

She unpacks the bag on the white cloth in the order of the crossing: the tin of kringel, the tin of soup ("it was meant to be cheese"), the owl with the wonky ears, three sad packets of rusks, and, in the middle of a scarf, the stone. The ring is dark. Dr Ong lifts it by the cord with two fingers, as she would lift a specimen from a jar, and turns it to the window, and looks for a long time at a small white thing that was once a gift to a nurse's mother.

"Noted," she says, very quietly.

Taavi has opened the kringel and is eating one with his eyes shut. Pille watches him do it, and her face does what faces do when they have been brave for ten hours and are asked for something small. "There's a message," she says. "Meilahti. Fourth floor, the bed by the window. She says she has a sock for you." She waits. "I took that for blood loss."

"What happened to her?" says Ilse.

"A locker door. The end of a finger. A great deal of blood." Pille's chin works. "On account of my mother's biscuit stone, and I'm not making the joke. Somebody else make it."

Nobody does.

The fourth floor at Meilahti smells of lemon polish and boiled milk. Marit lies in the bed by the window with her left hand in a white club of bandage, propped on two pillows, and her right outside the sheet with its short finger. Her braid is over one shoulder. On the locker a pair of needles lies useless on a paper bag. She watches the three of them come through the door as she might watch a boat come in.

"Yes," she says. "Sit."

She tells it in the order of distance, as a rescuer reports a wreck. The pigeons. A newspaper laid on a cold bench. A farm in Savo with a barn that does not leak. A man on a ship with a roll of tools who asked her three things for the Colonel, and asked none of them like a question.

"Where the nurse is," she says. "What she carries. And the hour."

"The hour of what?" says Ilse.

"The vote." Marit's eyes go to Ilse's left ear and then, correctly, to the right, and she shifts her head on the pillow to speak to the good side. "The Committee moved it. Sunday night, to be over before a newspaper could wake. Hint put the hour on a card, for me alone, so that I would stand in the right gallery. He does not trust his walls any more. The Colonel has the day and not the hour. He asked it three times in one night and never once as a question. A man who wants one thing very much does not ask for it. He feeds pigeons."

"What would he do with it?" says Taavi.

"Arrive at the minute. With a laptop, or a cassette, or a woman. And tell six ministers whose children they are." She tips her chin at the locker. "Under the pillow. A sock."

It is grey, half made, the needles still in. Ilse turns it inside out and finds the toe, and in the toe a card the size of a stamp, folded four times, in small upright pencil with the verbs underlined.

> Tues. Hall A. Press pool in 15:00, out 15:05. Doors close 15:05. Clause nine called 15:10. Gallery, second row. U.H.

She reads it twice. Five minutes of open doors, and ten to the vote. A hall full of everyone who matters, and not one of them able to leave.

"You could have given it to him," Ilse says.

"Yes."

"And then?"

"Then I would have a farm." Marit looks at her bandage. "I have a policeman outside who is twenty years old and brings me tea. It is also very good."

Dr Ong has borrowed a box of paper clips from the concierge and a floor plan of Hall A from the summit's delegate pack, and by ten to one they are laid out on a low glass table in the lobby, between a potted palm and a bowl of Christmas oranges. The hall is a long oval. Sixty paper clips ring the ceiling in a pale chain, one every four metres.

"The sponsor's gift," says Ong. "Pesa Concert units, set into the cove lights. Wired to one white box in the glass booth at the back. The box is the only door the hall speaks to the speakers through. Hand it a signed release, and it speaks to all sixty at once. Then it telephones Pesa to say a new release is installed, and Pesa's server, seeing a signature it knows, offers the same letter to every other nest on earth in the hour that follows."

"Forty thousand," says Taavi.

"A little over. Noted."

Ilse puts a finger on the paper clip nearest the booth. "The pumps? The monitors in the wards?"

"On their own board. The speakers sit on the building network. They play and they listen and they do nothing else. Nobody at Harju loses a drip."

"I would stake an ear on that," says Ilse.

"You only have one," says Pille, from the sofa, and it is the first joke she has made since the Nightboat.

Taavi turns up his cuffs. "The sponsor loop. Pesa's logo goes round on the big screen between sessions, off the same box, with the release notes underneath. Helle typed Thursday's. We type this one. Three listeners and their addresses. Dr Ong's logs. The ledger."

"Hint's card says the press pool comes in at three," says Ilse. "We send at three."

"The speakers take a little under two minutes to accept a letter," says Ong. "Then they sing the whole tune aloud, up to Jaan's seventh note, and wait for the eighth. They will wait ninety-four seconds. After that they return to what they were."

"And I sing."

"With one ear," says Pille.

"With the box." Taavi has opened the rucksack: the music box in its bread bag, seven notes and a bare tooth. And under it, in a pink-stamped bakery napkin, the other thing. He unwraps it on the glass between the oranges and the paper clips and says nothing at all, because it does not need him to.

A plain grey stick of plastic with a gold tongue at one end. It looks like something from a petrol station. It signs once.

Ilse picks it up. It has been against his chest since Friday, under three layers of wool, and it is warm. She closes her fist round it, and it sits there like a pebble taken out of a pocket on a long walk.
