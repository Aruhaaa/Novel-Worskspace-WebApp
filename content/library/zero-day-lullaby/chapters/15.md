# A Little Firewall

"If I'm not down in thirty minutes," Ilse says, "you go."

"Go where?"

"Anywhere that isn't here."

Taavi nods. He is standing at the tram stop on Mustamäe tee with a paper cup of something steaming in each glove, a rucksack on his back that holds the Nightjar laptop, the music box in a bread bag and a grey stick in a kringel napkin, and he has the look of a man who has been given a baby to hold in a railway station. The stop is a glass shelter with one dead advertisement in it and a bench no one has sat on since the Soviet Union. A grandmother is feeding crumbs to a pigeon. A tram drifts by with its bell asleep.

"Bullet points," he says. "One, you are going to talk to her. Two, you are going to be nice."

"I am not going to be nice."

"Two, you are going to be *you*." He gives her one of the cups. It is hot and tastes of cardamom, and she looks at him. "It was the only one they had," he says. "I swear it's a coincidence."

She drinks half and gives it back. In her coat pocket, folded in four, are the six pages of the sale and a seventh sheet, the little log, which she printed at the copy shop on Telliskivi with a clerk who did not look at either. Paper keeps its mouth shut. She does not take her hand off it all the way across the road.

* * *

"Sit, sit," says Helle.

She has opened the door before Ilse knocks. She is in a cardigan the colour of tea, with the orange glasses on their chain and a face from which all the colour has been carefully washed, and she says it again, "Sit, sit," and is already at the stove, filling the pot, setting out cups, taking down a plate and putting on it, in a spiral, kringel. There are a great many. They are glossy and plaited and heaped with almonds, and the whole kitchen smells like a bakery at the end of the world.

The table is long for the room, a pine one with a scarred top that must have come from a house. On the wall behind it hangs a clock in the shape of a cockerel, plastic, with a painted comb and a second hand that goes round with a cheerful tock. It is a quarter to two. The fridge hums. The photograph of Gert, in his gown, leans on it under its strawberry.

Ilse sits where the light is at her right, so that she can hear. Helle pours. Her hand is steady, a thing Ilse notices because it is the only steady thing in her.

"You'll have eaten nothing," says Helle. "You never do, when it's bad. You go thin and angry and write it in a notebook." She sets a cup in front of her, a plate beside it, a plaited pastry on the plate. "My little firewall."

Ilse takes out the pages. She puts them on the table between the cups, face up, and turns them so Helle can read them without having to lean, and lays the sixth on top. She does not say anything. She has learned over a lifetime that there is no sentence as loud as a piece of paper put down in front of someone.

Helle looks at the green loop for a good while.

"Yes," she says.

The clock tocks.

"Yes. That's mine. I've been wondering how long." She takes off the orange glasses and sets them on the paper, over her own name, so that the lenses make two small round windows on the ink. "Do you want to hear it in order? I would like to say it in order. It is the only thing I'm good at."

"And then?"

"Then I'll say it."

* * *

It begins, as everything in the firm begins, with money.

Spring 2017, the second year of a company in a rented loft that leaked, a lease nobody could afford, three engineers owed two months' pay, a mortgage of Helle's own that she had not told them she had put against the firm. Jaan came in from a trip to Vienna with a face like a boy who has stolen jam. He had a contract. He said the buyer was friends, careful men, cable men, and that the thing he had sold was a *demonstration*, a clever noise that could not hurt a fly. He said it in the doorway of her office, with his coat still on. "Sign here, Helle, you're a director, it wants two names." She signed. She did not read the schedule at the end.

"I read my own name three times," she says. "To see that I'd spelled it right."

"You didn't ask."

"I didn't ask. I thought, if Jaan says it's a demonstration, it's a demonstration. That is the whole of my defence. I'm sorry it's so short." She pours more tea than the cup will hold, and mops the saucer with a cloth. "And then the money came, and Gert's school, and the loft was a floor in a spice warehouse. And for two years I woke every morning and thought, *thank God*, and it did not occur to me to ask what to."

"And September."

"September." She sets down the pot. "A man came to the office on the eleventh. Jaan was at the dentist. A pleasant young man, he gave a name, Roos, and a card with a ministry on it that had no number. He knew about the visa already. That was the first thing he said, before he sat. He said he knew Gert's stamp was late, and he said they might help. And he said that I was, as he understood it, a woman who cared very much about her people. And how would I like to help them take care of their people, in a small way? A word now and then. Where Ilse was going. Whom she saw."

"And you said yes."

"I said, why Ilse? I did say that." Helle looks up. Her eyes are small and red behind no glasses. "He said she is the best, and the best are the first to be looked for by the wrong sort of people. He said it would be for her protection. And I thought, well, she is a little reckless. She doesn't say. She goes off in the night with a recorder in a sock. Somebody ought to know where she is."

Ilse says nothing. Her good ear is full of the clock.

"So I told him. Every evening a little text to a number, *home safe, has a cold, working late*. Friday afternoons the longer things, in this chair, over tea. He takes his shoes off. He's very respectful." She laughs, and it comes apart. "I told him you went to the hospital on Monday. I told him you were in the weighing room with the Singapore woman and the Lagos boy with the goat. I told him you'd got hold of an old laptop. I told him on Wednesday morning that you were going to see Jaan in the evening." The cloth stops. "That one I'd do anything not to have done."

"Did you know?"

"No."

"Helle."

"No. Oh, God, Ilse, no." She has put both hands flat on the table, one on each side of the cup, and they are shaking, a shiver that comes up the tendons from the wrist, so that the cup chatters on its saucer and the tea trembles in a ring. She looks at them as though they were someone else's. "They never asked about Jaan. I told you. They said he was safe, and I believed it, because I wanted to, because a person can believe anything for the sake of a stamp. If I had thought for one minute. If I had so much as. I made *soup*."

"And then?"

"And then I was eating it when the telephone rang."

She doesn't weep. She has done that, Ilse can tell, in a bathroom, with the tap running. She sits with her hands shaking on the pine, and there is nothing to do with her but look, and the hateful thing is that Ilse can. She has come up four flights with a cold clean stone in her chest, and she finds that the stone has gone soft at the edges and is something worse. She cannot hate her enough. She has wanted to, since page six. She can feel the shape of the hate she ought to have, like a coat that someone else was measured for, and it does not close across her.

She pushes the plate with the pastry an inch away, and picks up the seventh sheet.

* * *

"This is the log of the update he pushed on Thursday."

"I know it." Helle puts the glasses on, crooked. "I typed the release note."

Ilse's head turns slowly, with the good ear forward. "You typed it."

"The compliance note. Pesa wants one for every release by the retained contractor, a paragraph for their file, in a box on a form. I do them. I did that one on Thursday at nine at night, because Jaan stayed. He walked up and down. He dictated. *Sound pack, three point two point one, comfort tone channel.* I said, what is the comfort tone? He said, it is what the babies hear. I wrote it, and I thought: how sweet."

Ilse unfolds the sheet. Jaan's log is in his lower case, plain as a bus timetable, no capital letters anywhere, nothing like a confession. *3.2.1. halt hook restored (ik 2017). eighth retuned (jt). comfort tone: carrier level raised to audible in the ward. reason: so that somebody hears.*

Nobody says anything for the length of three tocks.

"He wanted it loud," says Ilse.

"I think so."

"It had been a whisper for a hundred and seventeen nights. A thing no one could hear. He turned it up so that a night nurse in Pelgulinna would put down her chart and look at a shelf." She reads it again. "And then he went to the hospital the same day. On Thursday. With a box of kringel. He sat an hour in the parents' lounge, and he gave Pille a card."

"Your card."

"With my number, so she would ring me." Her mouth does not seem to be quite her own. "He turned the volume up, and he made sure that someone would call me."

"He said so." Helle's face has changed again. "That night. He was at the door in his coat, and I said, go home, Jaan, you look like a shirt that's been boiled. And he said, *it's all right, Helle, tonight it sings. They'll call Ilse.* I thought he meant the contract. Pesa. That someone would ring about the new tone, and Ilse would be the one to take it." She pushes her glasses up with a knuckle. "And on Wednesday, he rang me at five from the office and told me to go home early. *Go home, make soup.* I thought it was tenderness. It's the first time in eight years he ever told me to leave before the lights."

Ilse sits still. In a rented loft in the spring of 2017, a man with a bad hip stood in a doorway with his coat on and said *it's only a demo*. In a kitchen in Mustamäe, eight years on, a woman in a tea-coloured cardigan puts the end of the sentence into her hands. He emptied the building. He lit the lamp in the window. He left the door.

"Who is Purchaser One?" says Ilse.

Helle's mouth moves. "A woman came last night."

"Who?"

"At midnight. A woman in a wax coat, with a mop. She said she did the stairs." Helle almost smiles. "She sat where you're sitting. She put a sock on my table. A grey one, half done. She had a finger missing, here." She touches her own little finger at the joint. "She said nothing. She had all night, she said. I talked for two hours, and she took one kringel and left the others. I think she was the kindest person who has ever been in this house in order to frighten me."

"She was from Purchaser Two."

"From One. I think. From the *other* friend." Helle is quite certain. "I'd never have known, but I looked them up in September when Roos mentioned the word, in the way you look up a thing you've signed. The Standing Committee. It's on the web, if you know where to put your finger. Six security chiefs, six countries, one secretary. Latvia. Lithuania. Poland. Sweden. Denmark. Finland. They meet on the first Tuesday, and nothing is ever minuted."

"Six countries."

"Yes."

Ilse puts her hand flat on the sixth page, over the two lines. She does not need the notebook. The pencil is in her coat, snapped, the cedar splinter in the page, SIX COUNTRIES underlined until it broke. The children on the screen. Finland, Latvia, Sweden, Poland, Denmark, Lithuania. The ministers' sons and daughters in their incubators, whose weights and birth dates went out through the speakers into the dark.

They bought the key. They own a copy. Somebody has been using a key that opens their own front doors on their own children, and the people they have sent to find it, a woman in a wax coat and a man with a ring of keys and a polite cough, are not hunting a thief. They are hunting the thing they own. The hunters are customers.

"They don't want it found," she says. "They want it *quiet*."

"That's the word she used," says Helle. "Quiet."

* * *

The cockerel says it is five to three.

It is Helle who sees it, because she has got up to put the kettle back on. She stands at the window with her back to the room and her hand on the tap, looking down five floors at the yard between the blocks, at a cold square of trampled snow and a line of rubbish bins and a child's red sledge, and says, in the voice of a woman reading a bus number:

"That's early."

Ilse goes to the window. A black car has come into the yard, low and polished, with a plate she cannot read from here, and stopped across the exit with its engine running. The doors open together. Two men get out. Long coats, grey, one with a bag and one without. They stand in the snow and look up the face of the block, counting, in the way men count who already know the answer. One of them takes off a glove and puts it in his pocket.

"He's never early," says Helle. "Roos. Four, on the minute. He told me once he is never early because early is a form of rudeness." She turns from the window. The glasses have slipped again. Her face, which has been washed out for an hour, has all its colour back in a rush, and with it something new, calm and flat and almost cheerful, as a person looks at the end of a long and bad week. "That's not Roos. That's not even his sort."

"Is there a back stair?"

"The fire door. At the end of the corridor, behind the mat." The ghost of the old Helle goes across her face. "I told that woman I prop it for the smell."

Ilse gathers the pages. She folds them into four and into her coat, and the seventh sheet last, and she is at the kitchen door with the latch in her hand before she turns. Helle has not moved from the window. She stands with the kettle in one hand and the other on the counter, a woman in a yellow room under a plastic cockerel, with her son smiling at her from the fridge.

"Come with me."

"I can't." It is almost gentle. "If I go, he'll know who was here. If I stay, I'm a woman who baked. Gert has a stamp." She sets the kettle down. "Go on. I'll say you left at half past two. I'll say it so well you'll believe it."

In the yard a car door shuts. Down in the stairwell, five floors below, a heavy street door opens with a groan, and a stair light clicks on. The sound comes up the shaft to the right side of Ilse's head, thin and complete: shoes on concrete, coming up in step.

"Run, my little firewall," says Helle.

She crosses the kitchen to the table in three quick steps and takes the pastry that Ilse left on its plate, the glossy plaited one, heaped with almonds. She looks at it as if it were a thing she had been meaning to try. Then she sits down in the chair that Ilse sat in, at the head of the long table, under the chicken clock, and she takes a bite.
