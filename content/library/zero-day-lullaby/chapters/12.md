# The Music Box

"Bullet points," whispers the dark in Jaan's hallway. "One. I am so sorry."

Ilse has come in by the back, through a garden gate that gave under her glove and a kitchen door that was not locked, and has been standing in the passage for ten seconds with the bag against her chest, listening with her single ear to a house that is listening to her. She has waited in the lane for most of an hour. A patrol car sat at the corner of Kotzebue Street until eight, its exhaust curling, and then drove away with the unhurried air of a shift that has been told to go home. The house is wooden and two storeys high, painted the dark red of old blood and trimmed in mustard, with a porch that has icicles on its fretwork. No light shows in it. Now a head torch swings round the corner of the hall and finds her, and under it is a white face with its mouth open.

"Two," says Taavi. "I know how this looks. Three. It looks exactly like it."

"Put the torch down."

"Down. Yes." He angles it to the floor. He is in his coat, with the mustard gloves on and a woollen hat pulled to his eyebrows, and he is the most frightened thing in the house, including the mice. "I used the spare key. It was in the drawer in the office. Helle labels everything. It said KOTZEBUE, with a little heart. Which, don't."

"You walked out in the middle of the message."

"I heard the message. 'Count the pins.' And I thought, the bread bag. Tuesday. He carried it like a baby." He swallows. "He said ducks. In minus eleven, Ilse. The ducks are frozen into the pond. That's not feeding, that's a tragedy. So I came. I left my phone in the biscuit tin, in your honour."

"I saw."

"I've been here since four." He is whispering faster, a boy emptying his pockets at the school gate. "I've been in every room. I've eaten his pickles, which are mournful. I looked in the wardrobes and the piano and the oven. The oven, Ilse. I looked in the oven twice. And I'm not an idiot, except about the oven."

"Taavi."

"Yes?"

"Has a car been by since eight?"

"One went past at nine and one at ten past. I counted them. They went slowly." He pauses. "That is a bullet point."

There is a clock somewhere, and the house breathes. It smells of old pine, pipe tobacco that has not been lit in years, and pickled things. In the beam of her little steel torch the hall shows itself: coats on pegs, a hat stand, a mirror gone cloudy at the corners, a runner of rag rug, and by the front door a long row of boots on newspaper, in sizes that go down the family: a man's black felt boots, a pair of walking boots with the left heel worn in a slope, women's rubbers, and something small and red.

She looks at the left heel of the walking boots. Jaan's hip made him swing that leg. And beside them stands a felt boot, alone, a little proud of the rest, like a man with something in his pocket.

"I looked in all the boots," says Taavi, behind her, at once. "I put my hand in. I took them out, looked, put them back. It was only boots."

Ilse kneels. In the right boot there is newspaper. In the left, a weight. She puts in her arm to the elbow, and her fingers close on wool, and she draws it out, slowly, like a calving.

It is a vest. A billiard-green one, the twin of the one he died in, and it has been buttoned around something, carefully, in the wrong holes: the second button in the first, the third in the second, the way a child dresses a doll. Underneath the wool is the cool, squared weight of a wooden box.

Taavi makes a sound like a kettle somewhere a long way off.

"You looked in the boots," says Ilse.

"I looked in the boots."

"You must have walked past it forty times."

"Forty-one." He gestures, helpless. "He swaddled it."

She undoes the buttons one by one. They are not easy; he tied it in the dark, a man with enormous hands. The wool falls away, and there, in her palm, is the music box she has watched for eight years on a blotter: walnut, a lid with a lip worn smooth, the steel comb behind the glass. She lifts the lid. Inside, the brass cylinder, the pins in their spiral. She counts. Seven. A stub where the eighth stood, a little bright dent of snapped metal, flush with the surface like a milk tooth that has come out.

She winds the key. It is a stiff, small turn. She sets the box on the rag rug, and the lever slides, and the cylinder begins to turn.

The notes fall in the cold hall one at a time, clear and sweet, with the tiny steel stammer of a comb: the staircase. Up, up, and over, and down. Seven steps. She has known this tune since a hospital corridor at four in the morning. And at the eighth, where the cylinder turns on, where the whole tune leans, nothing. A small blank. The turn of a page.

"That's it?" Taavi whispers. "That's all there is?"

"That's what's missing."

"Is that —"

At the front door, a key goes into the lock.

It makes the small intimate noise of a key that fits. The bolt withdraws. The door opens inward on the porch and the snow and a cold that comes down the hall like a hand, and a man stands there with his own light behind him, so that he is first a shape and then, when Taavi's torch comes up, a face. Neat grey hair. A jacket of soft leather. Black wool gloves with the fingertips cut off, holding a ring of keys. A brass tag among them turns and catches the torch, and Ilse sees three lions on it, passant, worn almost smooth. Jaan has carried it for as long as she has known him.

The smell comes in with him, clean and thin and mechanical: machine oil.

He coughs, politely, behind his fist.

"Good evening," he says. "Please excuse the intrusion. I did knock, in a way." His voice is quiet and pleasant, a little hoarse. "Mind the step."

Ilse's eyes go down. Brown leather boots. A tread worn smooth at the heel and sharp at the toe, a stack of chevrons, V upon V.

"I'd be grateful for the box," says the man. "I'm sorry for the hour. I don't want anyone else hurt."

Ilse closes the lid. The cylinder stops. The silence is very loud, and in it she hears Taavi, to her right, mouthing something that might be a prayer or might be a meme.

"You closed his eyes," she says.

"I did." Something in the pleasant face moves, and is put back. "It seemed right."

He steps in out of the cold, wiping his boots on the mat with care, and extends one cut-fingered hand.

* * *

She does not remember deciding. She remembers the box going into her coat, against her ribs, and the vest in her fist, and the coat rack, a heavy old iron thing, going over in the passage between them with a crash like a hundred spoons. Taavi had thrown it. "Sorry!" he is shouting. "Sorry! Sorry!" And the man says "Oh, dear," quite mildly, and "Pardon me," as he steps over the wreckage, and then they are through the kitchen, past the table with its jar of pickles and the open oven door, and out into the yard.

The cold is a slap. The snow in the yard is up to the shin. Behind them in the house a voice says, with real concern, "Please don't run, the ice is treacherous," and a back door bangs.

The fence is picket, painted white once, a hundred years ago. Ilse takes it in one pull, boot on the rail, over, and lands in a lane behind the woodsheds. She turns. Taavi is on the rail with his legs on both sides and his hat over one eye, and as she watches the whole stretch of fence gives way under him with a long dry sound like a very old man sitting down. He lands among the pickets on his back.

"I'm fine," he says, to the sky. "That's a bullet point."

She hauls him up by the coat. They run.

Kalamaja at night is a maze of narrow lanes between wooden houses, snow packed into hard rails by a hundred winters, lamps at the corners like yellow thumbprints. Their breath goes ahead of them. Somewhere behind a boot comes down on packed snow in a steady, even stride that does not hurry and does not tire. "Excuse me," says the voice. "I'm so sorry." A woman walking a dog has flattened herself against a fence to let it pass. Of course she has. It is the sort of voice you step aside for.

The lane gives onto a street with tram lines, and across the junction on a pole stands a camera with a small red eye. Ilse sees it as she runs and sees, at once, the man behind her see it: there is a half-second in which the stride falters, and the head turns away from the lens and the collar goes up, a man at a funeral avoiding a photographer. Taavi sees it too. He does not slow. He scoops with his mustard glove as he goes, a fistful of snow from the top of a bin, and without breaking his run, without a thought, throws.

It is the best throw of his life. It leaves his hand like a bird and crosses twenty metres of falling snow and hits the lens dead centre with a flat, wet slap. The camera jerks. The red light flares and goes mad, a small shocked eye, and the man behind them takes three strides to the left with his head ducked and his hand raised against the flash.

"Oh my God," says Taavi, delighted. "Did you see that?"

"Run."

A tram is coming. She hears it with her good ear, the long ground-down groan on the rails, then the bell, two quick notes, urgent as a cough. It slides out of the snow with its windows lit like a ship. The stop is thirty metres away. They cross the tracks in front of it, a hand's breadth ahead of the nose. The driver leans on the bell and says something short at them through the glass.

They hit the platform. The doors are opening. Ilse looks back, once.

He is on the kerb now, forty metres off, in the light of the stop, coming on at a quick, neat jog. At the kerb his foot goes out from under him on a skin of black ice. He doesn't fall at once. He goes through a long absurd moment of trying not to, one gloved hand out, and then he sits down hard on the road, and then goes over on his back with his keys flung out in a bright arc. A small voice floats across the street. "Oh. I do apologise."

The doors close.

* * *

The tram smells of wet wool and tea. Sitting at the front, in a plastic cabin of his own, is a ticket inspector in a reflective vest with a thermos flask between his knees; he looks at them, at the snow on their shoulders, at Taavi's hat pulled over one eye and Ilse's breath, and says, "Tickets," without the faintest conviction.

Taavi taps his card on the reader like a man defusing a bomb.

"You look like people who've been running from something," says the inspector, unscrewing his cup.

"The ice," says Taavi.

"It is hard, this winter." He pours something dark. "Sit. It's free until Hobujaama."

They sit. The tram takes a curve. In the rear window the street shrinks, a spill of yellow light, a small black shape standing up at last on the road, carefully, with one hand to the small of its back, and then it is a lamp, and then it is nothing. Taavi's whole body begins to shake. He puts his gloved hands between his knees. "I broke his fence," he says. "Jaan's fence."

"It was already broken."

"He'd have liked that, that I threw a snowball," says Taavi. His teeth are going. "He liked a bad idea. He said it at my interview. Young man, he said, a bad idea is only a good idea in a hurry." Then he stops, and she hears his throat close, and he looks out of the window at the dark going by.

Ilse leans back. Against her ribs, inside her coat, something moves. A quick thin whirr of metal. The lever must have been knocked in the fence, because the box, still wound, has begun to play by itself. It plays into the wool and the lining and her own side, muffled and small, and she sits quite still and listens to it through her body: seven notes, climbing, a staircase in the dark. She takes it out. Taavi sees it and says nothing. On the tram's bright floor, between her knees, the box turns its cylinder, and the seven notes ring out, and slow, and wind down, and stop, and at the place where the eighth should come, there is the blank. The page turned.

She reaches into the comb with her thumbnail. There is a tooth there, steel, bare, a hair longer than its neighbours, that no pin has ever touched. She plucks it.

It rings. It is not the flat note, the one that sags. It is not the true one she wrote in 2017, either, the one she thought she would know in her sleep. It is lower, rounder, and slower to go, a note with a different vowel in it. A note she does not remember.

Jaan retuned it.
