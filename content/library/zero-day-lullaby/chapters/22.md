# A Spiral of Peel

At a quarter past three the Christmas market is taking itself apart. A man in a bobble hat unscrews the plywood roof of a gingerbread house and lays it flat on a pallet. A woman carries a cauldron of mulled wine, still steaming, across the cobbles to a van, one hand under each handle, her face set like a mother's with a sleeping child. The last strings of lights have come down from the stall roofs and lie in coils on the stones, unlit, and the dusk, which in Tallinn in this week of the year starts in the middle of the afternoon, is being drawn over the Town Hall in grey layers, as a tarpaulin is drawn over a boat.

Marit Soini crosses the square by the long side, along the front of the old pharmacy. She does not look for him. In nineteen years of sea rescue she learned to find a person in water by looking at the water: the swell breaks differently where something is. Here it is the pigeons. They stand about the third bench from the well in a close grey crowd, shoulders touching, all facing one way, as pigeons face only one kind of man.

He has put a newspaper down on the slats for her. That is the first thing she sees. Then his hands.

They are broad in the palm and clean and not large, the hands of someone who has spent years holding out seed to things that were afraid of him, and has had the patience to be right. Between the first finger and thumb of the right one he holds a mandarin. The overcoat is charcoal wool, good, not new. Under it, a pale grey cardigan with a darn at the cuff.

"Mrs Soini. Aleksei Novik. Please." His voice is soft and very slightly hoarse, the voice of a man who has never needed to be heard from the back of a room. "The slats are cold, so I have put down a newspaper. It is yesterday's, I am afraid, but the weather has not changed."

She sits. She sits as she has sat on every bench for nineteen years, feet apart, weight forward, as if the bench might move.

"You sit like a person who has worked on boats," he says. "They never trust the furniture."

"Yes."

"Do you like pigeons?"

"They are honest."

"That is what I like about them." He tears the corner off a bread roll in the paper bag on his knee and scatters it, and the whole grey crowd moves as one animal with forty necks. "They have never once asked me what I do. Would you like to throw some?"

"No."

"Quite right. It is the kind of thing one should do with one's own hands." He finds the first edge of the peel with his thumbnail and begins to turn the fruit, slowly, the skin coming away in a single ribbon. "May I tell you what I know about you, to save us both an hour? You may stop me. Nineteen years with the sea rescue at Porkkala. You lost the end of a finger to a mooring line. You like Savo. Eleven years ago, in a dentist's waiting room in Lappeenranta, you read about a farm near Kallavesi. Thirty-one hectares, eleven of them forest. A barn with a roof that does not leak."

Marit watches the peel. It is coming off the fruit in an unbroken spiral, a thin bright rope that turns in his fingers and does not part.

"Who told you?"

"Nobody told me. It is the sort of thing one finds, if one looks at a life for long enough. It is not clever. Mostly people want what they looked at in a waiting room." The ribbon lengthens. "I have a pension fund with a good name and no staff, Mrs Soini. I have a farm, very like yours, presently owned by a company that would be glad to be owned by you. These are small things to me. They are a fraction of what you are owed, by all accounts, for six years. I should like to put both in your name."

"And?"

"And you would bring me the decoder. A laptop, I think, or a cassette, or a woman. I am not particular." Segment by segment, now, he takes the fruit apart. "And the nurse would be left alone."

A pigeon walks across Marit's boot and looks up at her with an orange eye. She does not move her foot. What she is noticing, with the slow attention that a long rescue gives, is the shape of his sentences. They all go up at the end. *Do you like pigeons. May I tell you what I know. And?* A threat is a statement. He has made no statement but the weather. He has put down a newspaper and asked her questions, one after another, politely, and every one of them has a place in it where she could put her hand and be bitten.

"And who would you like to be safe?" says Novik.

It is the same voice in which he asked about pigeons. She counts to eleven, the way you count between waves, and says, "The nurse."

"Of course. Mrs Rebane, who gives soup to people who come into her mother's kitchen. Leonid says it was pea." He puts a segment in his mouth and eats it. "She is safe so long as nobody asks the Committee."

"The Committee has asked."

"On Thursday," he agrees. "*Rebane, Pille. Harju Children's, neonatal, nights. Flat on Paldiski mnt, fourth floor. The nurse. Close her.* Mr Hint underlines his verbs. He thinks nobody has noticed. You did not answer. You burned the card in the washbasin at the Hotel Tuletorn, and in the small hours of Sunday you told Mr Hint that you would bring her in alive on Monday." He glances up at the Town Hall clock. "It is Monday. At six Mr Hint will be at the Katajanokka terminal, in the café where he sat with you, in front of a coffee he will not drink. When you do not come, he will ask the Committee again. And do you know what they will say?"

She understands it then, all at once and from below, the way a swimmer understands a roof of ice. Every envelope Hint ever gave her came with its seal whole. A whole seal is what a well-read letter looks like. She has carried the Committee's orders for six years, and each one was read by this man before it reached her hands.

"No," says Marit.

"Nor do I," says Novik gently. "That is the difference between a man who reads paper and a man who writes it."

He offers her a segment on his open palm. She looks at it. She does not take it.

"Not yet," she says.

"Not yet is a very good answer to a farm." He eats the segment himself. "I shall be here until the pigeons tire. And I should tell you, Mrs Soini, since I am telling you things, that I have no quarrel with paper. The list in the lining of your bag, for instance. Eleven names. Everyone should keep a list. I would only keep it somewhere dry."

Marit stands. The bench gives a small sigh as it loses her weight.

"Thank you for the newspaper."

"It is yesterday's," says Novik, and turns his face up to the last of the light, and takes a second mandarin from his pocket.

She does not hurry. A person who hurries is a person who has been told what to fear. She walks the long side again, past the pharmacy and the shuttered mulled-wine stall, and she does not look back, because she knows what she will see. At the far end of the square, where the last gingerbread stall still has its lamp on, a woman in a man's camel overcoat leans against a pillar with an unlit cigarette between her fingers and a paper cone of gingerbread hearts she has not touched.

Marit rang her yesterday from the lobby of the Tuletorn. *Raud,* said the voice, on the second ring, a voice like a gravel path in the rain, and Marit said, *I have paper,* and the voice said, *Slowly,* and then, when Marit had said it slowly, *Again.* Eleven years of December postcards, each with a pencil boathook on the front, and in all that time they have exchanged perhaps four hundred words.

Anneli Raud looks older than the woman Marit pulled out of the Gulf. Her hair, cut like a boy's, has gone entirely white. She does not say hello. "You look," she says, "like a woman who has eaten something she shouldn't."

Marit takes the tin out of her bag. It is an old tea tin with a stag on the lid, dented at one corner, and it weighs what paper weighs. She slides it into the pocket of the camel coat herself, as she once put a rope into a pair of hands that could not close.

"The Committee's orders. Six years. Hint's hand on every page. This month's are on top. The card with three names: Kantor, Tamm, a nurse."

"And the strip?"

"At the bottom. Eleven names. Under them a twelfth, which is mine. I was not told to write them. I kept them."

"What became of them?"

"It is on the strip. Each one has a date."

Raud's eyes, small and pale and used to bad news, stay on her face. "Eleven years," she says, "I have owed you a drink. I don't think this is the drink."

"No."

"They will find you."

"Yes."

"The sub-editors will weep, and the lawyers will weep." Raud tucks the cone of gingerbread under her arm and closes a hand over the tin through the pocket. "Slowly, Marit. Again. Who is the man on the bench?"

"A man who feeds pigeons."

"I have been to a great many parks," says Raud, and puts the cigarette in her mouth, and goes.

At the next stall a woman in felt mittens is wrapping slippers in tissue, row on row of grey and cream, each pair stitched with a small red heart. Marit buys a pair she does not want, for twenty euros, and asks whether she may use the telephone, because her own is dead, because it is for a daughter. The woman hands it over without a question. Phones leak. Marit knows it as she knows her own pulse, and any wire that listens to a nurse's telephone has been listening all week. She will say nine words, then, and hope that the sea is bigger than the wire.

She does not need to look up the number. She read it once, on the duty rota pinned over the linen shelf at Harju, between a roster of Christmas swaps and a notice about herring, and she has never in her life forgotten a number written beside a name.

It rings four times. "Yes?" says a voice, breathless and wary, the voice of a woman who has learned lately to answer a stranger the way she would lift a lid. "Who is this?"

"The old pipes."

A silence. Then a short delighted intake of breath that travels down a telephone line as plainly as a smile. "I thought so," says Pille Rebane. "I found your boots."

"The Nightboat. Cabin 7118. Bring what you took."

"I've got it in a bag with rusks."

"Good."

She ends the call. She gives the phone back to the woman in the felt mittens, who has wrapped the slippers in a little extra tissue, and Marit takes them, and thanks her in Estonian, the one language in the square she speaks badly.

At the end of the long side she turns, as she has turned at the end of every rescue of her life, to count what she is leaving. The stalls are down. The lights lie in their coils on the stones. The bench by the well is empty, and a pigeon has taken the place where he sat. On the newspaper where Marit sat herself an hour ago, laid out as neatly as a rope on a deck, lies a mandarin peel in a single unbroken spiral.
