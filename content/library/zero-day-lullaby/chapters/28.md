# Cloakroom

On the pavement outside the Merikatu Congress Centre, between a flagpole and a police van, Ilse gives away the only thing she owns that matters.

"Left glove," she says. "Palm."

"I sewed a pocket." Taavi has gone the colour of the snow, and his voice stays level as a plate stays level in a waiter's hand. "At six, in the van, after the ledger. I didn't know what for. I thought a person should have somewhere to put things."

"Nobody looks at gloves."

"Everybody looks at gloves. They think, that boy has a condition." He slides the grey stick into the lining and closes his fist over it. "Bullet point. If I drop it I will lie down in the road."

"Then don't drop it."

Behind them the Gulf lies grey under a low gold sun, a plate of ice with a bright crack across it where the icebreaker has been. The Congress Centre is a long block of glass and pale granite facing the water, with the flags of the Accord in a row along its front and a banner twice the height of a man: OFFICIAL SOUND PARTNER, and under it, in white on blue, the small round shape of a Pesa, smiling as a stone smiles. Ilse looks at it for the length of one breath. Then she pulls down the navy wool hat that Dr Ong bought in the hotel shop for twenty-two euros, over the white streak, to her eyebrows, and goes in.

The volunteer at registration has a cold and a green marker and writes ANNI PÕLD on the blue wristband without looking up. Anni Põld has had a busy week. On Sunday she was a locum in a borrowed gown; today she is technical staff to a delegate from Singapore. "Welcome to Helsinki," says the volunteer, to a point beside her head, and snaps the paper round her wrist.

The security frame is the airport kind. Two guards in black watch the belt, and the coats go through it, and Ilse's goes through in one long dark shape that holds four sheets of Helle's green handwriting, and nobody blinks, because paper is not a thing that frightens a machine. Taavi follows through the frame with his hands held out from his sides, like a man carrying two bowls of soup. It does not beep. Nobody looks at the gloves.

OUTER GARMENTS MUST BE CHECKED, say the signs, in four languages. The cloakroom is a long room beside the main doors with a counter, a brass bell and rails of hangers running back into the dark like the stacks of a library. The queue is a slow river of dark wool. Ministers hand over their fur hats at the counter as they have handed over everything in their lives, without looking where it goes: a Dane with a hat like a sleeping badger, a Pole in an astrakhan collar, a Swede who surrenders his scarf folded into a flag. A student with a rubber stamp gives each of them a brass disc on a loop of string. The hats go on the shelf above the rail in a long soft row, and the room smells of damp wool and aftershave and, from somewhere near the head of the queue, oranges.

She smelled oranges once on a radiator in a hospital. Ilse turns her right ear to the front.

He is at the counter in a charcoal overcoat worn open over a pale grey cardigan, folding a scarf. He does not look round. He takes his time, as the old do in a bank, and when the student reaches for the overcoat he says something that makes the girl laugh. Then he turns, as if he has remembered something, and walks the length of the queue against the current, with people parting round him as water parts round a post. By the time she thinks to move, he is behind her.

"Ms Kantor. Aleksei Novik. Allow me."

The hands are on her shoulders before she can answer, broad in the palm and clean and not large, and they lift the coat away from behind, the way a man helps a woman at a restaurant. For a moment his arms take the weight, and a very small pause goes through them. "Heavy," he says pleasantly. "It is a wet country."

She turns. He is shorter than she expected, soft in the face, the cardigan darned at one cuff. Up close he smells of wool and oranges. "Do I know you?"

"No. But you have been on my desk for a month. I have read your commits. They are very tidy." He carries the coat to the counter himself, and the student, seeing a lanyard that says OBSERVER and a voice that has never been refused, gives him a hanger instead of a disc. "Please. I should like to."

He hangs it with care. Shoulders squared, collar turned, a palm drawn down the sleeve, the way one hangs a thing one expects to want back.

"Did Jaan sleep, at the end?" he asks the coat. "I was told he sat on the stairs. His hip was bad. I hope it was not too uncomfortable."

"He was dead."

"Yes. I meant before. It was a long climb." He lays his hand on the coat's shoulder, as a man pats a horse. "Does the cold trouble the left ear? Or only the noise?"

"Only the noise."

"I thought so." He turns and looks at her, kindly, over the heads of the queue. "Deaf at six, and reading mouths ever since, so that you believe nothing until you have watched it being said. It is why you have been watching my hands since the door." He holds them up, empty. "They are ordinary hands. I feed pigeons."

"And then?" says Ilse.

"Ah. *And then.* The question of an engineer." Novik seems pleased. "Mine is a smaller one. Who would you like to be safe?"

The queue moves around them. A minister in an astrakhan collar lays a hat on the counter and is given a disc.

"Everyone in that hall," says Ilse.

"Everyone is a great many people. I find I can manage about four." He takes a mandarin from the pocket of the cardigan and turns it in his fingers without starting it. "May I tell you three things I know about you, to save us both an hour? You may stop me. The first you have heard. The second is that you count when you are frightened. Your lips have been moving since the pavement. Ninety-four, I think? I should like very much to know what it is for."

She had not known her mouth was moving. She closes it.

"And the third. You have Jaan's last gift on you. In the coat, I should say, beside poor Mrs Kuusk's ledger. A very heavy coat for the weather." He slides a brass disc across the counter. It says 212. "I shall keep it safe. You will want it afterwards."

"Afterwards," says Ilse.

"People generally do."

She does not look at the door. She does not look at the boy beside the fire notice, who is studying it with the whole of his attention, his left fist closed. She picks up the disc, which is warm from the student's hand, and walks out into the lobby, and behind her on her right, small and exact, comes the tearing sound of a thumbnail going in under the skin of a mandarin.

The lobby is marble and glass and full of weather. Along the far wall the press move as a single animal with forty cameras, following a minister who walks and talks and does not stop, and in the eddy beside the doors to Hall A stands a thin man in a grey overcoat the colour of the carpet, with pale scrubbed hands. Ülo Hint owns no telephone. He cannot call a guard, or a colleague, or the Committee. He can only look, and he does: at the white streak escaping the hat, at the scar through her eyebrow. His mouth makes the beginning of her name.

He takes a step. A cameraman walks backwards into him. A woman with a boom swings it across his chest, and a minister arrives in a ring of lights with six aides, and by the time the tide has passed there are thirty bodies between them, and the bodies are press, which he has spent six years keeping out of every room he sat in. He cannot cross it. To shout her name here is to say it into forty microphones.

Ilse lifts two fingers to the brim of the hat, as one greets someone across a field, and goes up the stairs.

Hall A is an oval with a ceiling like an upturned boat, and in the cove light that runs round it, one every four metres, sit sixty small white stones. Each wears its ring: white, steady, idle. She has seen a good many of these in her life, on shelves and cots and in a cell, and never so many in a room so large and so quiet. She cannot hear the room. She stands at the back by the glass of the booth and lays her palm against it, as she once laid it on a cot rail, and a thin steady tremor comes up through the pane that is only the air handling. She keeps her hand there anyway.

The booth is a glass cab, like the cab of a crane, with a desk the length of a sofa, and on a shelf above it a white box the size of a bread bin with one blue light. A man in black is eating a rye sandwich over a keyboard. He is Pekka, of Lumo AV; he has been here since five; and he reads the paper that Ong gives him twice, the way a man reads a parking ticket he is not sure is his.

"Clause fourteen," he says.

"Any connected device in the hall," says Ong. "I intend to inspect sixty."

"My boss says do what the delegates ask until three." He looks at the sandwich. "I am to stay in the booth."

"You are most welcome," says Dr Ong, and sets her glass mug down on his desk, on a coaster, and he is lost.

Taavi sets up the grey Nightjar laptop beside the desk and opens it offline. Ong plugs a stick of logs into its side, and the screen fills with two pale lists and a column of cold numbers. The release has a form, like a tax return: a number, 3.2.2, and a field headed REASON, in which the cursor blinks. Thursday's reason is still there from the old version, in Jaan's flat capitals. SO THAT SOMEBODY HEARS.

"Change one word," says Ilse.

Taavi's gloved fingers do not hesitate over it. SO THAT EVERYBODY HEARS. Under it, line by line, he types the three listeners and their addresses, and the dates, and then three names from the ledger in a column, Hartmann Edge, Larkspur, Pelican, each with the amount that Helle kept, because she is an office manager and kept every transfer.

At half past two the doors at the back of the hall begin to open, and the hall begins to fill. Ministers come first, in dark suits, trailing aides, and sit in the first three rows with their folders squared. Behind them come the delegates in their lanyards, and the interpreters, and a party from a Gulf delegation in white. In the gallery, second row, six men in identical grey take their seats in a line, with one empty chair left at the end. Ilse sees it as she sees everything now, through mouths and shoulders, without a sound. The press pool waits at the doors in a bright restless knot, lenses up, hungry, a flock for the feeding.

Under the gallery, at the aisle seat nearest the side door, a man in a pale cardigan sits with his hands in his lap. He has not looked toward the booth once. In his hands there is a mandarin, and the peel has been started, and a long thin spiral of it hangs down over his knee.

Over the stage door hangs a wall clock with a red second hand. Ilse has timed worse things by worse clocks. She stands behind Taavi's shoulder with three cardamom pods in her fist, uncracked, and watches the red hand go round, and the thin minute hand creep, and Dr Ong's mug does not move, and Pekka's sandwich has stopped halfway to his mouth.

The clock reads 14:58.
