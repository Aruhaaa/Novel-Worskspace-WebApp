# Holding

The blue lasts nine seconds. Then it sinks to a small slow amber blink at the base of the stone, the blink of a creature asleep with one eye open, and the ring goes dark.

Ilse puts her back into the corner of the bunk and turns her good ear to the wall. The cell is now on her dead side, a room of white tile and a basin and a ceiling, and the sounds of the building, a radiator ticking, a drunk two cells down explaining something about a boat, reach her only through the wall, through the bone of her skull, thick and warm as bathwater. If the stone hums, she will not hear it. If she does not hear it, she will not flinch. A woman who flinches has told a listener where her ears are.

She sits like that for an hour. She applies Jaan's rule to everything, the one he taught the firm before the firm had a name: whatever you would not say on a kitchen table, you do not say in a room. She applies it to thoughts as well, which is not required, but a thought kept for long in a room with a stone comes out of the mouth sooner or later, like a hair in the soup. Taavi, the van, the rucksack: not thought. The token: not thought. She thinks about tile. Twice her hand goes to the pocket for the foil twist, and finds the pocket clean, with only the lint in its seams. They have taken the pods. It is odd how much of a hole three small seeds leave. She had meant to crack one when she was sure of the pain, and now she cannot be sure of it.

The ninety-four goes round in her wrists. Thirty-eight times it goes round, and part of a thirty-ninth, and then she loses count to something that is nearly sleep and is not. At the end of the hour the stone is still blinking its one amber eye. Nothing has sung.

When she is as certain as she can be, which is not very, that the stone is only a stone, she puts her forehead against the cold tile and hums the eighth note into the wall.

It is not the tired flat note that sags out of the tune every ninety-four seconds. It is the other, the one on the comb tooth in the music box that Jaan retuned: lower, rounder, a note with its feet on the floor. She plucked it once, on a tram, with the box winding down in her coat, and it lives in her right thumb now, in the small resistance of the tooth, more than it lives in her ear. She hums. It comes out a hair sharp. She tries again, and it is a hair flat. She has one ear to judge by, and for a week she has listened to a bent note, and the true one has been pushed out of her head by the false. The tile takes the sound in and gives nothing back. Overhead the amber blink goes on, steady and untroubled, as if the stone had only heard a woman clear her throat.

A third time. No better. Nothing answers. She had not expected it to. She only wanted to know how far wrong she was, and she knows.

The key turns at ten past one.

"Up," says a voice she knows. "Come." Lepik fills the doorway like a wardrobe, in the ear-flap cap, with two paper cups of coffee in one hand and a packet of biscuits in the other. He looks as though he has been awake since Wednesday. He has. "Not the interview room. The stairs."

The stairs are the fire stairs, between the first floor and the second. There is a window with rain running down it, a landing the size of a table, and a vending machine humming to itself in the corner with a light on its face. Lepik lowers himself to a step and puts his knees up round his ears.

"The only place in the building with no Pesa," he says. "Nobody could find a socket." He pushes a cup at her. "Coffee. From the machine, so it is a disgrace. And a biscuit." He holds up the packet. "From the drawer."

"The one marked Evidence."

"Expired evidence." He eats one. "Your Mrs Kuusk ate three, last time. I counted."

Ilse sits on the step above him and takes the coffee. It is a disgrace. She drinks it anyway.

"I owe you an apology," says Lepik, to the biscuit. "I have not worked out for what. It is a large one, so I am starting small."

"Start with the door."

"The door." He shuts his eyes. "No force. I wrote it in the report on the Thursday. A man opened that door with a key. And then a woman arrives with a laptop, and all I can think is, who had the key? Four key rings. You, Kuusk, the boy, Tamm himself. And I looked at the one who was standing on the landing with a laptop. It is what you do. It is a stupid thing to do and it is what you do."

"It's what I'd have done."

"I had Tamm's bank records this afternoon. From a girl at the bank who did not want her name said, so I shall say only that she wears green. April 2017. One point two million euros, coming in from an account in Vienna. 'Consulting.'" He says the word like a man putting down something hot. "A firm that owed everybody. By June the loft roof was mended, the wages were paid, the tax was clear. By July the money was gone. Where does a man like Tamm get a million from Vienna for consulting?"

Ilse says nothing. The vending machine hums.

"You know," says Lepik.

"Yes."

"You are not going to tell me."

"Not on a stair. Ask me when you have found out who your superintendent is afraid of."

"Afraid of." Lepik takes off the cap and rubs his bald head with the flat of his hand, and puts the cap back. "At six this evening he took me into his office and told me there is a committee on this file and I am not to ask what the committee is. I asked. I have been asking since four. A man who tells you not to ask what a thing is has been told by something that is." He looks at his biscuit. "I am not sure of anyone any more, Ilse. Not my superintendent. Not the prosecutor. A little, not myself. Is that a thing one can say in a police station?"

"Only on a stair."

His phone buzzes against his ribs. It buzzes again, and a third time, like a wasp in a coat. He takes it out and reads the screen and says a word in Estonian she has not heard him use, and then, without a word, he turns the phone round and hands it up to her.

> THE COMMITTEE'S PAPER. By Anneli Raud. Handwritten orders obtained by this newspaper instruct a courier to "close" the witnesses to the so-called Lullaby breach, among them a night nurse at Harju Children's Hospital. The orders are in the hand of Ülo Hint, secretary to the Standing Committee on Lawful Access. A list of eleven earlier names, with dates, was kept by the courier herself, who describes herself as "the last pair of hands."

Below it are photographs of cards. Small upright writing, a thin pencil, the verbs underlined. *Kantor, Ilse. Tamm, Jaan. The night nurse.* Under the second name, a date: the Tuesday. He was found on the Wednesday. Ilse reads it all the way down, and scrolls, and reads it again. At the bottom is the list. Eleven names, a date after each, and after the dates, in a different hand, short words that she does not look at for long.

"Tuesday," says Lepik, who is reading upside down. "Tuesday."

"Who held the pencil on the Wednesday?"

"Nobody in that story says. It would be a very tidy world." His phone begins to ring, and the name on it is SUPERINTENDENT, and he turns it face down on the step and lets it ring itself out. "I think now I go and find out what that does to a prosecutor."

"And then?"

"And then," says Lepik, getting up, a long job, one knee at a time, "there is somebody in Interview Two who says she will speak to nobody unless you are in the room."

Interview Two has a table, four chairs, and a stone in the ceiling with a yellow label stuck across its ring. OUT OF ORDER, in capitals, in Lepik's hand. "Ten past one," he says. "I did it myself."

Helle is sitting with a plastic folder under her palms.

She looks twenty years older. She wears the orange glasses on their beaded chain and the same cardigan as on Friday, slept in, and her hair is grey at the root for the first time that Ilse has ever seen. There is no lipstick. The skin under her eyes is the colour of weak tea. Beside her sits Saar, the lawyer, a thin man in a raincoat still dark with rain, who rises half an inch for Ilse and sits again. Helle does not say *my little firewall.* She does not say *sit, sit.* She looks at Ilse across the table, and goes on looking, and that is all she gives her.

"My client wishes to make a statement under caution," says Saar. "She understands that she may be charged."

"Eight payments," says Helle. Her voice is dry as paper, with nothing sweet left in it. She opens the folder. "Four thousand three hundred Australian dollars each. To the University of Melbourne, for G. Kuusk. Paid from Hartmann Edge Pte, Singapore. Hartmann Edge was funded by Larkspur Holdings, Limassol. Larkspur was funded by Pelican Marine Brokerage, Lagos. When I telephoned Pelican in October and asked for Mr Roos, the Liaison Office answered." She turns the pages with one finger. "I kept every transfer. I am an office manager."

Lepik has taken something out of his coat. It is a black cloth notebook with a red thread marking the place, and he should not have it; it should be in a bag in a drawer. He opens it on the table beside the ledger and puts his thick finger on a line in Ilse's smallest hand. *A. Lagos. Pelican. Hartmann pays.* Then he moves his finger, one inch, to the ledger. Pelican Marine Brokerage. He doesn't say anything. For a good while no one in the room says anything, and the stone with the yellow label goes on not listening.

Helle signs the statement. It takes her three pages. At the end of her name, out of long habit, her pen makes a small loop and a curl, a pastry, a kringel, and Saar stares at it, and Lepik looks carefully at the wall.

At twenty to four he comes back with a form. "Prosecutor Lõhmus rang at ten past three," he says, "which is not a time that people ring with good news. She cannot drop the access charge. It is the law and it is written down. But she will not apply for remand, and she has seen a certain number plate, and she is, I think, making some inquiries of her own." He puts the form on the table. "Conditions. You stay in Estonia. You report here at nine every morning."

"Estonia is big."

"Not that big." He gives her back her belt, and the laces, and the hood-string. He does not give her the notebook, which goes back into his coat, or the pods. "There is a white van across the street with its engine running. For an hour. A skinny boy in yellow gloves. I sent a constable to ask what he was doing. He said he was warming up."

"Warming up."

"I have never seen a van warm up for an hour."

In the lobby the rain is loud on the glass doors. Helle is standing near the vending machine with Saar, her handbag held in front of her in both hands. She has a small package wrapped in greaseproof paper. As Ilse passes she holds it out. She says nothing at all, and Ilse takes it, and their fingers touch, and Helle's are cold. The eyes behind the orange glasses stay on hers, steady and dry and unrepentant. Ilse waits for the word. The word does not come. Then the glass doors open on the rain, and across the street a white van sits in the dark with its engine running, and a pair of mustard gloves rises from the wheel in what may be a wave.

"Bullet points," says Taavi, when she is in. His voice has come back, and it shakes. "One. I am sorry about the phone. Two. I did not use my brain. Three. There are noodles in the back."

He pulls out into the rain. Ilse unwraps the greaseproof paper on her knee under the dashboard light. On top is a kringel. Under it, folded in four, are pages in a small hand, in green ink: the original ledger, every transfer, every date, which the police do not have and the Committee does not know exists. She turns each sheet to the light. There is no word on any of them. There is no sorry anywhere. Helle does not say it, and it is never going to be said.

She eats the kringel, all of it, while Taavi drives. It is cold, and perfect, and tastes of almonds and long fear.
