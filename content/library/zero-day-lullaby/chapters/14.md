# Wrong Passwords

Taavi's den is colder than the street, and the street is minus eleven.

It is the first floor of an old tram-parts works in Telliskivi, over a bicycle shop called Kett, and it has one radiator that is hot on the side nobody sits at. There is a mattress on the boards. There are three monitors on a door laid across two filing cabinets, a rack of ramen cups, a jar of mustard gloves in every state of wear, and on the window, on the inside, a fern of frost. Ilse woke on the mattress at seven with her coat on and the music box under her cheek, and she lay there for a minute looking at the ceiling, which has been painted with a perfect, patient copy of the Mona Lisa's smile by some earlier tenant, and thought: count the pins again.

She counts them now at the table, by the one lamp. Seven, in their spiral, bright as new pennies. Then the bare spot. Then, because Jaan did not say *look*, he said *count*, she turns the box over and looks at the one part of it she has never once thought about.

The base is a brass plate the size of a paperback, held by four screws as small as eyelashes. "Hang on," says Taavi from the beanbag, and comes across the room in two pairs of gloves, one over the other, with a jeweller's screwdriver in his teeth. "If it's booby-trapped," he says round the screwdriver, "I want it noted I volunteered."

"By whom?"

"By the bullet point of my own dignity."

The screws come out one by one into a saucer. The plate lifts. Under it is a hollow in the walnut, lined with a scrap of green wool that she knows, because she has seen it buttoned wrong, and in the hollow a paper napkin folded into a square. A pink bakery stamp, a grease spot the shape of Finland. Ilse unfolds it with the tips of her fingers.

A microSD card, the size of a thumbnail. And a plain grey USB stick with no label on it at all.

Taavi stops breathing. "That's the token."

"It's a token."

"It's the one from his ring. I saw it in the dish on Monday, I swear it's the same grey." He breathes again. "Then what has the locksmith got?"

"Something that looks like it." She turns the stick over. It weighs what it should. It is warm from the lamp, and it feels, absurdly, like a thing that has been waiting to be picked up. "Find me a reader."

* * *

The reader comes from downstairs, in the hands of Madis, who owns Kett and the lease on the whole building and a moustache the colour of tobacco. He is sixty and wears a cap in the house. He has brought a space heater as well, and sets it down in front of the mattress with a grunt of contentment, as if he had delivered a calf.

"Ten years old," he says of the reader. "Honest. Not like the new ones, which are clever." He looks at the card in Ilse's palm and then at the three of them, and with great delicacy at the ceiling, and does not ask. "I'll stay and be useful. I have found, in life, that people in trouble need someone to hold the other end of things."

Ilse boots the Nightjar laptop with its wireless card still in the bag at her flat, so that it can speak to nothing, and slides the card in. The screen asks for a passphrase in a white box on a black ground. Under the box it says, in Jaan's flat, unhurried lower-case, *you have ten tries, and then I am sorry*.

"Ten," says Taavi.

"I read it."

"It wipes." He is already at the whiteboard, a cracked square of melamine propped on the sill, and he draws a grid with a marker that is dying on him. "Okay. Okay. Bullet points. We are not going to be clever. We are going to be *organised*." Across the top he writes names, and then, after thought, a column headed ILSE and another headed DOG.

"Why is there a dog?"

"There's always a dog. It's in the genre."

She types *Tiiu*. Jaan's wife, dead nine winters. The box shivers and says, *no*, and Taavi puts a stroke in her column. She types *Kotzebue*, then *Kotzebue 14*. No, and no. She types the date the firm was founded, with the dots and without. No. She types *Tamm*, which is cruel, and receives a dry little *no* that sounds like the voice of the man himself. Madis, who is perched on an upturned crate with his hands on his knees, says, "Try 'password'."

"He was a security man."

"Those are the worst. They think they're being clever." He nods at the screen. "My brother-in-law's was 'Hunter2'."

Taavi takes the keyboard. His gloved fingers hover. "Okay, I'm going to go with the swearword. Everyone has a swearword." He types *pagan*, which is as bad as Jaan's language ever got. *No.* He writes 5 on the board, ringed, in red. The ten are going down like a tide. "I want it noted that I don't like this part."

"Pontu," says Ilse.

It comes out of her before she has decided. Jaan's dachshund, fat as a draught excluder, dead since April, who used to sleep under the desk on Jaan's feet with his chin on the toe of the boot. She had forgotten him for four days. She types it with two fingers and her whole face hurts.

The box does not say *no*.

The black ground goes grey and a folder opens. In it is one file, a photograph. A fat brown dog asleep on a green wool vest, buttoned wrong. Under it a text file, three short sentences.

*Good guess, Ilse. Everybody guesses the dog. It was a good dog. Try again.*

Taavi makes a small broken noise, half a laugh. He looks at the board a long moment, writes 1 under DOG, and then, with a face like a man giving a eulogy, writes 0 under ILSE.

"That's not fair. I got him."

"You got a decoy," says Taavi. "Jaan got *you*."

Madis blows his nose, thoroughly, on a handkerchief of great size. "I liked that dog," he says.

The box in the corner has not closed. It is still waiting, white on black, a fresh bar for the second door. It has six tries left. Ilse sits with her hands on her knees and cannot think of a single word. Taavi, who thinks in bullet points, takes the marker, caps it, and says, slowly, as if it were coming up from somewhere:

"He gave you a hint."

"Did he?"

"He put a *dog* behind the lock. Jaan. He was never a man who'd use a name." The marker is turning in his glove. "What did he say all the time? The thing he'd say, the thing he'd get wrong?"

She has the answer before the sentence ends. She has had it since the first week she met him. She is going to be sick with the force of how much she can hear it. She types the correct one first, out of a kind of loyalty, *a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush*, and the box says *no* in the voice of a man gently correcting a pupil, and she puts down five in her head. Then she types, with her eyes shut:

*a lamp in the hand is worth two in the bush*

The grey goes away. A folder with four files. Madis, standing, takes off his cap and puts it back on, and says, "I'll go and be a bicycle," and leaves on his creaking stairs, and shuts the door behind him with great tact.

Taavi writes 1 under PROVERB. His hand is not quite steady. He stands looking at the three words on the board, ILSE 0, DOG 1, PROVERB 1, as though it were the final score of a match in which someone he loved had played.

* * *

There are four files. A folder of scans, six pages. A text called READ ME AFTER. A short log, which she does not open. And an audio file, ten minutes forty, named only *Ilse*.

She plugs the reader's output to the laptop's wired headphones, and then, after a moment, pulls out the jack, and lets it play aloud into the cold room. Taavi sits on the floor. The heater ticks.

A chair takes weight. A cough, a long breath out through a big nose. A radiator knocks in a room she knows. Then his voice, nearer than the answering machine's, close to the microphone, so near that she can hear his lips part.

"Ilse. I sold it."

Her whole spine goes still.

She has heard that sentence before. She has heard it without its meaning and without its words, the way you hear a neighbour's radio, for six years. In the old goods lift at Pikk Street, the iron cage that takes forty seconds from the street to the third floor and which Jaan took because of the hip, she has stood beside him a thousand times, with the gate rattling and the cables groaning, and in the gap between the second floor and the third he would turn his big face to the wall and move his lips. A mutter. A shape, the rhythm of a short sentence, said and said again. She took it for a man running through a joke. She took it for prayer. Once she asked him, and he said, *I am practising being a person who can say a thing*, and she laughed.

He has been practising it since 2019.

"I sold it," says the voice. "Nightjar. In Vienna, in the spring of 2017, in a coffee house with a ceiling like a wedding cake. A man named Fabian Zeller, who sells things to people who need not be named, and who is the only man I ever met who wore a waistcoat with no irony whatever. I took the money. It paid for our first three years, and Helle's accounts thank you for it, and so did your salary."

Taavi puts his glove against his mouth.

"He said they were friends of the Gulf. Cable people. Men who watched the sea floor for boats that should not be there, and needed a key that would let them see." A pause. A cup is lifted and put down. "I wanted to believe it, and a man who wants to believe a thing has already paid for it. Don't say it was only a demo, Ilse. I said that. I said *it's only a demo*, and you were twenty-eight, and I was the boss, and you believed me because I was kind. That is not your sin. It is the one I'll keep."

She does not move. Her right ear is full of him, her left of the room, with its frost and its heater and its dead silence.

"Three weeks ago I found my work humming under the floor of a children's ward. I won't tell you how, it is on kaka-paper somewhere and I have lost the paper. I could not go to the police; I know which police. I could not say it in the office. Somebody in my house has been telling somebody where I stand. I know it as you know a draught in a room. I do not know who. I have a guess, and I am a coward about guesses."

On the floor Taavi has gone very small.

"The stick on my ring is from a petrol station in Rakvere. It holds a picture of a cat. Let them have it; I hope the cat is a comfort. The real one is in the napkin with the card, and it is the best thing I ever did and the worst: Pesa never revoked the retained token, because nobody at Pesa remembers that we are retained. It signs one firmware, once. Spend it on a kill by itself and every speaker forgets, and with the speakers forgets the proof, and you are a woman who wrecked a children's ward for something nobody can show. So I did not spend it. I am an old man with a bad hip and a worse conscience, and I did not spend it, because the evidence is the only thing the dead can leave."

A long exhale.

"On Thursday I did something smaller, under my own name. An update. It looks like a sound pack. It carries your halt, Ilse. *Halt if true eighth heard: demo safety.* Fourteenth of March, 2017, in your hand, I found it in the Kõla patch where you left it and I did not delete it. For once in my life it was wisdom. I have retuned the eighth. The note you wrote is on a list in a dozen offices by now. Mine is not. It is in the bare tooth of the box, where the last pin should be. When you do send a kill, the speakers will play the tune up to the place where the eighth belongs, and then they will wait. A breath, no more. Sing it into that breath, or pluck it, and every one of them will hear it and stop. Without that, a kill is only noise. With it, it is a lullaby with an ending."

The chair creaks. When the voice comes back it is lower, and it is Jaan at his dullest and most precise, which is how she knows he is frightened.

"There is more in Thursday's update than the halt. It is in the log. You will be angry. I would be. Read it when you have heard the rest. The rest is the paper, six pages, scanned on the copier at the library that eats coins. I have not said the other name that is on page six. The page can say it better than me. I'm tired of being the one who says things." A breath. "There. That is the whole of it, and I did not say it well. The box will play you out. Count the pins."

The recording ends with a click and a long, soft hiss, as of a man deciding not to switch off.

* * *

Neither of them says anything for a time. The heater ticks. Out in the yard of the works somebody drags a bicycle across cobbles with a long iron complaint.

"Okay," says Taavi finally, in the smallest voice she has ever heard from him. "Bullet points. He didn't push the kill. Because it signs once. And the proof goes with it." His gloves are knotted together. "And he put a stop in. You wrote the stop, and he moved it. And he didn't tell anyone, because somebody was *listening*." He doesn't say who. He looks at the floor. "Ilse, I need to say. It wasn't me."

"I know."

"You don't. You didn't, on Tuesday. You looked at me."

"I looked at everyone," she says. "That's different. Open the scans."

They open on a six-page document, slightly crooked, with the grey shadow of the copier's lid across one corner. A German heading. *Kaufvertrag*. A contract, English clauses under it, in a lawyer's clean small type. Seller: Tamm Security OÜ, Tallinn. Intermediary: F. Zeller, Vienna. She reads down the clauses with her lips moving, *source, documentation, exclusivity, non-disclosure*, to the schedule at the end. Two lines, side by side.

*Purchaser One: the Standing Committee on Lawful Access, Secretariat.*

*Purchaser Two: the Directorate, Liaison Office.*

"Never heard of either," says Taavi.

"No."

"The first one sounds like a book club."

"Yes." She turns the page.

Page six is the signature page, ruled in boxes. Fabian Zeller has signed with a long flourishing Z like a scorpion's tail. Under him, in blue, in a heavy hand that she could pick out of ten thousand because it signed her cheques for six years, runs the name *J. Tamm*. And below it, in a smaller box marked *Co-signatory, Director*, in a loop of green ink that wheels and doubles back on itself like a girl's, is the other.

She knows the green. She knows the pen. She has seen that capital H sign eight birthday cards, one for each year, with a small drawing of a kringel under it.

*H. Kuusk.*
