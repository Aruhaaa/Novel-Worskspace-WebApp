# The Stairs on Pikk Street

The street door on Pikk Street is not locked.

Ilse has the key out already, a long brass tooth she has carried on the firm's ring for eight years, and the door swings inward under her glove before she can use it. She stands holding the key out in front of her like a woman offering a coin to a gate. Her watch says ten to eight. It is the only thing she is carrying that does not know anything else.

She has spent the evening in a café on Vene Street: three hours behind one coffee and a plate she paid for and did not eat, the laptop bag between her boots and her back to the wall, while a waitress with a stud in her nose asked her twice if she was waiting for someone. She was. For the building to empty. From the corner of Pikk Street she had seen the third-floor window glowing the amber of Jaan's lamp, just as it should, and she had come the last two hundred metres slowly, for no reason she could have put in a ticket.

The hall inside is black. By the door is a push-button on a timer that gives a minute of light to anyone who wants it. She presses it. It clicks like a knuckle, and nothing happens.

From the side pocket of her bag she takes the little steel torch that lives there with the pencil and the pods. The beam finds the stairs, worn in the middle by a hundred and forty years of sacks until each tread is scooped like a spoon. On the first landing she lifts the beam to the ceiling and finds the sockets bare. Someone has unscrewed the bulbs. They stand on the window ledge in a row, set down neatly, like eggs.

She goes on up, turning her head as she climbs so that her good ear points up the stairwell, the way a plant turns to a window. A radiator ticks. Old timber creaks as it gives up the day's warmth. A gull complains on the roof. On her left side everything is the same distance away. It is the oldest trouble she has: she cannot tell where a sound comes from, only that it does.

On the second landing the smell comes out to meet her. The importers who rent the floor sell tea and spice, they shut at five, and there is a crate of cardamom from Guatemala sweating through their floorboards. It is green and cool and exact. For a moment she is standing in her own pocket. She does not reach for a pod.

The third landing is the last. She lifts the torch and the beam finds two brown boots, soles toward her, toes up.

"Jaan?"

She says it the way you say it to a man dozing in a chair.

He sits against the wall below the red fire extinguisher with his legs straight out and his hands open on his thighs, palms up, as if waiting to be handed something. The billiard-green vest is buttoned wrong: second button in the first hole. His eyes are shut. His white hair stands off his head in the beam like a dandelion that has been told to be calm. Somebody has been tidy with him.

She kneels. She pulls off her right glove with her teeth and puts two fingers under his jaw, where the big pulse lives. His skin is not yet cold. That is a thing she would rather not know.

It is a long minute. She has no watch she can read in the dark, so she does what she learned at six in a hospital bed, when a nurse put a hand on her back and said, count it: she keeps time with her own blood. Her pulse is fast. She knows its number. Ninety-four to the minute, the number of a woman who is frightened, and she lets it count. Under her fingers there is only the warmth of him. At ninety-four she takes them away.

Below his left ear is a bruise no bigger than a stamp, the colour of a plum. No blood. No cut. He might be asleep, if sleeping men were that colour.

"Jaan," she says again, in the voice for people who cannot answer. She wants to fix his buttons. Her hand is already on the way to it. She makes it go back to her knee.

In the breast pocket of the vest is the stub of a carpenter's pencil, whittled with a knife. She does not take it. On his belt loop a steel clip hangs open with nothing on it, and she stares at that for some seconds before she understands: his key ring is gone. The stair key, the brass tag with Tallinn's three lions, the plain grey stick on its loop. She does not know whether he still hung the stick there. She has no way to ask him.

She stays crouched, her right ear turned to the stairwell above, and listens as hard as one ear can. The building is large and quiet and she has half of it.

* * *

The office door stands wide. The lamp on Jaan's desk is lit, left on as for a visitor, and the room is so neat she almost laughs. Helle's tins are in a row on the desks with their lids fitted. Taavi's beanbag has been plumped. The chair in Jaan's room is pushed in square to the desk, and the left-hand drawer is open a hand's width, nothing in it disturbed that she can see.

On the blotter is a clean rectangle in two years of dust, the size of a walnut box.

She looks at it. She thinks of Tuesday, and a man going down these stairs with a paper bread bag held in both arms like something that might wake, and then the thought has no floor, and she lets it go. In front of her on the desk is a beige telephone older than Taavi.

She lifts the receiver. A dial tone, flat and clear. She dials with her glove back on.

"Emergency."

"There is a man dead at the old spice warehouse on Pikk Street. Third floor, the landing. My name is Ilse Kantor."

"Is he breathing?"

"No. I checked for a minute."

"Are you in danger?"

Ilse looks at the open door, and the black beyond it. "I don't know."

"Stay on the line. A unit is on its way."

"I'll be on the landing," Ilse says, and puts the receiver down. Then she picks it up again and wipes it with her sleeve, earpiece, handle, mouthpiece, in the long steady strokes of a woman polishing a spoon. She knows as she does it that it will be the first thing she is asked about. She does it anyway, and puts it back.

She goes out to the landing and stands with her back to the wall, so that nothing can be behind her, and sweeps the torch low across the old green lino between the stairhead and the office door.

The print is at the threshold. A boot, half melted, wet as a tongue, the tread a stack of chevrons climbing the sole, V upon V. She holds the beam low to read it. She lays her own boot beside it, not touching. Hers is a forty-one. This is longer by three sizes, a forty-four, maybe, and it is pointing at the stairs, at the way out. It is the only print on the whole floor.

Ilse looks at it for a time that she would not be able to defend.

A man who unscrews every bulb on four floors and sets them out like eggs does not forget to lock a door. A man who closes a dead man's eyes and straightens his legs does not leave one print in the one place a torch will find it by accident. The door downstairs was left on the latch the way a door is left for a guest. *Tonight*, Jaan had written on the glass, and she had thought it was a thing only the two of them knew. Somebody else had known that the person coming up these stairs in the dark tonight would be her.

* * *

They come up loudly, the way people come who have nothing to hide and a great deal to carry: two constables young enough to still be embarrassed by their own radios, snow melting on their shoulders, torches in her face. "Down," she says, "please," and they lower them. Behind them on the stairs something heavy is breathing and not pretending otherwise. It stops at the second landing for a long moment and says a word to the smell of cardamom.

Inspector Kristjan Lepik comes onto the third landing like a wardrobe being delivered. He is the biggest tired man she has ever seen: a long loose wool coat, a cap with the ear flaps tied up on top, a face that lives somewhere between a smile and a sigh. He looks at Jaan, and for a while he does not look at anything else.

"Tamm," he says.

"Jaan Tamm. He owns the firm. Owned."

"Owned." He lowers himself, with a groan, to one knee. His hand does not touch anything. "Not breathing."

"No. I checked. For a minute."

"A whole minute. Most people give up at ten seconds." He gets up again, by stages. "You are the one who telephoned."

"Ilse Kantor."

"Kantor." He tries it for size. "What is in the bag, Miss Kantor?"

"A laptop."

"Why do you carry a laptop to a murder?"

"I didn't know it was a murder."

"No," says Lepik, quite kindly. "Most people don't."

He does not ask her to move. He walks the landing instead, slowly, with his hands behind him like a man in a gallery, and the constables hover at his elbows. He bends over the vest and straightens. He looks at the gap where the bulbs are not. "Cardamom," he says to the air.

"The importers on the floor below."

"Of course. The importers." He sniffs again, as if the smell might say more. "And you waited for the office to empty, you say."

"I didn't say. But yes."

"You say it now. Why?"

"He asked me to come tonight."

"Asked." His eyes come round to her, mild and steady, like a lamp on a ship that has already seen you. "When?"

"This morning. In writing."

"In writing."

"On paper, held against a glass wall. Through a glass wall."

"And the paper is where?"

"He took it."

"Convenient," says Lepik.

"Yes," says Ilse. "For somebody."

The inspector looks at her ear, the turned head, the white streak at her temple. She waits for him to ask. He does not. He looks down at the floor instead, where the beam of her torch has been pointing for a minute and a half, like a finger.

"There's a boot print at the office door," she says. "Wet. A forty-four, I think. Chevron tread. Going out."

"You measure boots."

"I measure everything."

"Do you." He steps over and looks. He is silent for as long as it takes a kettle to think about boiling. Then, to a constable, without raising his voice: "Photographs, please, before anyone breathes on it." And to her, more gently, like a man putting a coat round someone: "The telephone in his office. Did you touch it?"

"I used it."

"And then?"

"I wiped it."

"Why?"

"I don't know," Ilse says, and it is the truest thing she has said all day.

"No," he says. "Nobody ever does." He is looking at the laptop bag, and then at her, and then at Jaan again, who goes on sitting, patient, with his hands held out. "Tell me one more thing and then I shall let you sit down. This man had a cup of tea and a lamp and an appointment. He knew you were coming?"

The building is very quiet. Somewhere under her the street door, still on the latch, has begun to knock softly in the draught.

"Yes," says Ilse, and listens to the word go down the stairs without her.
