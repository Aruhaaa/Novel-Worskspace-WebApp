# Ice

The ship slows at ten past midnight, and Marit knows it in her feet before the engines change their note. A long even shudder comes up through the deck, the sound of something heavy being asked to lean on something harder, and out beyond the steel the sea stops being water.

She has sat in 7118 for five nights with a kettle, a sock and a number of things she has not let herself think about. Now she lays the needles down and stands as she has stood on every deck she ever worked, knees soft, weight low. Grey ice. Young, two hands thick, the colour of a dead television. She could draw the plate edges from the sound.

"It is the ice," she says. "It will be loud. It is only loud."

The nurse sits on the opposite bunk with the baby bag between her feet and the reindeer hat at last off, her hair standing up in a grey-brown halo, a pencil through the bun like a flag of truce. She has stopped humming. She has stopped doing everything else too, which is worse. In two hours she has not made one joke, and Marit has seen enough of the sea to know what it means when the talkers go quiet.

"He will come here," Marit says. "Not yet. When the corridors are asleep and the ice is loud. A locksmith likes noise. It does his listening."

"Then we go."

"You go. I stay. He must find somebody."

Pille looks at the left hand lying flat on the blanket. "That is the worst idea I have ever heard."

"Yes."

Marit has always known where the lifejackets are, on every vessel she has boarded, as other women know the exits of a cinema. On this deck they are aft of the stairs, behind a steel door with LIFEJACKETS in four languages and a spring catch worn smooth by thirty years of thumbs. The room inside is a wardrobe that someone has walked into. Orange jackets on shelves to the ceiling. A coil of line, a boathook, a green battery lamp in a wire cage, a smell of rubber and old rope and cold. At the back, on a rail, hang the children's jackets, small and orange and in a row, and behind them is a space the width of a woman.

Pille looks at them. "Like a nursery," she whispers.

"Sit. Whatever you hear, do not speak."

"I've kept my mouth shut for worse."

"No," says Marit, kindly. "You have not." She takes the swaddled stone from the bag, still faintly warm, and puts it in the nurse's lap. "Hold it quiet. If it sings, it sings to him."

The door will not latch. She pulls it to, and it settles a finger's width open, and swings back as the ship leans, and settles again. She leaves it so. A door that has been shut is a door that has something behind it.

In 7118 the ice reaches the hull. The first scrape comes up from below the waterline, long and rising, a sound like a man shouting one word through a wall, and then another, and the whole ship takes on a slow roll, a lean and a fall, a lean and a fall. Marit sits on the lower bunk with the half-made sock and works the toe. Inside the toe, where nobody will look who has not unpicked a sock, lies a card the size of a stamp. It came on a saucer on Monday morning, under a bellboy's thumb, and she has not yet decided what it is for.

At ten to one there are three knocks, evenly spaced, the knock of a man who has knocked on a great many doors and been let in by most of them.

He stands in the corridor in the toast-coloured jacket, a canvas roll under one arm, his hat in the other hand. His grey hair is damp. Under the strip lights he looks tired in the way of people who are very good at something they have begun to dislike. "Mrs Soini. I am sorry for the hour."

"No."

"No," he agrees. "I am sorry for what comes after it, which is a different thing." He looks down. Cabin doors on this class of ship stand eight centimetres proud of the deck, a steel sill against the sea. "Mind the step," says Sarapuu, softly, to himself as much as to her, and steps over.

She takes the glove from her coat pocket and sets it on the shelf by the kettle. "You dropped this."

He looks at it for some time. The thumb is darned in grey wool, small even stitches, a woman's careful work. "Aili," he says. "She says I am hard on a thumb." He puts it on, and it fits as a hand fits a hand. "You might have thrown it into the Gulf."

"I thought I might need to find you."

"And did you?"

"No. You found me."

He unrolls the canvas on the lower bunk, beside the sock, and lays his tools in a row on the blanket: a tension wrench, two picks, a flat pry, wire cutters, a torch, a short thin knife with a hooked blade for trimming weatherstrip. He squares them to the edge of the blanket with one finger. It is how a surgeon says he is sorry, in advance, to the table.

"I have been asked three things," he says. "Where is the nurse. Where is the stone she carries. And at what hour the Committee has called the vote. Today, I should say. It is today now."

"I do not know the hour."

"The Colonel says you do. He says Mr Hint is a man who writes things down and forgets he has been seen writing."

"Not yet," says Marit.

"Ah." Sarapuu nods, as at a recipe he has met before. "He said that is what you would say. He said it is a very good answer to a farm." His eyes pass over the sock and do not stop. "He asked me to tell you the farm still stands."

So the wire was not smaller than the sea. She gave a cabin number to a stallholder's telephone, nine words, and it has come down the Gulf after her in a toast-coloured jacket. She notes it as she would note a leak: a thing to be bailed, not wept over.

Sarapuu bends to the sill. When he straightens there is something pale between his finger and thumb. He turns it to the light, and smells it, and she knows before he says it.

"A rusk," he says. "For babies. Mrs Rebane carries three packets. I think she carries them as other people carry a medal." He lays the crumb on the blanket beside the knife. "They go out of your door, Mrs Soini, and along the corridor, one at a time, to a door with a lifejacket on it. I have not opened it. I wished to ask first."

Nineteen years of looking at water, and she has never once looked at what falls from a woman's bag.

"You were sea rescue," he says. "A frightened person on a pier: you put them where there is something orange. I would have done the same." He is rolling the tools back into the canvas, quickly, in reverse order, all but the thin knife, which he does not roll. She watches it go up his sleeve. "I am sorry. Truly. Please stand aside."

"Leonid."

It is the first time she has used his name, and it stops him with the roll in one hand. "She is a nurse," says Marit. "She has a stone and a tin of kringel, and she gave you soup. Go and sit in your cabin."

"I would like to," says Sarapuu. "I would like that very much."

The corridor is empty and lit like a dentist's. He walks to the lifejacket door without hurrying, and she walks beside him, a little behind, on his left. At the door he puts out his hand and she takes the wrist, thumb on the pulse, as she did in the stairwell at Harju with a sock needle. This time there is no needle. There is only the wrist, and under it a locksmith's tendons, and she feels him decide, a small flat thing, like a latch dropping.

They go in together. It is not a fight as films make fights; they are not people who would shout. It is quiet and close and ugly, two bodies in a room the size of a wardrobe, with the walls full of orange. The first lurch of the ice takes them both against the shelves and a soft absurd rain of lifejackets comes down, their little whistles on cords. The green lamp swings in its cage. The door swings with it, and bangs, and settles, and bangs. He is strong in the hands, as a man is who has spent thirty years persuading steel, and she is strong in the back, as the sea made her. She sees the thin blade come out of his sleeve and sees that he holds it low and away from her, like a man opening a parcel. He does not want her. He wants the rail at the back, and its small orange people.

She puts her left hand round the blade.

It is a neat cut, almost a letter opened; she feels it as cold across the palm. The ship leans, a long groaning lean, and they go with it, and she throws out her hand to catch the frame, and the door comes back on its hinge with the whole weight of the Gulf in it. There is a small plain sound, like a latch.

Marit does not look. She holds his wrist with her right hand, which has known worse, and kicks his legs from under him on the pitch of the deck, and he goes down among the orange. His head touches the edge of a shelf. He sits, winded, with blood at his hairline and the knife on the floor between them.

"Oh," says Sarapuu. "Oh, I am so sorry. Your hand."

Behind the row of small orange jackets there is not a sound. Not a breath, not the crackle of a rusk packet, not the shift of a knee. Marit has listened for the living in the dark for nineteen years, and she knows how a person sounds who is trying not to be one. The sound of it fills the room.

The ice goes on shouting along the hull. How long, she could not say. When the engines come astern and the whole ship shakes, Helsinki arrives in the porthole of the stairwell, low and orange.

Sarapuu has got to his knees. He does not try for the knife. He gathers the other tools instead, the ones that spilled, the wrench, the picks, the torch, and sets them on the lid of a crate in a line, squared to its edge. The thin knife he picks up last, by the blade, and lays at the end of the row.

"That one I used," he says. "I should like there to be no argument about the others." He sits down on the crate beside them with his hands on his knees, a cut on his scalp, an expression of great relief. "I think I shall be arrested, Mrs Soini. I have been in a great many kitchens this winter, and I find I would prefer a cell. It is the only quiet room left."

She leaves him there and goes to find the night mate.

He is a boy of twenty-five with a spotty chin and a torch, and he goes the colour of milk at the sight of her hand. She tells him in Finnish, the language in which she has never been polite. A man in the lifejacket store. A knife. He is not to be hurt; he is waiting. A passenger who must get off first. The boy, to his credit, does not argue. His radio spits.

At half past one the Nightboat noses against the quay at Katajanokka under the sodium lamps, and the gangway comes down, and the Finnish police come up it: two constables in black, and a border guard in green with a face like an unmade bed. They find a man seated on a crate with a cut scalp and his hat on his knee, and in front of him, laid along the lid in order of size, a wrench, two picks, a pry, the cutters, a torch and a short thin knife with a hooked blade. He rises as they come in, to exactly the height at which rising is polite.

"Good evening," says Sarapuu, in English. "I wish to make a statement. The knife at the end is the one. Forgive the disorder. There was a ship."

Marit parts the row of small jackets. The nurse is sitting on the floor with the bag at her knee and the stone in her arms, and her lips are white from being held shut. She looks at Marit's hand and at once the nurse arrives in her face. "Give me that." The scarf is off the bag and round the palm in five turns, tight, and she lifts the hand above Marit's heart and holds it there with both of hers. She does not say a word about what is missing from the little finger. Her own hands are quite steady. "Pressure, and up. Don't look. That wants a surgeon inside the hour."

"There is an ambulance."

"Good."

Marit takes from her pocket a slip of paper, folded once, and puts it in the nurse's hand. A number, in her upright pencil. "A woman will answer. She will say, Slowly. You say it again. She will find the tall one."

"Slowly," says Pille. "Again?"

"Yes."

"And you?"

"A hospital. I will be quite well."

Pille lays her free hand over Marit's right one, on the short finger, and says nothing, which for her is a speech.

Then she goes down the gangway with the other passengers, in her reindeer ears, the duck bag on her arm, through the cold orange light and past the constables, who have a man to look after and do not look at a woman in a funny hat. Marit watches from the rail. She watches the ears go under the first lamp and come out the other side, and the bag swing, and a tin of kringel catch the light like a small moon. The nurse steps from the end of the gangway on to the quay of a country she has never visited, a stone in a nest of rusks against her chest, and goes on walking, and does not turn her head.

Marit stands under the quay lamp with the bandage already red through. She holds her left hand up beside her right and looks at the pair of them. Nine and a half fingers now, she makes it. She was never any good at fractions, only at people.
