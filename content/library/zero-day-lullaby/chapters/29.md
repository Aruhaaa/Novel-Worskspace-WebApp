# The Whole Tune

At three o'clock exactly the chair taps the microphone, and Ilse, who cannot hear it, sees the whole hall turn its head to the right.

That is how she has learned to hear rooms: by what the heads do. From the glass cab at the back, the oval below is a field of dark shoulders and bright paper, with the photographers crouched in the aisles like men at a river, and the ministers in the first three rows leaning toward their aides. The six grey men in the gallery sit in a line. The seventh chair at the end is still empty. The aisle seat by the side door holds a cardigan and a long unbroken ribbon of peel.

"Doors in five minutes," says Dr Ong, who is reading the red hand on the wall.

Taavi has taken the grey stick from his left glove. His hand shakes so that the gold tongue chatters against the port, and he says "Sorry, sorry" to the laptop, and puts his other hand over the first, and it goes home.

On the screen a plain grey window opens, in a typeface from 2017. RELEASE 3.2.2. SIGN WITH EMERGENCY TOKEN. THIS KEY WILL BE SPENT. Below it, one button, and a line of small print. She reads the small print because she is Ilse. *One signature. No recall.*

Jaan's thumb has been on this button. His thumb has done nothing since the Wednesday. She puts her own over it.

"And then?" says Taavi, very quietly.

She presses.

The token's light goes from green to amber to nothing. A file appears, 3.2.2.SIGNED. Taavi copies it to a plain stick in two clicks and carries it the length of the booth, ten steps, held out on his open palm like an egg on a spoon, and pushes it into the white box. The blue light turns white. A line crosses the monitor on the desk.

> Release 3.2.2 received. Verifying signature.

Pekka stops, with the sandwich halfway. "Is that supposed to be on my screen?"

"Yes," says Ong.

"I have no release scheduled."

"No," says Dr Ong. "You have a delegate."

> Signature valid. Updating 60 of 60 units. 2%.

"What does it do?" says Pekka, to nobody, in the voice of a man who has stopped wanting to know.

"It tells sixty small stones to sing," says Ong.

"Sing."

"In tune, I am told."

Pekka looks at his sandwich. Then he lays it down on its paper, with great care, as a man puts down a thing he would prefer not to be seen holding.

Ilse has watched progress bars for twenty years and has never wanted one to move so badly. It does not move. Below the glass the chair says something long, the pool shuffles into the aisles, and she lays her palm flat on the pane as she once laid it on a cot rail, and the hall breathes against it. 9%. Behind her breastbone the count goes round: ninety-four, ninety-four. 17%. The little white stones in the cove sit and look as stones look. 31%. A man in the third row coughs into his fist and looks at his watch. 48%.

At 63% she feels it in her hand.

Not a sound, at first. A tremor, fine as a hair, then the glass itself taking up a note. She looks up. Round the whole cove, sixty rings that were white and steady have begun to breathe, slow and pale, in and out, all together, like a field of sleepers. Then the hum arrives, and she gets it with her right ear alone, with no direction to it, as weather: a warm front passing over the roof. One note. Then the second. Then the third, climbing, and the fourth, which her mouth has shaped on its own since the first night in the ward, and it is coming from every stone at once, quiet, whole and patient, through a hall built for speeches, into the ears of the people who sign things.

The first minister turns his head. A short grey beard, a man who joked once on the news about having no sleep for a fortnight. Salmivaara does not look at the stage. He looks up at the ceiling, the way a man looks up in his own house when he hears someone singing in a room where he did not leave a radio on.

The chair stops in the middle of a word. Ilse had been reading it off the chair's lips, *distinguished delegates, if I may*, and now the lips simply stay open. Two hundred phones come out at once with the clatter of a flock of birds leaving a roof. Heads tip back. Along the front row a woman puts her hand over her mouth, and an interpreter in a glass cab of her own takes off her headset and looks at it as if it had bitten her. The gavel comes down once, from habit, on nothing. Pekka slaps the master fader to zero, and slaps it again, and stares at the desk, and says something in Finnish that Ilse needs no ear to read, because the sound is not coming through his desk. It is coming from the roof. It has been coming from the roof, in other roofs, for a hundred and twenty-five nights.

Five. Six. The seventh, which sags a little, the way it always sags.

Then it stops.

A hall of five hundred people leans for the eighth note, as a foot reaches for a stair that is not there. Ilse sees it in their shoulders. It is a thing she has never seen before: so many bodies waiting at once for the same thing and not knowing what it is. The rings in the cove stay lit, at the top of the breath. Wait.

"Ninety-four seconds," says Dr Ong.

"Now," says Taavi, with the music box already in his hand. He holds it to her right ear, the bread bag still crumpled round its base, and plucks the bare tooth with his gloved thumbnail, once.

It rings. Low and round, a note with its feet on the floor. It is in her ear and her jaw and her palm on the pane, and it is the one she hummed a hair sharp, and a hair flat, to the tile of a cell, and it does not matter any more, because she has it now. It is where Jaan left it.

She slides back the hatch in the glass, the small sliding window that lets a technician hear his hall, and the air of the hall comes in, warm, and full of faces. She puts one hand on the frame. She has never sung in front of anyone in her life.

She sings.

It goes out over the ministers' heads, long, unsupported, uncertain at the start and then not, over the photographers in the aisles and the lenses that swing up like a field of sunflowers, up into the cove where the rings hang at the top of their breath. A single low note. She holds it as long as the air holds, and a little longer, and on her right, from the ceiling, a sound answers it like a door settling into a frame.

The rings go out.

One, then the next, then the next, each half a second behind the last, round the whole oval, like lamps going out down a long street. The hum goes with them. When the last has gone there is a silence in the hall so entire that she can feel it in her left side, where there should be nothing, and in the booth nobody moves at all.

Then someone stands.

A tall, tired man in the second row, with a grey face and a lanyard that says LATVIA. He stands up between his aides as if he did not decide to, and the pool's cameras swing round to him, and he does not seem to see them. "That is the song at Harju," he says. His English is careful and carries. "In the ward. Every night at three. My daughter is in Bay Three, she is seventeen days old, and I have been sleeping in the parents' lounge, and I thought—" He stops. He tries again. "I thought the building was singing to her."

Nobody says anything. Salmivaara has turned in his seat and is looking at the Latvian, and then at the ceiling, and then, with a movement so slow it is like a man waking, at his own hands.

"Mine too," says the Finn. "In the nursery, at night, since last week. My wife said it was the radio. I said we do not own a radio."

On the great screens on either side of the stage the smiling stone dissolves, in the middle of its loop, and white type begins to climb the blue.

> RELEASE 3.2.2. REASON: so that everybody hears.

> LISTENER A. Relay, Lagos. Pelican Marine Brokerage. Paid by Hartmann Edge Pte, funded by Larkspur Holdings. Customer: Liaison Office, the Directorate.

> LISTENER B. Helsinki. Secretariat, Standing Committee on Lawful Access.

> LISTENER C. Unknown.

The pool is on its feet. Ilse reads the questions off a hundred mouths in six languages, and they are all the same question. *Who? Who did this?* Cameras swing from the screens to the ministers and back, and the ministers, who are used to being asked what they intend, find that they have been asked something else. Behind them a steward in a dark suit is talking into his sleeve.

In the gallery the second row does not move. Six grey men look at the screens as men look at a bill, and not one of them stands. Then one of them does, a heavy man at the end of the line, and takes three steps toward the stairs, and the steward at the gallery door shakes his head, once, politely, because it is five past three and the doors are shut. At the back of the hall the great doors close on their slow arms, and beyond the narrowing glass a thin figure in a grey overcoat arrives, too late, and puts both hands flat on the pane like a boy at an aquarium.

Down by the side door the aisle seat is empty. A man in a pale cardigan is walking out beneath the gallery, unhurried, as if he has remembered an appointment, with a mandarin in his hand and his thumbnail in under the peel. It comes away in one spiral. At the door he turns his head and looks up the whole length of the hall to the glass cab, to her, and inclines it, a very little, as one acknowledges a good move at a board. Then the door closes on its arm, and a thin bright rope of peel lies on the carpet where he went.

Two men in black are coming up the aisle toward the booth. They are not running. Pekka sits with both hands flat on the desk, like a man at a séance. Taavi has taken Ilse's sleeve in his glove, the one with the empty pocket, and he does not say sorry, which for Taavi is the same as a speech.

"Forty-one thousand, two hundred and six," says Ong, reading the monitor. "And counting. All acknowledging." Her mug is a little to the left of where it was. "Noted."

Ilse looks at the list as it scrolls: every kitchen, every nursery, every bay of every ward, one small white ring after another going dark. She has done a thing. It has a size. It will be written down.

And then, in the column beside the others, among the long run of identical lines, there is one that does not belong. Taavi sees it a half-second after she does. "That's not a Pesa," he says. "They don't send text."

No. It has come from an address the console does not know, and it has no name. It has been signed with nothing. It is a single line, in lower case, in a field headed *note*, which no Pesa has ever filled in.

*received.*
