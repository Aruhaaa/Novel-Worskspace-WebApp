# The Nightboat

The lie takes Pille three tram stops to build, and she makes it the way she makes a bed on the ward: tight at the corners, with nothing underneath that a patient could feel through the sheet. A baby shower. A niece in Helsinki, first child, due on the twentieth. A tin of kringel from the Kopli bakery, one knitted owl, three packets of rusks. Nobody has ever asked a woman with a baby bag a question she could not answer with her eyes wet.

"What's the stone?" says the man at the X-ray belt.

He is sixty or so, in a harbour authority jacket cut for a bigger man, and he has plainly been watching bags go through this machine since before it had colours. On his screen the duck bag lies open in orange and green. Rusks, owl, tin. And in the middle, packed round with biscuit, a dense dark oval the size of a fist.

"Teething stone," says Pille. She has never said the words in her life and they come out like scripture. "You chill them in the fridge. Babies like something cold on the gums."

"My Kadri had one." He sends the bag down the rollers with two fingers, as a man hands back a hat. "Congratulations to your niece."

"I'll tell her, dear. She'll be over the moon."

She goes up the gangway in her father's fur hat, which smells of pipe tobacco and Kopli rain, with the bag on her arm and her pulse going like a call bell. The Nightboat is thirty years old and looks it. Rust runs down the white flanks from every rail, like mascara after a christening, and someone has taped a cardboard star to the funnel. The crossing is cheap because it happens in the dark, when nobody can use the view. Pille last took it on her honeymoon, to buy a camera and a coat. She spent the night in the cabin with a flannel over her eyes while Tarmo explained the horizon to a bar. She still has the coat.

In her pocket, folded in four, is a summons. *Nurse P. Rebane will present herself at Kesklinn at 09:00 on Tuesday.* It is set in a typeface that sounds like a door. She has pictured the constable at five past nine, looking at an empty chair, and for the first time since the hum began she feels a step ahead of something instead of being driven in front of it.

The reception desk is on the sixth deck, a low counter under a garland of plastic holly, and the queue for cabin cards winds round a pillar in the shape of a long grumble. The clerk has fox-red hair and the face of a woman who has told two hundred people that the lift is not working. Pille has no card to collect. She is only passing, head down, fur hat low, on her way to the stairs, when she sees the hands.

They are folded on the counter, third from the front. Black. Fingerless. Wool.

Above them, toast-coloured leather and grey hair as neat as a pillowcase. He waits his turn with that courteous attention of his, a man preparing to be sorry. Pille has now had Leonid Sarapuu in a ward corridor, in a service lift and in her mother's kitchen eating herring. At this rate he will be at her funeral and know where the sandwiches are. He has bought himself a cabin, of course. A man who thinks about a nurse's mother's herring thinks about beds.

She does not run, because running is what a woman does who has something to run from. She turns, with the casual swing of someone who has forgotten her purse, and walks into the first thing that offers, which is twenty-six pensioners in crimson scarves.

They stand at the foot of the stairs being counted. A tiny old man with a pitch pipe on a ribbon goes down the ranks touching shoulders, one, two, three, like a priest with a censer, and loses his place at nineteen and begins again. The Nõmme Pensioners' Chamber Choir, bound for a carol evening at the Estonian House. Pille learns it in four seconds, because the woman next to her has pushed a music folder into her arms.

"You're the new alto."

"Contralto," says Pille. "With a bad knee."

"We all have bad knees. Stand behind Leida, she's tall."

Leida is tall. Pille stands behind her. The choir goes up the stairs in a body, a crimson caterpillar with a conductor for a head, and in the middle of it a woman in a fur hat learns the alto line of a carol she has never heard by following Leida's elbow. At the foot of the stairs the queue shuffles on. She does not look down. There is a place at the back of her neck that knows exactly where he is, and she gives it nothing.

The lounge on the eighth deck smells of cinnamon buns, diesel and wet wool, which is the smell of any ship, and of beer, which is what this ship is for. On a plywood stage a karaoke machine is murdering a Finnish ballad. The words crawl by on a screen, and the singer, a bearded man in a Father Christmas tie, follows them a bar and a half behind, like someone running for a tram. The choir takes two tables by the window. Pille slides in with her back to the door and the folder held up in front of her face, the way a child holds a menu.

In the black glass, between the reflections of the tinsel, she watches the door behind her. At ten to eleven the toast-coloured jacket comes in with a cabin card between two fingers. He stands at the head of the stairs and does what he did on the ward: counts the exits.

The nearest is across the dance floor. She is working out how a woman of forty-four with a bad knee might cross twelve metres of lino unseen when a hand takes hers.

"Rouva." It belongs to a very large Finn in a felt hat with reindeer ears, red in the face and as happy as a man who has been to Tallinn for the beer and found it. "Valssi," he says, and bows so deeply that she fears for the floor.

"Dear, I don't—"

But he has her. The ballad is in four-four and he waltzes anyway. The horror of it is that he is good. He steers her out between the tables in one slow turn after another, his hand warm in the small of her back like a hot-water bottle, and nobody looks at her twice. They are all looking at him.

"Pentti," he shouts, tapping his chest. "Kouvola. My wife says, Pentti, go to Tallinn, drink, be ashamed. So."

"Pille. Nurse."

"Ah! Nurse!" He is delighted. "I have a hip."

"Everyone has a hip, dear."

On the turn, over his elbow, she sees the door again. Sarapuu is working down the wall by the window, not hurrying, and his eyes have stopped on the choir's table. On the grey fur hat above the empty chair. Of course. She has worn it since the harbour, and a man who counts exits also counts hats. The next turn will bring her face to face with him across eleven metres of floor.

*Take what's in reach,* Salme used to say, of everything from handbags to husbands. With one hand Pille whips her father's hat off and into her coat. With the other she lifts the reindeer ears from Pentti's head and claps them on in its place. Then the turn arrives, and she meets the pale grey eyes across the lounge with her chin down and her whole forehead under felt, and nothing happens. Nobody in the world looks at a woman in reindeer ears. It is the finest disguise she has ever worn. His glance goes over her and the glad ridiculous room and moves on.

"Rouva!" cries Pentti, hatless, bereft. "My hat!"

"I'll send a postcard," says Pille, and lets go, and goes.

She leaves by the swing doors beside the toilets and takes the aft stairs two at a time on her bad knee, which is how she learns how bad.

On the landing of the seventh deck, between a machine that sells toothbrushes and a machine that sells nothing at all, stands a vending machine with a picture of a cheese on it. Her hands are shaking in the fine fast way she has told a hundred relatives is perfectly normal. Shock wants salt and fat; she has said that too. She feeds in coins, presses B4, and the spiral turns, and something falls with the clang of real metal.

It is a tin. On the label a smiling spoon and the word HERNEKEITTO. Pea soup, with a ring-pull, and no stove, and no spoon, and no reason.

"I wanted cheese," she tells the stairwell. "I got Finland."

She laughs. It comes out too high and goes on too long, and she stops it with the back of her wrist, because laughter in an empty stairwell is how the other thing begins. Soup. Friday's soup on her mother's stove, the man who ate it, the oldest answer in the world. The sea has simply sent her another. She puts the tin in the bag beside the stone. If the polite man finds her, she will offer him dinner. It is the only trick she has.

The corridor on the seventh deck is long and carpeted the colour of a bruise, and the doors go by with their numbers and their small brass eyes. 7101. 7103. Behind one a man snores in the key of the engines. She counts as she walks, because counting is something to do with a mouth, and the mouth, left alone, finds the tune.

She does not notice. It is the only song her mouth knows now: eight notes, the eighth sagging flat, hummed to Ott on Saturday, hummed in the van, hummed into the pillow. She is at 7109 when the bag answers.

One note. Thin and wrong, from under the rusks, at her hip, like a kitten in a sock. Pille stops dead on the bruise-coloured carpet.

She lifts the owl. The stone sits in its nest of biscuit crumbs, and the ring round its base is alive: a thread of white as faint as breath on glass. Ilse told her once, in the van, that the things have a cell inside no bigger than a thumb, so that a smoke alarm can still shout in a power cut. An hour of life, perhaps two, if something wakes them. Something has. A mouth, in a corridor, humming at them.

She does what she would do for any baby that has found its own cry. She swaddles. The rusk packets go round the stone, one, two, three, tight as a bandage; the scarf goes over; the owl settles on top like a hen on a nest, and she presses it down with both hands until the flat note dies into wool and the ring thins to a hair and goes out.

"Our Father," says Pille, to the bag. It has been thirty years. "Who art..." and nothing else comes. She tries in her own words. "Dear Lord, it's Rebane, Pille, Harju, nights. I don't call often. I'd take it as a kindness."

Nothing answers. In nineteen years of nights she has found the Lord a good listener and a poor correspondent. She walks on with her mouth shut tight.

Cabin 7118 is by the main stairs, as promised, between a fire extinguisher and a door marked CREW. She knocks twice and then once, which means nothing; it is the only knock she knows. The door swings inward under her knuckles, unlatched, on a room the size of a hospital lift. Two bunks. A steel basin. A kettle on a shelf. And on the lower bunk, feet apart and weight forward as if the bed might move, a woman with a grey braid, knitting.

She finishes her row before she looks up. "You are the nurse."

"And you're the old pipes." Pille's voice has gone high and silly. "I found your boots."

"Yes."

"I put them back. Both. I thought, somebody's mother taught her that heel."

"Yes," says the woman, and something happens at the corners of her mouth that is not quite a smile but lies down in the same bed. She sets the sock aside, stands, and pours from the kettle into a tin mug. The tea comes out as black as a bell. There is sugar in it that Pille can feel with her teeth.

"How many?"

"Four."

"Shock?"

"Yes. Drink."

Pille drinks. Her hands stop. "You've done this before."

"Nineteen years."

"Same," says Pille. "Different end of the pier."

The woman crosses to the door. She opens it a hand's width, her left hand flat on the steel edge, and puts her head to the gap and listens up the corridor and down it with her whole body, the way Pille listens to a ward at four in the morning. Pille, who notices hands for a living, notices these. The left is whole, all five, square-nailed, scarred along the thumb. It is the right that stops early: the little finger ends at the first joint, healed clean, an old job well done. The woman checks the corridor with the hand she has more of.

"He's on the boat," says Pille. "The polite one. He was in the queue at reception. I danced with a Finn."

"I know." The door closes. A bolt goes across. "He bought a cabin."

"That's what I thought."

It is a relief to say it, and the relief comes out of her as a small sound, and the small sound is the tune. She is humming. It is the third time tonight. In the bag at her feet, under the owl and the scarf and three packets of rusks, the stone answers, flat and muffled, a bird in a coat.

The woman does not raise her voice. She sits down on the bunk opposite, takes up the grey sock, and finds her place with a needle.

"Sit down," she says. "Don't hum."
