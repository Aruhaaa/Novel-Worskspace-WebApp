# The Photograph

Director Ene Vilu has uncapped the pen before Pille is through the door.

It lies on the blotter at an angle that speaks of rehearsal, beside two sheets of cream paper squared to the edge of the desk. Pille takes in the pen, the paper, the silver bob cut by someone who charges for straight lines, the rimless spectacles, and a smile that closes like a ledger. And she thinks: oh, my dear. I have been to this wedding before.

She passed Evald Olesk in the doorway just now, orange puffer rustling, hands in pockets, eyes on the top button of her coat. "Pille," he said, to the button. "Dear." She thought nothing of it. She thinks nothing of it now.

"Sit down, Pille. Thank you for coming in on your day off."

"For you, Director, I'd come in on my funeral." Pille sits. The chair is the low kind, designed so that the person in it looks up. "Is it the Christmas rota? I'll swap if somebody else wants the twenty-fourth. Dead is very quiet, you know, on the twenty-fourth. Alive is the trouble."

Vilu does not laugh. She turns the two sheets round with one finger. "It is a short form. A confidentiality undertaking. The ministry has asked that members of staff with a particular responsibility for the unit's building systems should sign."

"Building systems." Pille reads the heading with her lips. *Confidentiality Undertaking in respect of Building Systems and Associated Matters.* "Associated with what, dear?"

"With the building."

"Everything is associated with the building. I am associated with the building. I have been for nineteen years, and it has never once signed anything for me."

"It is a standard form."

"Which ministry?"

"The relevant ministry."

Pille reads on. Associated Matters, it appears, means anything she has seen, heard or suspected on the premises since the first of September. She reads that line twice. September. She keeps her face where it is, which takes work, because it is a face made for saying everything at once.

"And if I don't sign?"

"I would hate that." Vilu takes off her glasses and rubs the bridge of her nose with one neat finger, and Pille sees she is tired too, tired to the bone, and then sees that it makes no difference. "There is still on the table, as you know, the proposal to consolidate neonatal services at the Mustamäe campus. The ministry has been generous in agreeing to shelve it. Shelves, Pille, are things that things can be taken down from."

It's a good line. Pille has seen worse used on better. For one second she is in the incubator room at four in the morning, and the little glass roofs are all in a row with their green lights, and every one of them has a name on a card in her handwriting.

"I'll think," she says. "I think very slowly. It's the chlorhexidine."

"Until Monday." Vilu replaces the glasses. "There is also the photograph."

"Which photograph, dear?"

"From September. The technician. I understand you took one. The ministry would prefer to have it than to have it loose."

"I take a great many photographs. Mostly Ott's toes."

"Pille."

"Mostly his left toe," Pille says. "The right one's shy."

Vilu says nothing. She has the stillness of a woman who can wait out a lie the way a heron waits out a pond. Pille stands, folds the cream paper in thirds, puts it in her bag, and walks out of the door with her back as straight as the pencil in her bun. It is only in the corridor, beside a poster about hand gel, that she finds she can't feel her knees.

She has told one living person that the photograph exists. One. And she knows what he was wearing when he came out of that door.

* * *

Her mother's flat is on the ground floor of a long grey block on Kopli Street, with a view of the tram loop and a window box of plastic geraniums that she insists need no watering. Pille lets herself in with her own key and is met by a smell of cabbage soup and hairspray and the particular warm dust of an old person's radiator. Salme Rebane is eighty-three, in a quilted dressing gown the colour of a plum, sitting under a crocheted blanket with a pencil and the crossword, and she lifts her head like a cat that has heard a tin.

"Who's that?"

"Mum. It's Pille."

"Pille's at work." Salme examines her, narrow-eyed. "You've her nose. Are you Jaak's second cousin? From Pärnu?"

"If you like."

"I don't like. But sit, sit, you'll have soup." She goes back to the crossword. "Seven across. Eight letters. A person who is always expecting the worst."

"Mother."

"Pessimist," says Salme, with great satisfaction, and fills it in.

On the shelf above the radiator, among the china dogs and the doilies, stands a white pebble the size of a fist. It is the gift the hospital gave every staff family last Christmas, with a card, *To thank you for your service*. Its ring of light breathes slowly, white to pale and back. Salme talks to it as she would to a nephew who wasn't bright. "Pesa, put on the radio." A man begins to read the shipping forecast. "Not that one. The one with the songs." Another man begins to sing. "That's the one."

Pille ladles herself soup because it would be rude not to. Then she puts the cream paper on the table, because she has to put it somewhere, and says, "What do you make of that?"

Salme reads, with the pencil moving along the line like a finger. At the foot of the page her lips purse. "It's a very rude wedding invitation."

"Is it?"

"*You are invited to attend, and say nothing.*" She sniffs. "I went to one like that in 'seventy-one. The groom was a Party man. They served a ham the size of a child, and nobody said a word." She pushes the paper back. "Don't sign. You'll be wanting to say something."

"What would I want to say?"

"I don't know, dear. You've that look. Like a person with a hot potato in the mouth."

Pille laughs. She can't help it; the laugh comes up out of her like a cork, and the old woman beams, pleased with herself, and Pille wipes her eyes with the back of her wrist. Then she takes out her phone, thumbs to the picture, and holds it out. "Mum. Do you know this man?"

On the screen, a pleasant man of about forty in a Pesa-blue fleece grins at the camera under a knitted owl, thumb up. He is a man the world has always found it easy to forgive.

Salme takes the phone in both hands and holds it a long way off. "Oh, that's the Rebane cousin," she says. "The one who sells cars."

"Which Rebane?"

"The one with the teeth. He sold Jaak's brother a car with no floor. You put your foot through and you were driving a bath." She nods at the thumb. "You can tell he sells cars. Look at the thumb."

"Mum, he's a technician."

"He sells cars, dear. You'll never see a technician with a thumb like that."

Pille takes the phone back and looks at the screen for some time. It is the stupidest, kindest piece of detection she has ever been offered, and it is no use at all, and it makes her want to put her head on the table. She finishes the soup instead. At the door her mother says, "Will you come again, cousin? You're good company for an Estonian," and Pille kisses her on the plum-coloured shoulder, and the pebble on the shelf breathes white, then pale, then white.

* * *

At the hospital at half past two she does not go up to the ward. She goes to the foot of its stairs and stands on the third-floor landing, in the corridor's mouth, with the smoked-glass dome at the far end looking back down the length of it. Two of the day staff pass with a trolley and give her a look. She gives it back.

Pille puts her heels together, turns out her toes, lifts her arms into a round, and begins to dance.

It is not a good ballet. She is forty-four, in clogs, with a bun like a bird's nest and nineteen years of lifting people in her back. She does a plié. She does a second, deeper, with a tremor. She extends a leg behind her in what the nurses will later describe as an arabesque and what the porter will describe as a woman trying to start a motorbike. She curtsies to the dome. "Swan Lake, dears," she tells it gravely. "Act Two. I'm the smaller one."

If nobody watches that camera, nothing will happen. If somebody does, he will come.

It takes three minutes and ten seconds. She is halfway through a pirouette when the stairwell door opens, and she turns on her heel and finds him at her elbow, orange, rustling, hands deep in his pockets and the collar of the jacket damp with sweat in a corridor that is nineteen degrees.

"Pille. Pille, please."

"That was quick, Evald."

"I was passing."

"From where? IT is on minus one."

"From... the Director's." He hears himself. His mouth tries a smile, which comes out the wrong way up. "You shouldn't clown at the cameras, dear. They log."

"Who logs?"

"I'm only saying."

"Did you tell Ene about my photograph?"

"What photograph?" He says it much too fast. And then, because he is a man who has never in his life been able to hold two things at once, he says, "I have a mortgage, Pille. I've two girls. They asked, and they asked so politely."

Pille looks at him. She sees the sweat, and the mortgage, and the two girls, and a man in a sailing-boat pyjama who stood in this corridor on Monday morning and told the whole ward it was a glitch. She was going to be very angry. She finds she can't manage it; there is no room for it behind the other thing.

"I deleted it, Evald," she says, and puts a hand on his arm, and watches the relief run all the way down him like water down a window. "Weeks ago. A mistake. It was gone before the Director ever asked."

"Thank God," says Evald, and means it, and goes.

* * *

The lodge by the loading bay has forty hooks on a pegboard, each with a green lanyard and a numbered card, and a ledger for the contractors on the counter beneath. A man named Tõnis, who has spent twenty years being the front of the building, eats sunflower seeds and spits the husks into a saucer. She says she wants to look at September. He says, why not, and turns the book round.

There is the Thursday. Pille runs a finger down the line of pale handwriting. Cleaners. A man to mend the lift. A woman from Pesa in the spring, in March, with a clipboard and a van. Between the first and the end of September, not one technician. She counts the hooks. All forty are full; none were taken down that week.

"He came at ten past two in the morning," she says. "In a blue fleece, with a toolbox."

"At ten past two in the morning the lodge is a dog, dear, and the dog's asleep." Tõnis spits. "Who let him in?"

"Me."

He considers her, and the saucer, and her face. "Ah," he says. And turns the ledger back round, gently, as if it were a patient.

* * *

At half past five she is under the shelter at the Kopli Street stop, with the four's headlamps still a long way off through the snow and her feet beginning to go, when a tall woman steps out of the dark and stands, slightly turned, as if the street had something to say on one side.

Pille knows her at once, though she has only seen her once, at three in the morning with a cot rail under her palm. The streak of white at the left temple. A scar through the right eyebrow. A coat to the calves and a leather bag across the chest, and the head angled to put the right ear forward, the way a person tilts a cup.

"Nurse Rebane."

"Dear God. You." Pille moves round to the woman's right, and stands there like a bodyguard. "I rang you. Twice. A lovely rhythm of ringing and nothing."

"My phone's in a freezer."

"Of course it is. Mine's in my bra." She pulls a face. "How did you find me?"

"A porter the size of a wardrobe. He said you take the four, and that you're always early."

"Rein tells anybody anything. It's why he's a porter and not a spy." The wind moves the snow along the street in long, quiet sheets. Pille looks up into the long, grave, tired face. "I had something to tell you. Wednesday morning, at nine, in daylight, I was going to ring."

"Tell me."

She tells her. In September, a Thursday, ten past two. A man called Aksel, a blue fleece, a lanyard turned backwards. A fifty-euro note under a mug, for the coffee fund. An hour alone with the records machine in the charting room, while she held the fort and did a drip-chart and thought about her feet.

Ilse listens. She does not interrupt. When Pille stops, she says, "And then?"

"And then I bought a kettle."

"A kettle."

"With the fifty. For the staff room. It screams." Pille's voice cracks on the word and she stops, and the corner of Ilse's mouth does something small and unwilling. "I let him in, dear. I held the door. I even took his photograph, with an owl on his head, for the Wall of Heroes. I thought he was funny."

"He was meant to be."

"I know that now, do I?" Pille wipes her face with the back of her mitten. "What is it? What did he put in my machine?"

So Ilse tells her. The carrier, the hum, the flat note every ninety-four seconds. A hundred and seventeen nights since the fourth of September. And what it carries, out through the speakers on the shelves among the knitted owls, a tune with names in it: weights, birth dates, children of ministers in six countries, off Harju's records, read out to the dark.

"Our babies," Pille says.

"Some of them. Babies of people who sign things."

"They're singing my babies." She says it quite quietly. "Every night."

"Yes."

"And me with the kettle."

The four comes round the curve at the end of the street with its bell going. Neither of them moves. When it has stopped, and opened its doors, and closed them again, and ground away, Pille says, "And the old man? Your kringel man. In the vest. He sat an hour in the parents' lounge looking at my speakers, and he gave me a card, and he said, if they ever sing. How is he?"

Ilse does not say anything for a while. She has the look of a person walking out onto ice, slowly, to the place where the weight is.

"He died last night," she says.

Pille cries. It is one proper cry, without ornament, doubled over a little with her mitten pressed against her mouth, the way she once cried at nineteen over a boy who was not worth it and a baby who was. It lasts as long as it lasts. The tall woman stands on her right side, saying nothing, and does not pat her. Pille is grateful for it. Then she straightens up and sniffs, enormously, and pulls the pencil out of her bun, and her hair comes down on one side like a bird that has changed its mind.

"Right," she says. "Hold out your hand."

"What?"

"Not yours, mine. Hold the pencil." She gives it to Ilse, rolls back her mitten, and holds out the back of her own left hand under the shelter light. Then she takes the pencil back. "No paper. Paper's in my bag and my bag has been in a director's office." She writes. It is a small hand, upright and strong, and it presses hard.

*PHOTO ON PAPER, NOT PHONE.*

*TELL EVALD NOTHING (KINDLY).*

*MUM'S PEBBLE?*

*FLOWERS, KRINGEL MAN.*

She runs out of hand. She turns it over, and begins again on the palm.
