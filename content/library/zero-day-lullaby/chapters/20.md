# A Mother in Kopli

"Aksel!" Her mother stands in the doorway of the flat in her quilted dressing gown, the cord knotted twice, at ten past seven on a Sunday morning, delighted. "Is the car fixed?"

Pille, on the landing, holds the banister and decides to be Aksel. It is easier. Nineteen years of nights have taught her never to argue with a patient about who the patient thinks you are. She is still in the ward fleece and the paper trousers, her hair smells of bleach and warm cotton and something less nice, and the first tram of the morning has shaken the last of the laughter out of her and left a sort of ringing.

"New wing mirror, Mum. Good as new."

"I told Pille. I said, he sells cars, he'll know a man." Salme turns and goes ahead down the hall, small and straight, eighty-three years old, with her chin up the way she has carried it into every room of her life. "She never listens. She's my husband's second cousin and she sits in my kitchen eating my biscuits as if she'd bought them. Wipe your feet."

"I'm Pille, Mum."

"That's what I said."

The hall smells of cabbage and floor polish and a thousand cups of tea. Pille wipes her feet. Her plan, formed in the van and refined on the tram, is modest: to check the dates on her mother's blood-pressure pills, fill the little plastic week, and sit for ten minutes in the one flat in Kopli where nobody has ever wanted anything from her but company. Then, at the kitchen door, she hears a cup set down on a saucer, very gently, by a man.

"Tarmo's come," her mother announces, in the voice of a woman revealing a surprise party. "Look, Tarmo. It's Aksel. He's mended the car."

In her late father's chair at the kitchen table sits the polite man from the ministry.

His jacket, the colour of toast, hangs over the chair-back. His grey hair is as neat as it was in the ward corridor. Both hands, in their black fingerless gloves, are wrapped round Salme's good cup, the one with the gold rim, and on the oilcloth in front of him stand the teapot in its knitted cosy, shaped like a hen, and a saucer of pickled herring that has not been touched. He rises as he rose on the ward, to exactly the height at which rising is polite.

"Mrs Rebane. Forgive me. Your mother was kind enough to let me in, and I have been given a part."

"He's Tarmo," says Salme firmly.

"Tarmo was my husband," Pille tells the kettle. "For six years. He left in 2009 for a woman in Pärnu and a better sofa."

"Then I gather I am a disappointment," says the man. "I have done what I could. Leonid Sarapuu. 'Facilities' was a little untidy of me."

Pille stands with her bag on her shoulder and takes her own pulse without touching anything, by watching the second hand of the kitchen clock. A hundred and ten. Quick, regular, nothing to write home about. She left this man in a lift three hours ago, with a rubber wedge and a grin, and he has beaten her to her mother's kitchen. The thought is so enormous that it comes out of her as something small and domestic.

"Have you eaten?"

"Your mother has been generous with the tea."

On the stove stands Friday's pea soup, so thick that a spoon will stand in it unaided. Pille has the lid off and the ladle in her hand before she knows she has decided. It is the oldest answer there is. When the bailiff comes, when the vet comes, when the war comes: soup. She can think of nothing better, and in her experience people who are eating soup are seldom doing anything else. "Sit," she says. "You'll have some."

"That is very kind."

He sits. She ladles. He eats the way he does everything, upright and unhurried, the spoon moving away from him. Salme puts the saucer of herring in front of him again with a click.

"Tarmo. Herring."

"Thank you, Mrs Rebane." He does not touch it. Salme, satisfied, trots off down the hall to look for a hot-water bottle she is certain he left behind in 2009, and the kitchen shrinks to two people and a clock.

"Your pigeons," says Sarapuu quietly. "On the Wednesday, on your ward. Mr Olesk was a saint with a small car, and you had been feeding some pigeons. I thought about the pigeons for a day and a half. It seemed to me that I owed you something true." He lays the spoon in the empty bowl, handle at four o'clock. "I have a son in Narva. Roman. He is twenty-six and works for the border service, and every Sunday at noon he rings to tell me that I eat badly. That is all. I would like you to know it."

"Why?"

"Because you have been told a great many things lately that were not." His eyes are a pale, clean grey, and his hands, round the gold-rimmed cup, are perfectly steady. "I am not here to frighten you. I would ask only that you not be foolish. There is a small grey recorder, ten euros, of the kind sold by a man with a past. A tall lady has it. I should like it, and I should like to go home afterwards and have no opinion."

"I don't know where she is."

"No." He seems relieved. "I did not think you would. I thought I would ask in a kitchen. I have been in a great many kitchens this winter, Mrs Rebane. They are the only rooms in a house where people tell the truth. I should like this to be the last one."

It is, she thinks, the most frightening thing anyone has ever said to her over soup, and he has not raised his voice or moved his hands or said one word she could repeat to a policeman. He has asked for a recorder. He has told her about his son. That is what she keeps coming back to, ladling nothing, with the pot lid in her hand: that the whole of the threat is in how nicely he has asked.

"Tarmo!" Salme is back in the doorway, hot-water bottle held like a trophy. "The herring!"

Sarapuu looks at the saucer for the length of one breath. Then he picks up a fork, spears a piece, and eats it, the way a man takes medicine from a child, with a faint dignified wince. His eyes water. "Delicious," he says thickly.

"He never ate it," Salme tells Pille, with deep satisfaction. "Twelve years. Not once."

He washes his bowl at the sink. He dries it, and puts it away in the correct cupboard, which he finds without asking. Then he puts on the toast-coloured jacket, and at the kitchen door, with his hat in his hands, he says, "Your mother's front lock was stiff, Mrs Rebane. I took the liberty. You will find it turns very sweetly now."

Pille opens her mouth.

"And there is a loose tread on the second landing. The third from the top. You will want to speak to the housing association." He inclines his head to Salme, as to a duchess. "Mind the step."

She listens to him go. Slow, even, rubber-soled. At the second landing there is a pause, and she waits for the creak of the loose tread, and it does not come. He has stepped over it. Then the street door sighs, and he is gone.

Pille shuts the flat door and turns the key. It turns like butter. She hates it so much that she has to put her forehead against the wood.

"He's thinner," says Salme from the sitting-room doorway. "But he ate the herring, I'll give him that. Tarmo never ate the herring."

"Mum, that wasn't Tarmo."

Salme considers this with her head on one side, the way she once considered a column of figures at the cooperative. "No," she agrees. "He had better manners."

Pille laughs. It comes out wet and high, and she puts her hand over her mouth, and her mother, pleased, pats her arm.

In the sitting room, on the shelf between the wedding photograph and a china cat with a chipped ear, the Pesa stands as it has stood since Christmas, white and fist-sized and polite, its ring of light breathing slowly. The hospital gave one to every staff family, in a box with a bow. Pille carried this one home on the tram and set it up with her own hands, because Salme likes to ask it the time.

"It hums at night, you know," says Salme, following her eyes. "Quietly. When I can't sleep. I thought it was your father."

Pille does not laugh at that. She has been a nurse too long to know whether to believe it, and a daughter too long not to. She crosses the room and takes the plug out of the wall. The ring does not fade. It simply isn't, like an eye that has been closed.

"Who turned off the pebble?"

"I did, Mum. It was tired."

Her phone shivers in the fleece pocket. She takes it out.

> Nurse Rebane. Following events on Ward 3 at 03:40 and your departure from your post, you are suspended from duty with immediate effect, pending review. You will not attend Harju or access any hospital system. Please return your lanyard by post. E. Vilu, Director.

Pending. The hospital has a great many things pending and is in a hurry about only one of them. She turns the phone face down on the mantelpiece.

"Who writes to you at this hour?"

"A very rude wedding invitation, Mum."

"Don't go. They'll want a present."

The stone is warm in Pille's hand, and lighter than she expected. Ilse's pebble from Bay Four sits behind police tape on Pikk Street. This one came out of a private flat in a box with a bow, and no ward has ever touched it, and nobody can say afterwards that a nurse made it up.

She could give it to someone. That is the thought that comes first, and it comes in the kindest voice. The boy in the gloves would be here with the van in twenty minutes. The tall woman would take it from her on any pavement in Tallinn. She has only to hold out her hands. And she sees, clear as a chart, what it looks like when other people carry it: an old man in a wrongly buttoned vest, who sat an hour in her parents' lounge with a box of kringel and said *call Ilse*, and is dead; and a tall woman with one good ear, sliding three floors down a chute in one clog. She let the thing into her ward herself, for fifty euros and a kettle. Everyone since has been carrying the cost of it.

She goes to the hall wardrobe instead. At the back, behind her father's coat, is the blue quilted baby bag with a duck on it, bought in 2008 for a cousin's baby and never given, because the cousin moved to Perth and the postage was a scandal. From the kitchen cupboard come the rusks: three packets, because Salme buys one every time she goes to the shop and has forgotten the others for twelve years. From the sofa she takes the grey knitted owl with the wonky ears that the Thursday knitters send home with every staff mother. The stone goes into the bag in a nest of rusks, the cord coiled beside it, the owl sitting on top like a hat. Anybody who looks will see a woman on her way to a baby shower with an unwise amount of biscuit.

The bag goes into a brown paper carrier from the Kopli bakery, one of the hundred in the drawer under the sink. Pille takes the pencil from her bun and writes on the side, in capitals, pressing hard: EVIDENCE. A woman who writes EVIDENCE on a bag has stopped hiding it. Good. She is tired of hiding.

"Aksel," says her mother at the door, straightening his collar, which is not a collar. "Mind the car."

"I will, Mum."

On the second landing she steps over the third tread from the top. Then she steps back and tests it with her toe. It wobbles. He was right. He was right about everything.

The bus stop is outside the bakery, in the thin blue snow of eight o'clock. The bus, when it comes, is the long kind with an accordion in its middle, nearly empty on a Sunday, and it kneels at the kerb with a sigh. The doors fold open. Pille lifts the paper bag onto the top step, and then she does not get on after it. She stands on the pavement with her arms hanging and finds that she has let go. It is the best feeling she has had since September. Her hands are empty. The cold gets into her fingers. Someone else is carrying it now, someone with a ticket and a place to be.

The doors fold shut. The bus kneels, rises, and pulls away, with a paper bag marked EVIDENCE sitting on its top step like a dog left outside a shop.

She watches it go. She sees what she has done. She sees the old man in the vest, and the tall woman with one good ear, as plainly as if they were standing beside her on the kerb, and then Pille Rebane, forty-four years old, with a bad knee and nineteen years of nights, picks up her feet and runs.

"Wait!" She has one arm over her head, the way women have flagged down buses in this city since there were buses, and she is shouting in the voice that has stopped fights in four wards. "Wait! That's mine!"
