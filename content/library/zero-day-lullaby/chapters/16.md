# Dead Drop

By noon on Saturday there is nothing left of Helle's flat but the smell.

Taavi reports it from a bench in the Kalamaja library annex, in a whisper, with his hat in his lap and a hi-vis vest folded under his arm. He has been to Mustamäe and back. He went in the vest and a cap, carrying a clipboard with nothing on it and a spanner, and said "boiler" to every person he passed, and nobody on any of the five floors asked him a single question. "Never underestimate a spanner," he says. "Bullet points. One. The door of 5C is open. No tape, no police. Two cleaners in white coats, very quiet, with a trolley. Three, the oven is still warm. I put my glove on it. The tray's in the rack, washed. The fridge has a strawberry on it and nothing under the strawberry."

"The photograph."

"Gone. Gert and all." He turns the hat over. "The woman on the sixth, the one with the pigeons, was on the landing. I said I was checking the pipes. She said two nice men went down at ten past three, with Helle between them, and Helle was carrying a tin." He swallows. "She said Helle was quite cheerful. She waved to the pigeon lady. She said, *Pipes, Maimu, it's only pipes*."

Ilse sits very still beside him. On the long table before them lies a newspaper that neither is reading, and across the room, in a pool of green lamplight, the librarian is stamping something with the dull regularity of a woman at the end of a war. Helle ate the pastry. Ilse has gone over it on the tram, and in the night, and over the porridge she did not eat: the bite, the chair, the quick calm face. She went down five flights of stairs with a tin, in the company of two men with a bag, waving, and she took a lie with her to hold up while Ilse got away.

"Right," says Ilse. "The call."

* * *

The Kalamaja annex has a silent reading room, long and high and cold, with green-shaded lamps and tables worn pale by two centuries of elbows, and at the far end of it a glass booth the size of a lift, marked STUDY ROOM B and booked in the name of Madis's niece. It holds a table, four chairs, a radiator that ticks, and a wheeled whiteboard on which some earlier occupant has drawn, in green, the full skeleton of a cat.

The laptop on the table cost sixty euros that morning at the flea market behind the railway station, from a man in a fur hat who said, honestly, that it had a past. It has no past now. Taavi wiped it in the tram, cursing sweetly, and loaded nothing on it but a browser and a small, ugly program of his own. On the headset that Ilse wears over her good ear and the one Taavi shares with her, the line crackles, clicks, and fills with the sound of a sea of rain.

Singapore comes first. Dr Mei Lin Ong sits in front of a window full of towers, with a glass mug of hot water at her elbow, and behind her the evening has gone the colour of a bruise. It is six o'clock there. She looks as she always looks, a woman who has never once been surprised in a lift.

"Good afternoon, Ilse. Taavi." She considers the screen. "That is a cat."

"It's on the whiteboard. It was here."

"Noted."

Lagos arrives with a roar. Chidi Abara is on a pavement. Behind him are a petrol queue, a bus painted with the face of a saint, and a vast sky the colour of a tin plate, and he is wearing the loudest shirt Ilse has ever seen, and holding, she counts, three phones: one in his hand, one under his chin, one stuck in his breast pocket like a flower. "You. Ilse. Look at you, you in a library, in a *library*. Is it quiet? Tell me it's quiet. I can't remember what that is."

From behind the glass, a long, sustained, unfriendly shush.

"That's her," whispers Taavi. "The stamp lady. She's coming."

"Sorry! Sorry, madam!" Chidi drops his voice to a stage whisper that carries a good deal further than his speaking voice, and from somewhere behind him in Lagos there comes a high, ragged, outraged *meh-eh-eh-eh*. Referee has found the microphone. The goat is in the frame. It looks into the lens with its yellow rectangular eye and says its piece again. In the reading room, the stamp stops. The shush that follows comes in two parts, a rise and a long fall, like a tyre going down.

Ilse turns the volume to nothing. She takes a marker from the tray and says, very quietly, "Listen. I have four things. I have twenty minutes of the glass. And one of them is that we are going to catch somebody."

* * *

It takes her ten of the twenty minutes to say the first thing, which is what she knows.

She says it on the board, in the marker, in her small upright hand, because it is a thing she cannot hear herself say. A tool. Old. Sold in 2017 by a man who thought he was selling a demonstration. Sold twice. The implant in the ward is a descendant of it, an offspring, a bad one. She does not say *Vienna*. She does not say *Helle*, or *token*, or *comb tooth*. She holds the marker still over the board a moment and then writes, at the bottom, the plainest thing she can:

*SOME OF IT WAS OURS.*

Nobody speaks. In the window in Singapore the hot water steams. Chidi, who has not been silent since the age of four, looks into his phone with an expression she has not seen on him, as if somebody he liked had turned out to have a limp.

"Noted," says Ong.

It is not the voice of the barometer. It is a different note, lower, and she holds it a little longer than usual, so that the word has a floor in it. Then she picks up the mug. "Go on."

The second thing is the plan, and for it the whiteboard serves. In the cat's belly Ilse draws a ward, and a box in it that is the charting-room computer, and from the box a long wavering line that goes up out of the building like a smoke. "Three o'clock every night, the machine in the charting room reads out its records into the hum. The thief takes only what it wants: ministers' children. The speakers hear it, decode it, and send it on at 03:12 to your rack." She taps the Singapore end. "And from there, a hop to Lagos, and on to someone. We have never seen who. We have never been able to, because everything in that line is a record of someone else's baby."

"Show me a baby that is nobody's," says Taavi softly.

"Exactly that." She draws a small box and writes in it. "A canary. We invent a child. A child with a name that no one else has ever had, a weight that no one else has ever weighed, a birth hour that is nobody's birth hour. We put the record into the ward's system from the infected machine, on a night when the implant is listening. It sings it out at three. And then we watch who comes for it."

"A fake baby," says Chidi with real tenderness.

"With a signature. Wherever she turns up, we'll know the road she took."

She writes it small, under the box, as if it were a secret: *LUMI TAMM-KASK. 1,611 G. BORN 02:14.* Taavi reads it over her shoulder and says nothing at all. It is the name of a child with Jaan in it. Snow, in Finnish, and a birch. She does not look at him. She caps the pen.

"Unofficially," says Ong, after a moment.

"Unofficially."

"I will say it twice. *Unofficially.* I shall watch the rack at 03:12, your time, 09:12 mine, from a laptop that is not issued to me, and I shall write nothing down." She sips. "I would add that if this record reaches the rack, I shall be able to see who reads it. Not who they are. Which door."

"That's all we need."

"Then you shall have it. And since you have been honest with me, I shall be honest with you." She lifts the mug a little, a person touching a rail. "I have been watching Rack 14-B since you called me on Wednesday. It has two kinds of reader. One I can place: it arrives by Lagos, by Mr Abara's gateway, a courier of sorts. It is the bridge. The second I can place less well, but it is in the north: Finland, I think. They read in the morning. The files go out as the sun goes up." She puts the mug down. "And there is a third."

The reading room has gone quiet around the glass. Somewhere beyond the green lamps the stamp comes down.

"It has touched the rack four times since September," says Ong. "I found the traces only yesterday, because it takes pains. It authenticates to the management plane with logins named for old streets in Singapore. Sago Lane. Hill Street. Tank Road. Streets that were pulled down fifty years ago to put up office blocks. It does not go through Lagos. It does not go through anything I can see. It reads, and it leaves no scratch."

"A third reader," says Ilse.

"A third kind of friend," says Ong. "I do not know whose it is. I should like to."

* * *

Chidi's news arrives with a crack of the knuckles.

"So." He holds up the third phone, the one that is not a bank or a football. "You'll want to know. Ten minutes ago, my regulator, the Central Bank, those gentlemen in their nice grey suits, sent an announcement to every payment company in Nigeria. A *surprise audit*. Of Kobo Lane. Next week. They say it is routine. Hear me, you know what is *routine*? A man leaves the pitch on a stretcher, and the ref says, play on, it's routine." He laughs, his big one, and it goes on a little too long. "They have gone for the ankles. Late tackle, no ball. And do you know what date is on the letter?"

"Wednesday," says Ilse.

"Wednesday. Five hours after our call. I counted. They kept it in a drawer three days to watch me sweat." He stops laughing. "You think that's the Pesa on the wall of my office. The speakers. You think they heard us."

"I think someone knows you were curious."

"Ah, it's okay, you know." Chidi lifts the phone and shakes it, as if there were rain in it. "I have friends who owe me. I have a goat." The goat, on cue, steps into the frame and eats the edge of his sleeve. "Go on, Ilse. Do the last thing. You've got four minutes."

The last thing is the thing she has been dreading since the start of the call, and now that she comes to it she finds it has been there all along, like the edge of a stair in the dark. She looks at the board. At the ward, the box, the smoke. At the canary. And for the first time she asks herself the plain question she should have asked when she drew it.

Who types it?

Not Taavi, who cannot be in the ward. Not herself, who is a name on a police form. It has to go in at the infected machine, the charting-room records computer, the one with the Events tab, the one that has been singing at three o'clock for a hundred and twenty-two nights. A record typed on any other terminal on the ward would sit in KindWard and go nowhere, because the implant reads one machine and no other. And that machine is under glass, beside the nurses' desk, behind a login with a green lanyard on it, in a charting room watched by a smoked-glass dome.

"I need Pille," she says.

"The nurse," says Taavi.

"Only her. Only on a night when she's on shift."

"Tonight," says Ong, who has been looking at a calendar. "It is Saturday. Tomorrow is Sunday, and the rack reads every night. I should like to see a result before the Directorate does something worse."

"I know," says Ilse.

"Then ask her."

"I haven't a phone."

"Noted," says Ong, gently. "Borrow one."

* * *

The boy on the library steps is about fourteen. He is sitting on his school bag in a puffy jacket, doing algebra on his knee, and he holds a phone in a cracked case with a small, pale sticker on its back: a white pebble with a ring around it, and the word *Kuula*. It is a Pesa sticker. It would be.

"Twenty cents a minute," he says.

"What?"

"The call. It's my data." He considers her, the white streak, the scar, the angle at which she holds her head. "It's fair," he says. "It's a market."

She gives him two euros. He unlocks the phone with a thumb and passes it over, and turns his face with great politeness into his algebra. On the stairs behind her, the stamp lady has come out for her cigarette, and is watching with the eyes of a person who has shushed more children than she has had hot dinners.

The number is in the notebook, on the inside of the cover, under the red thread. It is not in her handwriting. It was written under a shelter light on Kopli Street, in a small, upright, pressing hand, with a pencil out of a bun: PILLE, and ten digits, and a drawing of a kettle.

It rings four times. Five. She thinks of a phone in a bra, and of a ward at three in the afternoon where a woman who works nights is asleep with her feet on a pillow. Then the line opens on a clatter and a gasp and the sound of a kettle, and a voice thick with sleep says, "If you are selling me a boiler, dear, I will cry."

"It's the tall one," says Ilse. "I need your machine."

There is a pause. It is perhaps three seconds. She can hear a radiator, and a clock, and, very faintly, the breathing of a woman who has been waiting two days for this and is working out how to say it. Ilse has time to think of the boy, and the algebra, and the cold on the steps, and then Pille Rebane says, quite calmly, in a voice that has done nineteen years of nights:

"Tonight. Three."
