# Who Else Hears

"Nine thirty-six," says Dr Ong. "Out from the rack's public address. Forty-one seconds, one point nine kilobytes, to a number in Lagos. That is your first reader."

She is a square on the left-hand screen, in front of a window of rain-bright towers, a glass mug of hot water at her elbow with a thread of steam going up. It is nearly midnight in Singapore. She has not hurried one syllable since the call began. She holds each line of the log up to her camera as if it were a specimen in a jar, and reads it aloud, and does not send it. "Unofficially," she says again. "I am a woman reading a clock."

In the den in Telliskivi it is twenty to six on Sunday evening and has been dark for two hours. Frost has grown inward from the corners of the windows. The Mona Lisa on the ceiling looks down on three screens, three time zones, and one cup of tea gone cold since noon in a mug shaped like a cat. Ilse sits on an upturned crate with the notebook on her knee and a pencil behind her good ear. Taavi, in his mustard gloves, types with the speed of a man who has lately learned how much rides on his fingers. On the whiteboard, under ILSE 0, DOG 1, PROVERB 1, he has drawn three boxes and lettered them A, B and C.

"The Lagos address is mine," says the right-hand screen.

Chidi Abara is in the server room at Kobo Lane because it is Sunday and the building is empty, and he says it is the only place where nobody can tell him to take a day off. He wears an orange shirt. His three phones lie in a row on the desk like a hand of cards. Behind him the racks hum in a cold blue light and, from somewhere beyond the door, comes the indignant voice of a goat.

"Not mine, mine," he corrects himself. "On my relay. My gateway, you hear me? The pipe a whole country pays its electric bill through. Somebody has been using it as a drainpipe since September. Two thirty-six this morning our time it comes through, and from my gateway it goes on to a wallet. You know who tops up that wallet? Hartmann Edge. First of every month. Same hand that pays for Mei Lin's rack."

"Pelican Marine Brokerage," reads Taavi. "Lagos. Two directors, both lawyers, both born on the same day."

"Noted," says Dr Ong.

Ilse writes in the notebook, in her smallest hand: *A. Lagos. Pelican. Hartmann pays.* "Two buyers," she says. "Two readers on that rack all autumn. One of them sits in Helsinki in the mornings. Whoever the Lagos one is, he is the other buyer."

"You say the Directorate like it's a dirty word," says Chidi. "I have a guy who says it in his sleep."

"Nine forty-one," says Ong. "Helsinki."

Ilse unfolds page one of the contract from the inside of her coat. She printed it herself, on Friday, on paper, because paper keeps its mouth shut, and it has been folded so long that the creases have gone soft as cloth. Taavi reads out sixteen addresses in a block. Her finger goes down the page and stops. *Purchaser One. The Standing Committee on Lawful Access, Secretariat.* The same post-box office, the same street. Not a hint of disguise. They did not think anyone would ever check.

"B," she says.

"Nine fifty-two," says Ong, and for the first time since the call began she puts down the mug. "This one I do not like. A consumer fibre line. A flat. Block 112, Toa Payoh, fourteenth floor, installed twenty-three days ago in the name of a retired schoolteacher who died in March. The login is Pickering Street."

"One of the old streets," says Ilse.

"Sago Lane, Hill Street, Tank Road, and now Pickering. It read Lumi and it left no scratch. I should like to ask the flat a question." She types. In the small square her face is lit from beneath, and perfectly calm. "Reply. Reply. Reply." A pause the length of a held note. "Request timed out. Request timed out." She sits very still. "I have asked the registry. The registry says there is no such line. It was there four seconds ago. I have watched many addresses stop answering, Mr Mägi. I have not before watched one stop having been there."

"Listener C," says Taavi, hushed, and writes it on the whiteboard in a box that is already empty.

Ilse writes C in the notebook. She leaves the line beneath it blank. After a moment she adds a question mark so small it could be a speck. Two of the three are customers, men who bought a weapon and have come to see what it carried. The third bought nothing. The third came in by an old street's name, in the small hours, and watched a baby leave a building, and took its router out of the wall.

"You see?" says Chidi, suddenly loud. "You see this, now?"

He has turned his laptop on the desk, and in the right-hand square the room swings: racks, cold light, a window in a door. "Hold on. Hold on, my guy." Beyond the glass stand four men in grey shirts with lanyards, a woman with a clipboard, and a seventh figure in a suit and no lanyard at all, who waits at the back with his hands folded. They do not break in. They have a key, and have had it for some time. The first of them says something polite about the Central Bank examination directive and a letter dated Wednesday.

"This is the surprise audit," Chidi tells the camera, standing up, spreading his arms. "You see how surprised I am. Look, they have parked the bus. Four at the back, nobody's scoring today." His voice is a performance, bright and loud, a man whistling past a graveyard, but his eyes have started to go from face to face, counting. The men in grey begin on the racks. One at a time they unplug a unit, ease it out, and coil the cable over the elbow, while the woman writes a label on a strip of tape and reads it back. "You notice they coil? In my country that is a man who has been to a good school."

His three phones ring. All three, together: the bank, the regulator, the football. The sound fills the little square like a flock of birds, three different tones landing at once. Chidi throws back his head and laughs, a great shout of a laugh. "Everybody loves me today! Ha! Everybody—"

He picks up one phone, and then the second, and then the third. The laugh goes out of him piece by piece. In the cold blue light, his face does something that looks like arithmetic. "It is the same number," he says, in a voice she has not heard from him. "On all three. That is not a Lagos number. That is not any number I gave to anybody."

The man in the suit turns his head and looks into the camera. He does not smile. A hand comes down from above and closes the laptop lid, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, and the right-hand screen is black.

For a while nobody in Telliskivi says anything. The frost ticks on the glass. The cat mug goes on being cold.

"Seventeen fifty-four," says Taavi, who has been timing. He swallows. "We said Pelican at seventeen fifty. Four minutes."

Four minutes. Nobody gets from the Central Bank's office to a server room in four minutes on a Sunday. They were already in the car park, with the engine running, waiting for a reason. And someone gave them one. Ilse thinks of the four of them on the call, Ong, Chidi, Taavi, herself, and her eyes go to Taavi's gloved hands and rest there. She does not believe it. The part of her that believes things puts it down at once. The other part gets up and looks round the room, as she would look round a client's server cupboard: not for who, but for what has ears.

A bike lamp with a dead battery. A jar of screws. A tin of noodles. And on the shelf above the whiteboard, between the jar and the lamp, a Pesa Mini wearing a hat.

The hat is knitted, in the red and white of a football club she does not know, and it has a bobble. She has looked at it a dozen times since Friday. Her eye filed it with the pot plant and the Mona Lisa, as part of the ordinary mess of a young man's room. The ring around its base is white, and it breathes.

"Taavi."

"What?"

"How long has she been on that shelf?"

He follows her eyes, and for a second he does not understand. Then he does. "No. Ilse, no, that's Susan. She's a bit. I bought her for the group chat. Two weeks ago, for a joke, the hat's from a pub in Riga. I never even set her up. She's not connected to anything." His voice climbs. "She is on the wi-fi. Madis gives the password to everyone, it's on a beer mat downstairs."

"Since?"

"The twenty-second."

Ilse sits quite still on her crate. Ten minutes and forty seconds of Jaan's voice, played aloud in this room by a man with a card reader and a space heater: *Ilse. I sold it.* The names. The token. Helle's signature. And two grey coats on a stair in Mustamäe, an hour early. She puts that thought down very carefully, like a full cup, and does not pick it up again.

Taavi has gone the colour of porridge. He crosses the room in three steps, takes the plug out of the wall, and holds the stone in both gloves, with the cord hanging and the hat sliding off over the bobble. "Sorry," he says. "Sorry, Susan. Sorry, Ilse. Sorry. Sorry." The ring does not fade out. It simply is not there, as a word is not there once it has been rubbed from a board.

"Is there anything else in this room that listens?"

"The laptops are wiped. There's the card reader." He hesitates. "Your pockets."

"What about them?"

"There's a paper from Bochum." He wipes his nose on a mustard wrist. "A phone in the next room can hear a cardamom pod crack and tell it from a knuckle. It's the crunch, it has a fingerprint. I'm not saying anyone has your pods. I'm saying you do a lot of cracking."

Ilse takes the three pods from her coat. They are the last she has, small, green, papery, each one a small decision she has not yet had to make. She tears a square of foil from the lid of last night's noodles and wraps them in it, twisting both ends, like a sweet for a funeral. When she puts the twist back in her pocket, it is silent. It has never been so silent.

On the left-hand screen Dr Ong has not moved. Her mug has stopped steaming. The towers behind her go on shining in the rain, a long way off and perfectly bright.

"Mei Lin," says Ilse. "The stone on this shelf was a Pesa. It has been plugged in since the twenty-second. Everything we have said to you since Friday, and everything you have read tonight, I have to assume has been heard."

Dr Ong looks into her camera for a long time. Then she folds her hands.

"Noted," she says, and the call drops.
