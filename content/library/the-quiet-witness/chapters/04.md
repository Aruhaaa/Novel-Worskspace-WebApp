# Union City, After Dark

The rice was already done when Rigo got off the bus at Bergenline and Thirty-Second, and he could smell it from the street door: garlic, oregano, the sofrito Yocasta made on Sundays and on any other day she decided the flat needed to smell as if nothing was wrong. He stood at the foot of the stairs with his hand on the rail and let the smell come down to meet him. Three flights, above a phone-repair shop and a botánica and a window of quinceañera dresses that looked like the inside of a wedding cake. His knees counted every step.

A deputy he did not know had driven him from Newark in a grey sedan and let him out at the corner, with a card that had a number on it and a reminder not to discuss his testimony with anyone, his wife included. The sedan was still at the kerb when he looked back from the door. Its engine ran. Its driver was reading something on his knee.

Yocasta did not ask. She took his jacket and hung it on the hook, and the tie he had forgotten to retie she lifted off him with two fingers, as she would lift a thread from a collar, and laid it on the sideboard to be returned to the cousin it belonged to. Then she put a plate in front of him, rice, red beans, a slab of fried cheese, and went back to the stove, and watched him in the dark glass of the window above it, where she thought he could not see her watching.

The table was a ruin of chemistry. Yaritza had her hair in a towel and her phone propped against the salt, and around her lay worksheets, a periodic table with a highlighter run across the halogens, a calculator whose purpose her father could not read from its keys. "Papi, don't touch the moles," she said, without looking up.

"I'm not touching any moles."

"You were going to."

"I was going to touch the salt." He moved the salt. The phone fell over, and she set it up again with the patience of a girl who had been raised by people who broke things lovingly. "Is it going okay?" she said to the periodic table. "At the court."

"I can't say, mija."

"I know you can't say. I'm not asking what. I'm asking if you're okay."

He found that he needed a moment before he could answer, so he cut his cheese into pieces smaller than it needed. "Soy un testigo," he said. "Un testigo no tiene que estar okay. Tiene que estar presente, ¿me entiende?"

"A witness doesn't have to be okay," she translated, for her own amusement, in the voice of someone reading a fortune cookie. "He has to show up."

"That's what I said."

"That's what you said," she agreed, and gave him the small private smile that was the only wage he had been paid in weeks.

On the refrigerator, under a magnet of the Virgen de la Altagracia, hung the paper he looked at every night whether he meant to or not. SANTA ROSA MANAGEMENT. RECEIVED WITH THANKS. OCTOBER. $1,350.00. Purple ink, a rubber stamp set a little crooked, a date. It came in the mail on the third of every month, in a plain envelope with no return address but the name of a building, and Yocasta pinned it up each time. It was the one piece of paper in the house that arrived without anyone asking for something.

He had met the man who made it possible in June. After the ten o'clock Mass, in the lot behind the church where the parish kept its folding tables, his cousin Fello had waved him over to a stranger in a linen jacket with silver hair, who smelled faintly of a cedar closet and looked up at the sky and said it was going to be a hot summer. The stranger took Rigo's hand in both of his. "Señor Salas. Your cousin tells me you are a man of faith." He spoke Spanish like somebody's favourite uncle, with the s's fallen out of it. He did not mention the case. He mentioned the roof of the church, which needed work, and the children, and a fund Mr. Hanlon kept for the families of men who had trouble at the yard, any kind of trouble, because families should not pay for what men do. He said it the way a man says it will rain. Then he walked off across the asphalt toward a car, and Fello, who had been holding a manila envelope the whole time, held it out to Rigo in the Sunday sun.

Nothing in it was money. Money would have been a crime. There was a letter on heavy paper, and a form with Yocasta's name already typed into the blank, and a number to call. Rigo had gone over that conversation on many nights since, sentence by sentence, the way you go over beads, and there was not one word in it, in either language, that a judge could hold against the man.

Yaritza turned a page. A pot lid ticked as it cooled. And the word he had said that morning came back and stood in the kitchen with him.

*Eso.* It had come out of him without his leave. He had opened his mouth in front of the flag and the twelve strangers and the lady with the toothpick, and it had been there, plain as a stone at the bottom of a clear pool. It was the truest thing he had said in three years. Saying it, he had felt the oddest relief, the relief of a man whose dentist has just pulled the tooth that had been talking all night. And then lunch, and the licenciado, and the lesson.

He did not tell that night from start to finish. He had never been able to. He could only hold it in pieces, the way you hold a broken plate up to the light to find which side has the glaze. The cold, first. Crane 9 forty feet above the yard in fog, the glass of the cab sweating on the inside, the red lights on the boom arms gone soft and round. The handheld on his belt, hissing a channel that was supposed to be quiet after eleven. The spreader coming up through the controls into his wrists, a box hanging off four pins above a yard in the dark, thirty tons that answered to the tips of his fingers, even the one that was not there. Diesel and wet steel. A cough, his own. Then the radio spoke, and what it said was two words, *Swing it*, in a voice he had taken orders from for nine years, and after the words there was a name, and after the name there were his own hands, and there he stopped, at the door, with his palm flat against it, like a man outside a room in which someone is sleeping badly.

"Papi. You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing with your fork. You're not eating, you're, like, auditing."

He ate. At the stove Yocasta found something that needed wiping.

After dinner he went into the bathroom, turned the lock and ran the tap. The bathroom was the size of a confessional. It had a cracked mirror, a sticker of the Virgin on the cabinet door, and a window onto the airshaft, up which the neighbours' bachata climbed all night as though the building were a throat. He waited until the water was loud. Then he looked at the man in the mirror, a thick man of forty-seven in a haircut that was not his, and said, very low, "Me dijo: encárgate de él."

It came out clean. That was what he hated. His mouth had been an honest tool all his life, slow, cracked, honest, a spade. Now it did a lie as smoothly as a plumber does a joint. He said it again. "De él." The lips closing at the end like a small door. He said it with the stress where the licenciado had put it. The bracelet knocked against the porcelain each time he gripped the sink, a small dry sound like a tooth. *Without the funeral face.* He arranged his face. It did not look like anyone he knew.

The licenciada Whitcomb had told him, in August, in a room with a bad air conditioner, through the other interpreter, the young one with the ponytail: *I don't need it pretty. I need it true and loud.* He had liked her for that. He had been true this morning, and loud enough, and by two o'clock he had been something else, and he did not have a word for it in either language.

"Rigo?" Yocasta, outside the door, not knocking. "The water has been running a long time."

"I'm washing."

"For ten minutes."

"I am very dirty," he said, and she did not laugh, and went away.

When he came out she was drying the last plate with her back to him and her shoulders high. He sat down at the table in the wreckage of the moles. For a while there was only the squeak of cloth on china. Then Yocasta said, to the cupboard, "The government's letter."

"What about it?"

"The one that says you helped them. Are they going to send it?"

"Yes."

"Rigo."

"The lawyer says yes."

"The lawyer says." She put the plate in the rack. "The lawyer is paid by the hour. And the others, the ones who pay the rent. In nine months they have never been late once. Not one first of the month. Rigo, do you know how many people I have met in my life who never miss?" She turned. She had a laundress's forearms and a face that had got through two countries. "None. That is how many."

"Yocasta."

"I am not asking what you said today. I know you can't say. I'm asking whether the government will keep its word."

He opened his mouth. He wanted to say: it will, it will, the licenciada Whitcomb is a serious woman and she shook my hand. But he had shaken the other one's hand too. "Dios dirá," he said.

"God is not the one who signs." She hung the cloth over the oven door and squared it, and that was the end of that.

Later he sat in the front room with the television on and the sound off, and thought of the interpreter.

He had not meant to. She had been at his left shoulder all day, a presence like a warm radiator, and he had hardly looked at her. But what came back now, with the screen flickering blue on his hands, was her voice. Once in the morning, when she turned the lady lawyer's question about the cranes into Spanish, she had said *usté*, quick, as if she had stepped on a loose board, and then smoothed it back to *usted* before the sentence was over. He had heard it. He had heard, too, the s's she let go after that, in *los contenedores*, in *buenos días*, falling off the ends of her sentences exactly as they fell off at home, in his mother's kitchen, in the mouths of the women outside the church in the village, in the hill country where everything ended a little early. In a day of English she had been the one person in the room who spoke the language of his mother's house. He had called her doña without deciding to.

And you could not lie in front of such a mouth. He did not know where he had got the idea. It was only that a lie told to her would have a sound, and she would hear it ring false the way a bank teller hears a bad coin.

"Papi. Look."

Yaritza had come in with the towel off her hair and her phone in her hand, and she dropped onto the arm of his chair. "Somebody does the courtroom pictures for the news site. With chalk, like it's the Old West. Look."

It was a drawing in coloured pastel, smudged at the edges. The judge sat high in black with his glasses on the end of his nose. Below him a thick man in a blue tie, with hands like two gloves, stared at nothing. Beside the man a woman had been turned half away, a thin woman with a pencil raised and a streak of grey above one ear, her mouth open as if halfway through a word. Behind them stood a tall lawyer with her lips in a line.

"Why does the old judge look at you like that?"

"He looks like that at everybody. He's old."

"No, look. Everybody else is looking at the lawyer. He's looking at you."

The artist had caught it. The head lowered, the eyes over the rim, the pen stopped in the air. A man looking at a bridge before he decides whether to drive across it. Rigo handed the phone back without a word.

"Show your father the interpreter," Yocasta called from the kitchen. "She looks tired."

"La intérprete es de Santiago," Rigo said.

"How do you know?" said Yaritza.

"Por cómo habla." He did not say the rest.

Later, in the dark, with Yocasta breathing beside him and the bachata coming up the shaft, he lay on his back and listened to the building: the pipes, a baby two floors down, the neighbours' argument turning into something else. He thought of the little plastic click he had heard at noon in 5C, before the licenciado came in, from the room next door. A kettle shutting itself off. He had taken it for a guard's lunch. Now it came back with the tiny clarity things have at night, and with it his mother's saying, *las paredes oyen*, which he had always taken for a warning about neighbours.

The wall in 5C had been a thin one. He had put his shoulder to it, once, leaning to the licenciado's whisper, and felt it give.

He lay awake and wondered whether a wall could hear, and if it could, what in God's name it would do with what it knew.
