# The Oath

Loretta Pinckney's text came in at 7:40, while Marisol was crossing Market Street against a light that had not made up its mind. It said what it had said before every witness who mattered, for eleven years: *Big day. Be a pipe.*

She did not answer. She never answered. A pipe carries what is poured into it and keeps nothing, and the highest praise the federal courts had for an interpreter was that twelve people had gone home without remembering she was in the room. Marisol put the phone in her bag, next to the green reporter's pad, and walked the last four blocks to Walnut Street with her mouth shut and her ears open, which was the whole of her warm-up.

By nine-twenty she was in the chair. It stood at the left shoulder of the witness box and a little behind it, where she could hear a witness breathe and the witness could not easily read her face. She laid out her things in the order she always did. The pad, squared to the edge of the shelf. The water glass, to its right. The reading glasses, on the chain her mother had strung from small blue glass beads and, where she ran short, three red ones that she had not minded. Over the jury box the wall clock said 9:24. It ran three minutes fast. Everyone knew and nobody had fixed it, because Judge Okoro had remarked once, mildly, that it kept the lawyers honest.

It was the sixth day of *United States v. Hanlon*, and the room had grown the manners of a long trial. Pale oak. A jury box of fourteen seats, twelve for deciding and two for waiting. At the near defence table sat Gerald Hanlon, who owned Halyard Freight Services, broad and pink and courteous; on the first morning he had brought pastries for the clerks, and he still nodded to each of them by name. Beside him Vincent Lazzaro, silver hair, camel coat folded over the chair, a smell of cedar that reached the interpreter's seat from ten feet away. At the far table Calvin Pryor, in a grey suit and reading glasses, was finishing a letter on a yellow pad in a small even hand. The clerks said it went to his daughter every morning before the jury came in. A pocket Bible lay by his elbow. Next to him Neil Banks, whose suit had been cheap in an earlier decade, shook a tin of almonds once, softly, to find out how many were left.

In the second row of the gallery a woman in a church coat sat with a steel thermos upright in her lap, as she had on every day of the trial. Odessa Cole never drank from it. She held it the way other people hold a hand.

"All rise."

Judge Okoro took the bench as some men take a pew. Sixty-three, clipped grey beard, reading glasses pushed up into his hair, where they never seemed to be wanted or lost. He tapped his pen twice on the blotter and looked down at the interpreter's chair.

"Madam Interpreter. The Court renews your oath before each witness who requires you. Humour an old habit. In both languages, please. A witness is entitled to hear what has been promised on his behalf."

Lucille Granger, the courtroom deputy, read it from a card she had long since stopped needing. "Do you solemnly swear that you will interpret accurately and completely, adding nothing and omitting nothing, so help you God?"

"I do," Marisol said. Then she said it again, in Spanish, to the empty witness box. "Lo juro. Juro interpretar con exactitud y por completo, sin añadir nada y sin omitir nada. Que Dios me ayude."

Her Spanish was good, and she had sanded it for court. It was her mother's Spanish, Santiago under the polish, and the polish held, mostly, except on the long days when the s at the end of a word began to wear thin.

Deputy Marshal Desai brought the witness in by the side door. She was a compact woman with a black braid pinned flat and a scar that split her left eyebrow, and she delivered a man to a witness box the way other people sign for a parcel. Rigoberto Salas was forty-seven, thick through the shoulders, in a blue tie that had been made for a smaller neck. The knot sat high and hard under his chin. The tip of his left ring finger was missing, and a bracelet of dark rosary beads slid up and down his wrist as he raised his right hand. He did not look at the interpreter. Witnesses seldom did. She was the sound he made in English.

She gave him the oath in Spanish, a half-beat behind the clerk, in the clerk's own cadence. *¿Jura usted decir la verdad, toda la verdad y nada más que la verdad?* "Sí, lo juro," Rigo said, and Marisol said, "I do," and he sat, and the pencil on her pad took its first marks.

The notation was her own, built over twenty years from a shorthand manual, her father's union minutes and her bad habits. A circle with a line through it was *world*, and from world everything followed: *all, everywhere, the whole of it*. A slash was *not*. An arrow was *because*, or *toward*, or *therefore*, depending on how it leaned. She never wrote down his words. She let go of the sound and kept the shape, and twenty minutes later the shape would give the words back, in order, with the cough in the right place.

Dana Whitcomb rose with a toothpick in her mouth. She took it out, laid it on the corner of the lectern like a small brown comma, and began. She was tall, with her hair gathered in a clip that looked as if it had been fastened in a taxi, and she wore the navy suit she had worn on days one through five. She asked short questions, each one as though it cost money.

"Mr. Salas. Where did you work?"

Marisol gave it to him in Spanish, level, the question's shape without its tone. "Yo trabajaba en el muelle catorce, en Port Newark," Rigo said, and she turned to the jury and became him. "I worked at Berth 14, at Port Newark. I was a crane operator."

"What is a spreader?"

"Es el marco que agarra la caja por las cuatro esquinas, licenciada."

"It's the frame that grips the box by its four corners, counselor."

Dana moved through the morning like a woman walking through a house she had cleaned. The straddle carriers: tall yellow frames on wheels that stood over a container and walked off with it, "like a gate that has decided to leave," Rigo said, and Marisol said it as he had, and a juror smiled. The stacks, four high. The red lights on the crane booms at night. A diagram on an easel, Berth 14 drawn in marker, with the yard and the water and an X where nobody wanted to look. Container numbers off Exhibit 22, read one character at a time, HLYU 4461207, HLYU 4461208. Numbers were the only place the pad matched the speech. A serial number has no shape, only the number.

The jurors watched Rigo. They always did. Juror 6, the oldest man in the box, a retired postman with a notebook held shut by a rubber band, leaned toward the Spanish a moment before the English arrived, as though the sound alone might tell him something. Nobody looked at her. That was the job, and she wore it well.

"I want to turn to the night of March thirteenth into the fourteenth, 2022," Dana said. "Were you working?"

"Toda la noche."

"All night."

"That night, did you receive a telephone call?"

"A las once y cuarenta y ocho."

"At 11:48." The number went across the well and something in the room sat up. In the second row Odessa Cole did not move.

"Who called you?"

"El señor Pryor."

"Mr. Pryor," Marisol said. At the far table Pryor took off his reading glasses and folded them.

"Was the call in English or in Spanish?"

"En inglés. Yo entiendo el inglés del trabajo."

"In English. I understand the English of the job." It was the truest sentence anyone had said in that courtroom all week, and she handed it over exactly as she had received it.

"What did Mr. Pryor say?"

Rigo's bracelet clicked once against the rail. "Me dijo," he said, and stopped. "Me dijo: encárgate de eso."

She let go of the sound and caught the shape: an arrow, pointing at a small empty box, the mark she used for *that*, a thing indicated and not named. Then she gave it to the jury in his voice. "He told me: take care of it."

Pryor had said it in English first, so the sentence had already crossed one border. Rigo had carried it into Spanish, and now she carried it back. A long way round for four words. At the near table Gerald Hanlon coughed, a short dry sound into his fist, like a man clearing a path. She marked it with a tick. A cough was a thing that had happened in the room, and she took down what happened.

"Take care of what?" Dana said.

"¿De qué?" Marisol said, to Rigo.

"De eso." His voice had gone small. "La queja de Cole. La que él puso por escrito."

"Of that. Cole's complaint. The one he put in writing."

Dana picked up a stapled report from the lectern: four pages, the Bureau's seal across the top, a yellow stripe down one paragraph. "Mr. Salas, in November of 2022 you were interviewed by agents of the FBI. I'm showing you the report of that interview. It says that you told them Mr. Pryor said, quote, 'Take care of him.'" She laid it on the shelf at his elbow, open to the stripe. Marisol, who had been reading upside down for twenty years, read the line without trying. *SALAS stated that PRYOR told him to "take care of him."* "Is that what you told them?"

Marisol put the question into Spanish, and the report's sentence inside it, carefully, so that the pronoun came through whole. Rigo bent over the page as a man bends over a well to see how deep it goes. He did not read English, not that kind. He found the one word and moved his lips on it. *Him.*

"No, licenciada," he said. "Eso."

"No, counselor," Marisol said. "It."

The room made no sound at all. Dana went very still, and she did not look at the jury, which was how Marisol knew how much she wanted to. At the far table Neil Banks half rose, the almond tin rattling in his pocket, like a man who has watched a gift fall into his lap and suspects a trap. He looked at his client, at the bench, and sat. At the near table Vincent Lazzaro studied the cuff of his shirt, and did not move.

Judge Okoro tapped his pen twice. Marisol, who counted silences for a living, watched his lips. One. Two. Three.

"Ladies and gentlemen, we will take the luncheon recess. You will not discuss this case among yourselves or with anyone else, and you will not form any opinion until it has been given to you. Back at two." The clock above the jury box said 12:23.

The room emptied the way a church empties, in twos and threes, talking low. The jurors went out by their own door. A paralegal lifted the stapled report off the shelf, and Marisol watched the yellow stripe go until it was gone. Desai took Rigo out by the side door with a hand not quite on his elbow. Two other deputies came for Calvin Pryor.

He stood between them in his grey suit with his reading glasses folded in his hand, and he looked at the interpreter. Of the forty or so people who had been in that room, he was the only one who did. He looked at her across the empty well, level and patient, the way a man looks at a gauge he has been told to trust. Then the deputies turned him, and the side door took him.

She sat alone with the pad open on her knee. Her mouth still held the shape of the word she had given the jury. *It.* It took one breath and no effort, a vowel and a tap of the tongue behind the teeth. Its neighbour, *him*, ended with the lips closing, like a small door. Between the two sounds a man would go home or would not go home, and everything that had happened in a yard at two in the morning would be one thing or another thing. She had said it correctly. She was certain she had said it correctly. She closed her lips, slowly, and the shape did not go.
