# The Plasterboard

Before it was the interpreters' alcove it had been a closet for mops, and the building had never entirely agreed to the change. The deep sink was still there, capped with a board, and on the board sat an electric kettle, a jar of manzanilla tea bags and a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST AUNT, which nobody had ever claimed. There was one chair, bentwood, liberated from a jury room under some earlier administration. If she let it, the place smelled of pine cleaner and old rope. On the east side stood the partition: two sheets of plasterboard on studs, painted the colour of a bandage, a wall in the sense that a curtain is a door. Beyond it was Witness Room 5C.

Marisol came in at 12:34 by her phone, shut the door, and sat. She unwrapped the pan con queso Nilda had pressed on her that morning, a pan de agua split and warmed with a slab of white cheese that wept a little into the paper. She did not go to the cafeteria on trial days. The defence bar took the long table by the window in a pack and discussed their witnesses at the volume of advertisements, and in eleven years she had heard enough about other people's cases to fill a library she was forbidden to open. In the alcove nobody told her anything, which was why she loved it.

She chewed and did not think about the morning, with the effort of a woman not looking at a dent in her own car. *Eso.* It. A pronoun with nobody inside it. Somewhere on six, Dana Whitcomb was explaining to someone why the case had come apart in her hands, or why it had not.

The kettle clicked off with a sound like a knuckle. She poured the water over a tea bag. Through the partition a chair took a man's weight and let it go, and then a murmur began, low and even, a rhythm she had known before she knew words. The Our Father, in the Spanish of the hills, and under it a faint ticking: beads. She felt the small shame of having overheard a prayer and moved her chair a few inches away. The alcove was five feet by seven. There was nowhere to move to.

Out in the corridor, a few feet from her door, a handheld radio squelched and a dispatcher read out a string of numbers. Then a phone buzzed against a belt.

"Papa?" said Deputy Marshal Desai.

It was not the voice she used for *Noted*. It was high and quick and very young. Then, lower, in a language Marisol did not have, the rising syllables of a question asked three times. A stool scraped back. Boots went away down the corridor at a pace no deputy uses on duty, toward the stairwell, and Marisol looked at her phone out of the habit of timing things. 12:41.

She listened to the corridor. A post that is manned has a sound, and she had never noticed it until it stopped. The silence outside 5C was the silence of a place where someone ought to be standing.

It was none of her business. She drank her tea. She was a pipe, and pipes do not wonder who is supposed to be guarding the door.

At 12:43 the latch of 5C ticked over. Leather soles on linoleum, not the rubber squeak of a marshal's. The door closed again, softly, and a voice she knew from across the well said, warm and quick and very close to the board, "Compai."

She had sat six days in a room with Vincent Lazzaro, and he had never once let her hear him speak Spanish. Now it came through the plasterboard as if the wall were a kitchen door: Cibao Spanish, the real thing, the Spanish of the hills outside Santiago, the s dropped from the ends of words and the final l and r turned to i like her mother's. He had learned it, she thought, at a table, from a woman who had been patient and been paid.

"Compai. Esta mañana te hundiste."

Her head made the English on its own, as it always did, a second voice in the same ear. *Buddy. This morning you sank.* From there the Spanish and her English ran together, and she stopped being able to tell which of them she was hearing.

The alcove door had a hinge that sang. Every interpreter in the building knew it, and the clerks made jokes about it. If she stood now and went out, the whole corridor would hear her go, and so would the room next door. If she stayed, she would hear everything. Nobody had offered her a choice. The hinge had made it.

A chair. Then Rigo's voice, low, thick, unmistakable. "Yo dije lo que él me dijo, licenciado."

*I said what he told me.*

"You said what is left in your head three years later, compai. In November of twenty-twenty-two you sat with the agent, with everything fresh, and you gave him an answer. It's on paper, with a date. Mi hermano, nobody is asking you to lie. I'm asking you not to let your own memory betray you."

Her hand was moving. She noticed it the way you notice you have been humming. The pad lay open on her knee, and the pencil had made a circle with a line through it, then an arrow, then a slash. She had decided nothing. It was twenty years of habit, the same habit that had once taken down a dying man's will in a Hackensack ward, forty minutes of his Spanish and his niece's lawyer's English, because the words were not hers and so she could not afford to lose them.

"This afternoon she asks you again," Lazzaro said. "Gently, you'll see. Not like this morning. And you say *de él*. Firm. Like saying your name. Say it."

"De él."

"Again. Without the funeral face."

"De él."

"Bueno. And if she puts the paper in front of you, you say, *Ay, licenciada, I got confused this morning.* Say that."

"Me confundí esta mañana."

"Beautiful. Like a prayer. And don't look at the interpreter when you say it. Look at the lady lawyer, or look at the rail. A jury doesn't like to watch a man talk to the help. Tomorrow, when I ask you something, look at me. Breathe. You'll find the answers are easy."

The pan con queso went cold in her lap. Behind the board nobody spoke, and the ticking of the beads started up, and stopped.

"Pero doña," Rigo said. "La intérprete. Ella lo oyó tó."

*But the lady interpreter. She heard it all.*

Marisol felt her own title go by on the wrong side of a wall, like hearing her name called in somebody else's house.

Lazzaro laughed, softly, the laugh of a man asked whether the tablecloth is a witness. "Mi hermano. An interpreter is furniture. Does a chair remember who sat on it? You say a thing, she makes it English, she goes home to her dinner. That is the whole of her job. I've watched her all week, and she's very good."

It was a compliment. She had been working toward it for eleven years.

"¿Y si ella dice algo?"

"What would she say? She says what you say. You say *de él*, she says *him*. She has no choice in it. She isn't allowed one." A pause, the creak of a good chair taking a good coat. "And nobody is going to believe you against a paper from the FBI. Nadie te va a creer contra un papel del FBI. Not you, not her, not the saints on your wrist."

In the margin her hand made a small hook each time he said the name for brother. She noticed the second hook before she noticed the first.

Rigo said something she could not catch. Pryor's name was in it.

"Pryor made a telephone call," Lazzaro said. "It is on the phone company's paper too. He is a grown man with a Bible, compai. Let him carry his own call."

"Now. Something else. Yocasta is well? The apartment, on Bergenline?"

"Bien," Rigo said. "Gracias a Dios."

"Santa Rosa gets its money on the first of the month. Thirteen hundred and fifty dollars." He said the number in English, the way people do who have said it often to themselves. "The Fund pays on the first of every month, through March. Hasta marzo. That was agreed. This is not a gift, mi hermano. A gift you can lose. This is family, and in a family nobody asks what the rent is for."

"¿Y después de marzo?"

"After March this is a bad memory, and we will talk about April in April."

She wrote the figure plainly, 1350, and under it *hasta marzo*. Numbers had no shape to keep. Only the number.

From the other side of the board came a sound: a breath dragged in wet and held, as a hand is clapped over a mouth. It happened once. It was the quietest weeping she had ever heard, and she had interpreted at sentencings.

"Ya," Lazzaro said, much more gently. "Ya, compai. Breathe. Yaritza is sixteen, no? A girl that age needs a door that locks."

Marisol had stopped chewing. She did not remember stopping. She sat with a mouthful of cold bread in a closet that smelled of rope and pine, and a stranger's child's name went through the plasterboard like a nail through a card, and her pencil kept moving.

She wanted, with a strong clean wish, to stand up and open the door. To step into the corridor and tell the empty air that the interpreter required a word with the Court. She saw it whole: the singing hinge, the deputies, Lazzaro's face turning to her, polite and mildly interested, a man hearing that a coat has been left behind. Then she saw the second half of the picture, which was a lawyer in a camel coat explaining that the interpreter had been eating lunch in a closet and must have misheard. She stayed in the chair.

"Eat something. Rest, mi hermano. Five minutes." The chair creaked again. "You were not wrong to be afraid this morning. You were only early."

Rigo said something she could not catch, and then, clearly, "Sí, licenciado."

The latch. The door opened, and in the corridor Lazzaro said "Afternoon!" in English, brightly, to nobody at all, and went away down the hall whistling two bars of something from a musical, his leather soles unhurried on the linoleum.

Almost at once, boots again, at a half-run. It was not the pace of a woman coming back from a phone call. It was the pace of one who has just remembered what she left. They stopped outside 5C. A hand tried the handle, once, the way you try a door you already know is shut. A knuckle rapped. "Mr. Salas? All good in there?" A pause, and a hoarse "Sí. Bien, gracias." Then the clipboard came down off its hook with a clack, and a pen scratched, and Marisol heard the long, shaky exhale of a person who believes that nothing has happened.

She stayed where she was. Her mother used to say that Santiago was a city with a wall for every secret and a hole in every wall, and she had meant the neighbours. Two sheets of plasterboard stood between the interpreter and the other room, with a hand's width of air between them, and Marisol had never before thought about how thin that was. In the next room the beads began again, one at a time, and a man who had been told he was only early began to say a prayer for the second time.

The page was nearly full. She wrote the last symbol, an arrow aimed at a wall, and the pencil broke on the stroke. She took the stub between finger and thumb and kept writing: the handle, the clipboard, the whistle, the time.
