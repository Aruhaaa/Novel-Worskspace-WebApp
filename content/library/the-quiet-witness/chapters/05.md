# Pan con Queso

At ten past six on Ferry Street the Portuguese radio and the Dominican radio were already arguing across the awnings. One played fado out of the door of a fish shop, the other played bachata from an upstairs window, and neither gave an inch while between them the streetlights went off one at a time. The Ironbound woke the way it always did, in two languages and in a third, the language of delivery vans, whose doors let out a long hydraulic sigh at every kerb. Somewhere a shutter went up with a noise like a cough.

Panadería La Espiga had been awake since four. Nilda Cabral stood behind the counter in a hairnet and a cardigan, floury to the elbows, small and round and built to take a collision with a rack of trays, and she had the paper bag in her hand before Marisol's boots had finished on the step.

"I haven't said anything," Marisol said.

"That is what I'm telling you." Nilda looked at her across the glass. It was the look she gave a loaf when she thought it might not have risen: not unkind, only thorough. "You have a face like yesterday's bread. Hard on the outside, and inside, nobody home."

"Nilda."

"Pan con queso. Café con leche, I put the lid on already. Sit. Eat. Here, on the bench, in front of me, where I can watch it go in."

"I'll eat at the court."

"You will eat at the court a sandwich made by a man who hates sandwiches." She pushed the bag across the counter. Her eyes dropped to Marisol's hand on it, the left, with the ink on the middle finger, and came up again. "Have you called your brother?"

"I'm going to be late."

"I'm only asking. A person can ask. Your mother never said what she wanted either. Twenty-two years of Saturdays, and she would point." She turned to slap a tray of pastelitos into the case and said to the pastelitos, "Eat."

Marisol ate on the bench outside the shoe-repair, under her own windows. Mr. Teixeira, who owned the shop and the keys to her radiator, lifted a hammer at her through the glass and went back to beating a heel. The bread was warm and the cheese was salt and this time she tasted it. She had not slept. She had lain in the dark conducting the Code in her head, article by article, until the radiator came on and spoiled the quiet.

The card was in the back pocket of the pad. They had handed it out at orientation in 2014, laminated, with a hole punched for a lanyard that no one wore, and she had signed it, and renewed under it every year since, and had never once read it as a text. She read it now.

> CONFIDENTIALITY. The interpreter shall hold in strict confidence all information learned in the course of an assignment, whether or not privileged, and shall disclose it to no person. The interpreter may address the Court only on matters touching the accuracy of the interpretation.

She read it three times, the way you read a lease for the clause that lets you out. *Whether or not privileged.* Privilege protected a lawyer and his client. This protected everyone: the one who coached and the one who was coached, the lie and the person who heard it told. *Disclose it to no person.* She looked for the word *unless* and it was not there. Whoever wrote the card had imagined every kind of error an interpreter might make and no kind of crime she might hear. The one exception it allowed was to the Court, and the Court had asked her yesterday whether it was a linguistic matter, and she had said no.

At the other end of the bench a man in a flat cap turned a page of a Portuguese newspaper. "Bom dia," he said.

"Bom dia," she said, and put the card away.

* * *

The cafeteria on the third floor had been designed by someone who believed hunger was a character flaw. At ten past eight it held a few clerks, a marshal asleep upright over a doughnut, and the smell of the grill being scraped for later. At the till a woman in a hairnet took a pair of tongs from a hook and turned the only egg salad sandwich in the glass case a quarter turn, so that it should not develop a bad side.

"Don't," said Loretta Pinckney, from the corner table by the radiator, without looking up. "That sandwich has been in that case since the Obama administration."

"It's a different sandwich every day."

"Is it? Has anyone ever seen one sold? I believe it's the same sandwich. I believe it has tenure."

She was five feet and not much more, with silver braids pinned in a crown and a mustard cardigan buttoned to the chin with leather buttons the size of cookies. A paper cup of tea steamed in front of her, the tag of the bag hanging over the rim. She had grown up in Charleston and a good part of the Canal Zone and she spoke both Englishes and a Spanish that sounded like a ship's radio, and in eleven years Marisol had never once seen her hurry. Loretta moved a chair out with her foot. "Sit. You look like you slept in a filing cabinet."

"I didn't sleep."

"Mm-hm." It was the first of several. Loretta's *mm-hm* came in at least nine varieties, and Marisol had spent a decade learning to tell them apart. This was number four: *I see you, and I will let you get to it in your own time.*

They talked about the fog, which had come in off the Meadowlands overnight and sat on Newark like a lid. They talked about Banks, who was due to cross at nine-thirty ("God help the transcript"), and about Loretta's knees, which were forecasting rain. Loretta folded a sugar packet in half, and in half again, and did not open it. Her hands were always doing something small and exact while the rest of her sat still.

"Can I ask you something?" Marisol said. "Hypothetically."

"Mm-hm." Number seven. Wary.

"An interpreter is in a corridor. On a break. She hears something. Not on the record. Two people, and one of them is a lawyer."

"Hears what."

"Something she wasn't meant to."

"And she's on the case."

"Hypothetically."

Loretta took the tea bag out with two fingers and laid it on the lid. "Go on."

"That's all. That's the hypothetical."

"That is an awfully short hypothetical."

"I'm trying to be brief."

"Mm-hm." Number two, which meant that Loretta did not believe a syllable of it and was going to be courteous about that for as long as it took. She turned the cup a quarter turn on the table, as the woman at the till had turned the sandwich. "Well. Hypothetically, she keeps it to herself. The Code isn't subtle on this. She goes on interpreting. She doesn't repeat it to her coordinator, or her sister, or her priest. She does her job."

"And if what she heard bears on whether the record is accurate?"

"Then she may raise a linguistic problem with the Court. If it is one."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then she hasn't got one." Loretta looked at the sugar packet. It was the size of a postage stamp now. "Let me tell you about Delia Renko."

Marisol waited.

"2009. Spanish and Polish, which in Newark gets you the shipping offices and half the bakeries in Elizabeth. Good ear. Bad stapler. She was working a fraud trial for Judge Halloran, and on a break she was in a stairwell, and she heard a defence lawyer tell his client what he was going to say when they got back. Not what the truth was. What he was going to say." Loretta's voice stayed flat as a table. "It was raining. She had her good shoes on, she told me afterward, as if the shoes mattered. She did it properly, in writing, through the clerk, with a date and a time. The judge thanked her. Nobody said another word about it. A month later the Administrative Office wrote to her about *concerns regarding the maintenance of professional boundaries*. Six weeks after that she was off the list."

"For what?"

"Nobody says for what. That is how it's done. It's never for what." The sugar packet had been unfolded and smoothed flat on the table, and Loretta began to fold it again. "She sells insurance in Cranford now. She says she likes it. She says people tell you what they want."

"And the lawyer?"

"Oh, he's still on Broad Street. His name's in gold leaf on the glass. He sends a fruit basket to the courthouse every December." She picked up the tea. "I've eaten the pears."

Marisol watched her hands. They never stopped, and they never hurried. Loretta knew the year, and the judge's name, and that it had rained, and what shoes Delia Renko had worn. She knew how many weeks. *She was told,* Marisol thought. And then, because she listened for a living, and a story is never only what it says: *Or she was there.*

"Why did she do it?" she said. "Renko. If she knew."

"She didn't know." Loretta's mouth moved a little at one corner. "She thought the Code was about what it said. It's a common mistake. People who read for a living make it all the time."

For a little while neither of them said anything. The marshal woke, looked at his doughnut with dislike, and went back to sleep. Down at the till the woman turned the sandwich again.

Then Loretta did what Marisol had not expected. She lifted her eyes and looked at her, properly, over the rim of the cup, for as long as it took the steam to clear. "Mm-hm," she said. It was number nine. Marisol had heard it only once before, when a rookie had asked whether a glass of wine on a lunch break was strictly forbidden. It meant: *I know. I am choosing not to.*

"Loretta, I haven't said—"

"Good. Keep not having." Loretta set the cup down with both hands. "I'm going to finish my tea. I'm not going to ask who, or which case, or which room. If you tell me, I will have to do something with it, and I have forty-one interpreters, a budget the size of that sandwich, and a pension I intend to live long enough to collect. I'm sixty-four years old. I have a knee that tells me when the Fed is going to raise rates. Do you understand me?"

"I understand."

"Do you? Because you have the face of someone who hears a bell and is looking for the rope."

"Cross is at nine-thirty," Marisol said. "I should go up."

"You should. Banks and then the other one. Pace yourself, it'll be a long day with a short man." Loretta stood, which did not take her very far. She gathered her cup and the folded square of sugar and her cardigan around her, and stopped with one hand on the back of Marisol's chair. Her voice, when it came, was lower and without any music in it at all. "Whatever it is, kid, don't carry it into my building."

She went out between the tables with her small, unhurried step, and the marshal did not wake.

Marisol sat on. At the till, the woman in the hairnet took up her tongs. In the bag at Marisol's feet, behind the card with its hole for a lanyard, lay the green pad, and in the pad a page she had written through a wall. She put her hand down and felt it through the canvas, the stiff cardboard of the cover, the spiral, the small known shape of it.

It was already inside.
