# The Cinnamon Toothpick

The government's trial room on six smelled of cold pizza, toner and the first hot dust of the radiators, and at 6:50 on Tuesday morning Dana Whitcomb was the only living thing in it.

She had slept four hours and come in at six with her hair wet and a cinnamon toothpick already between her teeth. She had quit cigarettes the winter she made AUSA and had never quit the shape of the habit. On the long table in front of her lay the daily transcript in its blue binder, Monday's pages, flagged in three colours, and above the table, held to the wall by blue painter's tape, hung her father's calendar.

It was a Port Authority calendar from 1998, twelve photographs of freighters at dusk, and under every date, in pencil, in the block capitals he used for everything, were his notes. A vessel. A berth. A tonnage. A small tick for each box he had counted. He had been a checker at the Elizabeth terminal for thirty-one years, standing out in all weathers with a clipboard and a flashlight, matching the number on the steel to the number on the paper. She had grown up in Bayonne with the port coming in at her bedroom window: the chain-rattle, then the long boom, like a door slammed in another house. She still slept badly in quiet places.

The radiator knocked like someone who had forgotten his key. She turned to page 112.

*Q. What did Mr. Pryor say to you? A. (Through the Interpreter) He told me: take care of him.* That was 2:07 in the afternoon, and it was the line the murder count stood on. Forty pages earlier, at 11:51 in the morning: *He told me: take care of it.* One word. She had been so glad of the second answer that she had not asked about the first. At half past four she had been composing her closing in the car on the Turnpike. Now, with the room quiet, she asked.

She had asked herself once already, in the car, whether anything could have happened over lunch, and had answered, because it was easier, that nothing could have. Salas had sat in 5C with a deputy outside the door. Witnesses fell to pieces and came back; she had watched a man weep through breakfast and give the best testimony of his life by three. Nerves. She wrote *nerves?* in the margin, and put the question mark on the end of it like a hat on a hook.

There were two other things from the afternoon that she did not write down. The first was the interpreter, who had stood up in the middle of the answer and asked for a moment, and had looked, for the space of one breath, like a woman who has tasted something in her soup. The judge had asked if it was a linguistic matter. She had said no and sat. Dana had thought *nerves* again, and had been grateful. The second was Okoro himself, who had looked at Salas over his glasses for what felt like a minute. A man listening hard, she had told herself. A man listening very hard.

At 7:05 the door banged and Special Agent Kyle Gaffney came in with two coffees and the step of a man whose week was going well. He was thirty-six, broad through the chest, with a haircut like a hedge and a wedding ring he turned when he was pleased. He set the larger cup in front of her without asking how she took it, and beside it a blueberry muffin the size of a fist, in a paper bag gone translucent with butter. He had known how she took her coffee for two years, and that she would not eat the muffin and would not be able to leave it alone. It was one of the things about him that made it hard to say what was wrong.

"Morning, counselor. Heard we had a day."

"We had a morning and an afternoon, and they didn't match."

"He got nervous." Kyle dropped into the chair opposite and stretched out his legs. "They do. Put a guy like Salas in front of twelve strangers and a flag and the first thing out of him comes out sideways. By two o'clock he'd found his feet. That's all the afternoon was."

"He said *eso* like a label, Kyle. He didn't hesitate."

"So he said something. Then he told you what he told me."

"Say more."

"What's to say?" He grinned. He had a very good grin. "I was in the room. I know what he said."

She did not answer. She drank the coffee and rolled the toothpick to the other side of her mouth. On the wall behind Kyle's head, the October of 1998 showed a freighter the colour of a bruise going out under a sky like the inside of a shell, and under the fourteenth, in pencil, her father had written one vessel's name, crossed it out, and put the right one beside it.

"Play it for me," she said.

"114A?"

"The place."

He had the file open before she finished the sentence. Exhibit 114A was fifty-one minutes of a man and a recorder in a conference room at the Bureau's Newark office on the sixteenth of November 2022; the squad's paralegal had typed it up in thirty-one pages, and Kyle knew the timestamp by heart. "Twenty-two forty," he said, and turned the laptop toward her.

A vent hummed. A chair creaked. Somebody set down a cup. Then a man's voice, hoarse, dragging, a voice that had been up all night and meant to be up all week, said something rapid and low in Spanish, and a second voice, slower, pitched the way people speak to a foreigner or a dog, came in over the top of it. "Pero... ¿de él? ¿De Cole?"

She knew those two words. She had learned them in August.

And the first voice again, fast, flattened by exhaustion, almost kind. "Sí, sí, lo que uté diga."

Kyle stopped it. "There. 'Yes, yes, whatever you say.' He's agreeing."

"'Whatever you say' is agreeing?"

"It's yes, yes. Twice. It's a guy saying yes to his own memory with a federal agent in front of him." He spread his hands. "What do you want, a notarised nod?"

"Play it again."

He played it again. She watched him while it ran, and Kyle was watching the screen the way a man watches a fireworks display he has paid for, not worried at all. The thirty-one pages said *usted*. The tape said something shorter, with the end bitten off.

"What's that word? 'Uté.'"

"Cibao. Hill country, outside Santiago. They eat their s's." He shrugged. "Half the Dominicans I've interviewed say it. Like a guy from Brooklyn saying *foist*. It's accent, Dana. It's the same word."

"You're sure."

"I sat in that room. I know what he meant."

"How's your Spanish, Kyle?"

"Good enough. Two semesters at Quantico, a summer in Salamanca, and my wife's people are from Quito. I've done two hundred of these."

"Salamanca is Spain."

"Spanish is Spanish." He said it lightly, with the grin, and she let it go by, because it was early and he was usually right and because the muffin was getting cold.

She wanted it to be agreement. That was the plain fact, and she looked at it as she would have looked at any exhibit. She wanted it so badly that she could feel the wanting, as one feels a draught, from some particular direction. In August she had stopped the clip at this same place and run it back, and listened to the three words under the three words, a tiredness in the voice that she could not translate and did not like. She had written something in the margin and turned the page. The trial date was fixed. The indictment was the indictment.

"Fine," she said. "Thank you."

"You want me to talk to Salas again? Settle him down before Banks?"

"No." It came out faster than she meant. "No. Nobody talks to him. He's on the stand."

"Sure. Right." He stood, finished his coffee, crumpled the cup. At the door he turned, and the grin had a little tenderness in it, which was worse. "Trust me, boss. We got this."

When he was gone she sat for a while with the toothpick going slowly from side to side.

In the front cover of the binder, behind the plastic sleeve, she kept a photograph. Reynard Cole stood on a quay in a hi-vis vest with a clipboard held up like a trophy, grinning at someone just out of frame. The someone, his widow had told her, was a woman who had just said he looked ridiculous. The complaint he filed was in evidence, four lines on a Halyard form, written in the neat print of a man who expected somebody to read it. Odessa Cole had come to the second row on the first day of trial and had taken the same seat every morning since, a steel thermos upright in her lap. She never drank from it. Dana had asked her once, in the second week, what was in it. "Coffee," Mrs. Cole had said. "Made the way he took it. I can't drink it, and I can't pour it out." On the day of the indictment, in January, in the corridor outside the grand jury room, she had taken Dana's hand in both of hers. "Just tell them what happened," she had said. And Dana had said, "I will, Mrs. Cole. I'll make sure someone answers for it."

She had learned, years ago, never to promise a result. She had promised one anyway.

The Chief of the Criminal Division had told her in July, over a salad he did not finish, that the Deputy Chief's door would open in January, and that the people who sat behind it were people who had never lost a case they chose to try. He had said it kindly, as one man tells another where a stream is shallow. And there was Judge Okoro, who liked a clean file the way some men like a clean shirt, and whose reversal rate was spoken of on six in the tones people use for weather in the Arctic. A verdict on all counts, upheld on appeal, would be the kind of file that opens doors. A mistrial in week two would be the kind that closes them.

She pulled the legal pad toward her and uncapped her pen.

*Certified retranslation?*

She looked at it. It would cost a week, at least, and a motion, and a month of Neil Banks asking the jury why the Government had needed a second opinion about its own agent. It would put in Okoro's mind, at the one moment she needed him to stop thinking, the idea that the Government was unsure. And it would tell her what Kyle had already told her. She knew that. She knew exactly how it would come out.

She drew a line through it. Then a second, then a third, until it was a bar, until it was a thing a person could not have read.

On the wall her father's October went on being October. Outside, very faintly, from the river or from the next block or from fifty years ago, she heard a crane let something down on a hard floor, the chain-rattle and the long boom. Behind her back teeth the cinnamon toothpick, which she had been biting without noticing, went with a small dry crack.

She spat the two halves into the wastebasket, took a fresh one from the box in her drawer, and went down to court.
