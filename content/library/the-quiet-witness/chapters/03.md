# Faithful Rendering

Rigo Salas came back from lunch with his tie loosened by a finger's width and his eyes on the floor, and Marisol, who had spent the recess in a closet with the reason, took her chair and did not look at him. Not looking was a skill. She had learned it from the families of the accused, who watched interpreters hungrily, as if a face could be bribed.

He did not look at her either. All morning he had spoken to Dana, to the jury, to the middle air. Now he spoke to the rail of the witness box, and he examined its joints with the attention of a man checking a weld.

Desai put him in the box and went to stand against the wall by the side door, as she had all week, but there was something wrong with how she did it. Her face had a scrubbed look. On the back of her left hand, where she wrote her times in blue ballpoint, a column of small numerals had been rubbed with a thumb until it smeared into a bruise. Marisol noticed it and then, on purpose, did not notice it. A woman who wrote on her own skin and then rubbed it out was none of the interpreter's business. Not much in the building was.

The clock said 2:02. It was 1:59 in the world. "Mr. Salas, you remain under oath," Judge Okoro said, and Marisol put it into Spanish, *Sigue usted bajo juramento*, and Rigo nodded at the rail.

Dana stood with the stapled report in her left hand, as she had before lunch, but she held it against her thigh now like something she was not proud of. "Mr. Salas. I want to go back to the telephone call. Take your time. I would rather have it right than quick, and so would everyone in this room. This morning you said one thing, and the report says another. I'm not asking you to pick whichever is easier for anybody. I'm asking you what happened."

Marisol rendered it, every clause, and nothing else. It was a fair question, fairly put. Dana had held the door open as far as a prosecutor can hold it, and she stood back from it now with the look of a woman who has done what she could. At the government table Special Agent Gaffney, the case agent, a broad man with a haircut like a hedge, had been sitting with his arms folded and a sleepy, pleased look. He uncrossed them.

"What did Mr. Pryor say to you?"

Rigo looked at the rail. "Me dijo," he said. A breath. "Encárgate de él."

It was a good clean word, one syllable with the little accent mark that tells English readers where to put their weight, and it had been practised. She could hear that it had been practised, the way a coin is smooth from hands. It came out of him without the hesitation that had gone before the other word, with no stop and no click of the bracelet. A man reading a label.

Her mouth had the English ready before the Spanish was over. *He told me: take care of him.* Seven syllables, and the last was the whole trouble.

She went through the Code the way she would walk through a house after a noise. Accurately, completely, impartially. Nothing added, nothing omitted, nothing explained. She was not a witness. She was not counsel. She was not the Court. She could ask the witness to repeat an answer she had not caught, and she had caught it. She could ask for the Court's help with a word she could not render, and this was one of the smallest words in the language. The Code gave her one door to the bench, and the door said *language*: a word with no equivalent, a dialect she couldn't place, an error of her own. A lie was not a linguistic matter. A lie was a lie in every language she spoke, and carrying it faithfully across was what she was paid for.

She stood.

It is a small thing, standing. Interpreters do it perhaps twice a year. A juror's pen stopped. Dana turned her head.

"The interpreter requests a moment."

Judge Okoro's glasses came down out of his hair and onto his nose. "Is it a linguistic matter, Madam Interpreter?"

She looked across the well, because that was where her eyes went, to the near table. Vincent Lazzaro was studying the signet ring on his little finger. He turned it a quarter turn with his thumb and tilted it to the window as though it had said something in poor taste. He did not look up. In six days she had learned to read the room by what it declined to do, and what Lazzaro declined to do just then was the loudest thing in it.

"No, Your Honor."

Okoro tapped his pen. Once. Twice. She could see him counting in the set of his beard. Three.

"Then please render the answer."

She sat. She put her hand flat on the pad. "He told me: take care of him."

Her voice was level, the stress where he had put it. She gave the word to the jury in his skin, and it left her mouth the way a coin leaves a pocket in a crowd, without her feeling it go.

The court reporter, Joyce Ferrante, took it without lifting her eyes. Her hands ran on over the keys like a small tireless animal, and by the time Marisol finished the sentence it existed for good, in a font, with a page and a line number. A thing said aloud can be argued with. A thing in the transcript can only be quoted.

Judge Okoro looked at the witness over the tops of his glasses. It was a long look, and a quiet one. Rigo, studying the rail, did not meet it. In the box a younger juror, a dental hygienist who had told the court at voir dire that she hated confrontation, took the word in and looked at Calvin Pryor for the first time all week.

Juror 6 opened his rubber-banded notebook, wrote a single word, and underlined it twice with a ruler's patience. She could not read what he had written, and did not need to. A man who had delivered the mail for forty years knew better than most what one wrong digit does to an address.

"Objection." Neil Banks was on his feet, and for a second nothing else came up with him. "Your Honor, the, the witness, this morning he, it's been, that's asked and, and answered. This morning he said, he said the, he said —"

"Mr. Banks," Okoro said, "take whatever time you need. You will not need it for the ruling. The record is the record, and the jury has the same memory you and I do. Overruled."

Banks sat. The almond tin came out of his pocket, was looked at, and went back.

"Thank you, Mr. Salas," Dana said. She did not use the report. She laid it face down on the lectern. Her toothpick lay beside it, and she picked that up, put it in her mouth, and then, as though she had caught herself, took it out again. She carried him through the next forty minutes, the cranes, the police, the morning after, with the care of a woman walking a full tray across a crowded room, and Marisol rendered every question and every answer and afterward could not have told you one of them. Her voice did what it always did. Under it, somewhere behind the breastbone, a thing that had sat quietly for eleven years in the part of her that kept the rules got up, looked around, and began, very politely, to pace. He had not known she was behind the wall. He could not know that she was the one person in the room who understood what that word had cost him to learn, and that she was handing it to twelve strangers for him, polished, in his own voice, and could not even say that she had heard him weep.

At the government table Gaffney had folded his arms again. He nodded once, slightly, at the carpet, the nod of a man whose forecast has held.

At ten past four by the clock, Banks rose to ask leave to begin his cross. "Y-yes, Your Honor, I'd ask the, I'd ask —"

"You will not be cross-examining this witness in the last twenty minutes of my day, Mr. Banks. Nine-thirty tomorrow."

The deputies came for Pryor. As they turned him toward the side door he looked at the gallery. In the first row behind the defence table sat a girl of seventeen in a school blazer, her hair scraped back hard, with a woman in a church hat at her elbow. "Daddy," the girl said, without sound, with her mouth only. Then, before his eyes could reach her, she turned her face into her aunt's sleeve, as if she had said something she had no right to. Pryor's gaze touched the place where she had been, and the door took him.

Marisol packed her bag in the order she had unpacked it. Pad. Glasses. Pencil, the stub, in its own pocket. She left the water glass for the clerk. She took the stairs down all five flights so that no one would get into a lift with her and say *long day*, and she came out into the lobby with its marble and its brass, where the afternoon filers were queueing at the metal detectors with their cartons of paper. Her phone buzzed against the pad. *How did it go?* Loretta, from the interpreters' office, who read the daily list and would already have heard that a witness had turned. Marisol let it lie in the dark of the bag.

At the Walnut Street doors a man was holding one open for her. He was small and trim and somewhere past fifty, in a raincoat the colour of wet cement, damp across the shoulders though the rain had stopped at noon. A steel thermos was tucked under his arm, and the cap was in his hand, with a thread of steam standing up out of it. Behind his left ear sat a small beige hearing aid. His face was mild and pleasantly forgettable, the face of a man who has spent a career making sure that it was.

"After you, Ms. Quintanilla," he said.

"Thank you," Marisol said, because the body does manners before the mind does. "Mind the third step, it's greasy when the fog comes in," he added, and she went through the door and down two steps and a third, carefully, before the name caught up with her.

Nobody had said it in that building all day. The judge called her Madam Interpreter. The clerks called her Ms. Q. Loretta's text had called her nothing. She turned, and the man was already crossing toward Market with his thermos, unhurried, a man with all afternoon and a use for it. He did not look back. He didn't need to.

Down on the pavement the girl in the school blazer was being steered toward Market by the woman in the church hat. "Not here, baby," the woman was saying. "Not on the steps." The girl nodded with her chin up, the way you nod when you have decided to cry later.

Marisol sat down on the granite beside the nearest pillar, where the stone gave off the day's last cold, and took out the pad. Six pages back lay the page she had written through the wall, and she did not turn to it. Her hands were steady. That was what she noticed. She turned to a clean page and wrote one line in symbols nobody else alive could read, and then, because she needed to see it in words, wrote it again beneath.

*302 = him? Who checked?*

Somebody had sat in a room in November of 2022 and typed that word. Somebody had had a tape, or a memory, or a bad night, and had decided what a frightened man meant. She did not yet know if it mattered. She did not know what she would do if it did. She knew one thing, which was how to check a fact, and checking a fact cost nothing. She would check one. Just one, quietly, before she let herself decide anything else.

She closed the pad. Across the street the fog was coming up off the river, taking the tops off the buildings first.
