# The Ninth Floor: story bible

For the writers. Readers never see this file. Everything here is canon unless the continuity log records a deliberate change.

## Logline and premise

**Logline.** In the rainy November of 1952, a former police dispatcher turned private investigator is hired by a hotel night clerk who swears he found a dead man on the ninth floor of a building that has only eight, and she follows the lie from the lobby desk to the people it was built to hide.

**Premise, in order, with every reveal.**

*What really happened before the book begins.*

1. In December 1946 a church sexton named Stefan Kowal, with his brother-in-law Tadeusz "Teddy" Wozniak (a bellhop at the Harbor Hotel, later its night clerk), began hiding refugees who had come off ships without papers. They used the one place nobody inspects: the hotel's ninth floor. The Harbor Hotel was built in 1926 with eight storeys and a deep terracotta cornice (the Frieze) along the top. Behind the Frieze lies a low floor, officially "tank and machinery space" on the plans. It was the Calder Club, a Prohibition card room, from 1929 to 1933, run by the Calder family's secretary, Ambrose Quill. After Repeal it was shut and forgotten. The elevator can reach it only with a service key turned in a hidden switch, and its landing is disguised by a painted brass plug on the car panel below the "8" button.
2. Stefan wrote every arrival into his mother's Polish prayer book, *Pod Twoją Obronę* ("Under Your Protection"), in the blank leaves at the back: true name, ship, date (by saint's day), days spent "na górze" (upstairs), and what was paid. He believed that a man who writes a false name in a holy book lies to God.
3. On the night of Sunday 14 March 1948 the Liberty ship *Esther Vane* landed thirty-one people at Pier 6, Slip 3, entered on the paper as extra seamen who would "desert" ashore. Police cars were kept out of the area for ninety minutes by an order from Captain Desmond Rafferty. The dispatcher who took that order was Ruth Szymanski. She refused to log it as an equipment fault, wrote it down word for word, and kept the carbon. Her friend Sergeant Frank Dolan, who relayed the order, asked her to alter it. She said no, and has kept the carbon since.
4. The racket grew out of Stefan's rescue, and he lost control of it. Aldo Ferrante, business agent of Longshoremen's Local 41, saw an opening for his hiring hall. Everett Plum, councilman and president of the Calder Pier & Terminal Company (the lessee of Pier 6), sold the cover. Declan Maher, a Customs tally clerk, altered tallies and manifests. And the Calder Estate Trust, owner of both the hotel and the pier, took a fee for "storage". The standard price became $400 a head: $150 to Ferrante, $100 to Plum, $150 to the Estate. Stefan hated it and stayed because the alternative was no pipeline. Paper people were listed by Ferrante and Maher as "ship-jumpers". The Estate's receipts listed them as freight.
5. The Estate's real head is Ambrose Quill, 63, sole trustee since 1938, hidden behind the Estate's lawyer Gilbert Swain, who signs everything as attorney-in-fact. Quill lives in room 806 of the hotel and is known to guests as "the resident auditor". The Calder heirs (two elderly nieces in Santa Barbara) know nothing and are comfortable. The Estate has been insolvent since 1946; the hotel loses money and the pier lease barely covers the pensions of forty old retainers. Quill took the money to keep the House of Calder standing, and to keep "his" people employed. He did the arithmetic and decided that three hundred and eleven strangers were an acceptable price. He is courteous, tender to staff, and lethal to anything that threatens the building.
6. Stefan Kowal died of a stroke in the vestibule of St. Casimir's in June 1951. His widow, Bronisława "Bronia" Kowal (Teddy's older sister), kept the prayer book in her handbag, where nobody looks. Teddy continued the entries in his own hand until October 1952, when the last family left.
7. Declan Maher was fired by Customs in September 1950 and has been bleeding the racket since. He blackmailed Ferrante, a few of the refugees (including the singer Yvette Marchand), and finally, in 1952, decided to sell the one thing that could hang the Estate: the original *Esther Vane* manifest, which shows a crew of forty and, in a separate hand, thirty-one "cases, personal effects" received for the Calder Estate Trust and signed by A. Quill, Trustee, in his own home-mixed green-black ink. Maher also knew, from a drunken confidence of Dolan's, that Ruth Szymanski kept a carbon of the Pier 6 order. If the Estate would not pay, he meant to hand everything to her.

*What happens in the book, in order.*

8. Friday 31 October 1952: Maher checks into room 807 of the Harbor Hotel as "H. Delaney of Portland, dried fruit". Teddy recognises him and says nothing. On Monday 3 November Maher, riding the elevator, hides the original manifest behind the framed Certificate of Inspection in the cab, using two thumbtacks, and tells Lambert the porter that the certificate is "the only honest thing in the building". He also writes Ruth Szymanski's name and address on the desk blotter.
9. Tuesday 4 November, election night. Plum's victory party fills the Pacific Room. Quill leaves it at 11:50 p.m. At 12:10 a.m. he carries a tray with a decanter of rye (dosed with chloral hydrate from his own prescription) and two tumblers to the linen cupboard on eight and up the hidden stair, in a dark red quilted dressing gown with his cane over his arm. The singer Yvette Marchand, in the corridor looking for 807, sees only his back. At 12:30 Maher rides to nine with the service key that Swain delivered with a note ("Nine. Half past midnight. Bring it."). Quill means to put him to sleep, search him and the room for the manifest, and make him talk when he wakes. He does not mean to kill him. Maher, who had drunk four whiskies in the party bar and has a weak heart, sleeps, then slowly dies. The key, once turned, holds the car on nine, deaf to the lobby buttons, until a key at the lobby panel or the recall in 806 sends it down. The hotel is nearly empty that week and the Pacific Room party guests use the service lift; Teddy is told the car is "sulking" and first notices the needle on the blank at about 2:10 a.m. Quill goes down at about 2:00 to place the alibi call (see below), returns up the hidden stair, searches the body and the floor, finds nothing, and sits with the dead man until after three. The ninth-floor dial needle sits on the blank past eight for two hours.
10. At 4:10 a.m. Teddy, uneasy about the needle, turns his own key at the lobby panel (which recalls the car), rides up and finds Maher on a cot, barefoot (shoes off, left sock with a hole at the heel), two glasses on the card table. He telephones the only line on nine, which rings in room 806. Quill says: wait at the desk. Quill and Lambert carry the body down the hidden stair to 807, put the shoes on him, remove the glasses and decanter, and call Dolan at 5:20 as a natural death. Quill then tells Teddy there is no ninth floor. That morning he has the brass plug on the car panel repainted so that nothing looks disturbed (it is still wet at eleven). He leaves the key switch itself working, because he needs it, until Ruth uses it (13 November), after which he has it cut out.
11. Wednesday 5 November, 7:40 a.m.: Teddy, unable to prove he is not mad, hires Ruth Szymanski. The book follows eighteen days, to Thanksgiving Eve and a few days after.
12. Ruth learns that "Delaney" was Declan Maher, who telephoned the police wire the night of the Pier 6 gap; learns the ninth floor exists; is paid $500 by Gilbert Swain to certify that Teddy is mistaken (the Estate's money); is warned off by Ferrante; overhears a search of the ninth; finds the singer Yvette Marchand is not who she says she is.
13. Lambert, who helped move the body and cannot live with it, telephones Ruth and arranges a midnight meeting on 11 November. Quill kills him that evening with a blow and a push into the shaft, staged as a fall while greasing the guide rails (the second body). Ruth finds him. She takes his service key from his neck before the police arrive.
14. Midpoint (Thursday 13 November, small hours): Ruth enters the ninth floor with Lambert's key and finds not a crime scene but a shelter: cots, child-sized coat hooks, a wall of chalked first names. She finally understands that the man she was hired to avenge was a blackmailer of the people hidden there, and that her client lied to her about all of it. The question changes from "who killed a stranger" to "who is protecting what, and who gets hurt if it comes out".
15. Quill, a limited-hangout expert, admits to Ruth that he moved the body "to save the hotel's licence and sixty jobs", and says he knew the floor existed. He asks her to find the killer. He is the best client she has. Meanwhile suspicion points elsewhere: at Ferrante (menace, motive), Plum (motive, slip of the tongue), Swain (who signs for the Estate), Yvette (who was in the building at 12:10), Teddy (who found the body), and Dolan (who hid the Pier 6 gap).
16. Bronia gives the prayer book to Ruth's mother. Ruth's mother Helena reads the Polish aloud, and the ledger turns out to be the key between the false names on the paper manifest and the real people. Yvette is in it. So are three hundred and ten others. The fees "do Domu" (to the House) go to the Estate.
17. Quill frames Teddy: an anonymous call, a brown bottle of chloral hydrate in Teddy's locker, Maher's room key. Dolan arrests Teddy. Quill visits Teddy in the cells and threatens, softly, to expose Bronia's immigration papers if Teddy does not confess. Teddy confesses to both murders to protect the names in the book. His pills (nitroglycerin) are taken as property at booking. He dies in his cell at 3:10 a.m. on Saturday 22 November (the low point).
18. Ruth, her licence suspended on Swain's complaint, rebuilds the case from small things: the chloral prescription number on the bottle's torn label (Sorkin's Pharmacy, Rx 41872, A. Quill); the open telephone line that makes his alibi (a 75-minute call to Santa Barbara from 2:05 to 3:20 a.m.) a lie, since Miss Honor Calder spoke to him for ten minutes and put the receiver on her pillow; the perfect, unscuffed rubber tip of his cane (a man who limps leans); the home-mixed green-black ink that dries rust-brown, which is the ink on the hatband note found in Maher's hat; the word "Mezzanine"; Yvette's tray and dressing gown; the jail's visitors' log; and, last, the recalled remark that Maher read the elevator certificate "a good minute" and called it honest.
19. Ruth finds the original manifest behind the certificate on the morning of 26 November, with a note from Maher to her. Dolan takes it to the District Attorney, who stalls over Thanksgiving. Ruth sees a light in the Frieze. Quill has gone to the ninth to burn the Estate's receipt ledger in the old card-room safe.
20. Climax on Thanksgiving Eve: Ruth climbs the hidden stair (fourteen steps) and confronts Quill, who says almost nothing, lights the lamp oil, bars the stair door and leaves her on a burning floor. She escapes along the cornice through a louvre in the fog, Dolan counts the Frieze from the street and arrests Quill at the foot of the fire escape. The fire destroys the chalk names, and with them the only public record of who lived there. Ruth is glad, and not proud of it.
21. Ending: solved at a cost. Quill is held for both murders and for fraud and signs for nothing else. Swain talks. Plum resigns his seat. Ferrante is indicted. Dolan testifies about Pier 6 and loses his pension and his future. Teddy is buried; Lambert too. Yvette takes the night train north and starts again under a name she chooses. The manifest and Quill's ledger go to the District Attorney. The prayer book does not. Bronia gives it to Ruth to keep, and Ruth, who spent four years keeping a carbon as leverage, learns the difference between a keeper and a hoarder. She keeps the book.

## World and setting

**Port Calder** is a fictional West Coast shipping city of about 410,000 people, built on a long bay called Calder Sound and named for the family that once owned the waterfront. It rains, or fogs, from October to April. The year is 1952. Eisenhower has just beaten Stevenson. Korea grinds on. Television is a rumour in shop windows. People take streetcars, drink rye and coffee, read the *Port Calder Beacon* (evening) and the *Courier* (morning), and speak on party lines.

**Map in words.** All distances are on foot unless stated.

- **Front Street** runs north-south along the waterfront. From north to south: Calder Point (a headland with the lighthouse and the foghorn the city calls the Heifer), then Pier 6 at the foot of **Capstan Row**, then the Customs Annex on the corner of **Cutter Street**, then Anchor Alley, then the Harbor Hotel at the corner of Front and **Ferry Slip Row**, then the ferry slip, then the Flats.
- **Pier 6** is at the end of Capstan Row, four blocks north of the hotel (six minutes). Slip 3 is the third berth on its south side. The **Shape Hall** (Longshoremen's Local 41) stands at the corner of Capstan Row and Front, two blocks from the pier.
- **Cutter Street** runs uphill (east) from Front to Lamplighter Hill. On it, going up: the Customs Annex and Harbor Commission records room (Front and Cutter), the **Selkirk Building** (Cutter and Second, Ruth's office, room 309, third floor, frosted glass), **Central Station** (police headquarters, Cutter and Third), and City Hall at the top of **Lamplighter Hill**. Municipal Hospital and the county morgue are on the hill's east flank, a ten-minute climb from Central Station.
- **Anchor Alley** is a narrow lane off Front Street, north of Cutter. It is the old Chinese quarter, now a few blocks of tailors, herbalists and a laundry. **Fong & Son, Tailors to the Sea** is at number 14.
- **Brine Street** runs parallel to Front, one block uphill from the hotel (three minutes). The **Cormorant Room**, a jazz club in a former chandlery, is on Brine Street.
- **The Flats** are reclaimed tideland south of the ferry slip. Polish, Lithuanian and Irish families live in frame houses and tenements there. **Hake Street** is the main street of the Flats; Ruth and her mother live at 1127 Hake (second floor), Teddy lives above Sobczak's Bakery at 1131. **St. Casimir's Church** is at Hake Street and Eleventh Avenue; the Kowal flat is on Eleventh, behind the church. The Number 14 streetcar runs from the Flats up Front Street to Cutter.
- **Calder Heights** is the rich district at the top of Lamplighter Hill; Plum's house is there. The Calder Estate's offices (Swain's) are in the Calder Building on Cutter Street, fourth floor.

**The Harbor Hotel.** Built 1926 of brick and terracotta, eight storeys over a lobby (ground floor is "L"), 143 rooms. The main entrance faces Front Street. The **lobby** has black-and-white marble, a clock that runs three minutes slow, a brass rail round the desk and a fern nobody waters. The **Pacific Room** (ballroom) opens off the lobby on the Ferry Slip side. The **desk** stands opposite the lift. There is one passenger elevator (an Otis of 1926, converted to push-button in 1941), a service lift at the back for linen and luggage, and two stairwells. **The cab** is oak veneer and brass, with eight brass buttons marked L, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, a brass arc above the door with a needle that swings across the numerals, and a framed **Certificate of Inspection** (State Division of Elevators, No. 4471) that reads "Landings served: 9. Floors open to public: 8." Beneath the "8" is a round brass plug the size of a half dollar. It hides a keyway. The needle's arc has a ninth, unnumbered stop past 8.

**The Frieze.** From the street the building shows eight rows of windows under a cornice fourteen feet deep: terracotta waves with fish between, and a band of louvred vents. People take the Frieze for ornament. It is the face of the ninth floor. The louvres are painted black on the inside.

**The ninth floor.** The ceiling is eight feet six inches (a tall man can lay his palm flat on it). The elevator door opens onto a corridor of fourteen paces. At the end is the former Calder Club: a long room, thirty-six feet by twenty, with green baize card tables stacked against the wall, a dead chandelier, a Prohibition-era safe in the east wall, and a wall telephone with no dial that rings only in room 806. Leading off the corridor are five dormitory rooms with iron cots (forty-two beds in all), a kitchenette with a gas ring, a laundry copper, a lavatory, and a rail of coat hooks at child height. A **hidden stair** (fourteen steps) runs from the ninth down to a locked door at the back of the linen cupboard at the east end of the eighth-floor corridor. The elevator and the hidden stair are the only two ways in apart from the Frieze.

**Rules and costs (law, trade, technology, period).**

- **The service key.** Three exist: Lambert's (on a shoelace round his neck), Teddy's (in his watch pocket), and the Estate's (in Quill's safe in 806). Turning it in the keyway behind the plug, while pressing "8" and holding for a slow count of three, sends the car one stop higher. The ride to nine takes forty-four seconds; to eight, thirty-eight. The needle swings onto the blank. The car then stays on nine, deaf to calls from other floors, until someone turns a key at the lobby panel or uses the recall switch beside the dial in 806. The cost is that the needle stays on the blank for as long as the car stays up there, and anyone watching a dial will see it. There is a second dial in Quill's room 806, over the door.
- **The telephone.** Direct toll dialling does not exist in Port Calder in 1952. Long distance goes through the operator, is ticketed by the minute, and the ticket records start and end times. An operator hears an open line. A call to Santa Barbara is about 3 minutes' charge for 75 cents.
- **Chloral hydrate** is a common sedative (knockout drops, "Mickey Finn") available on prescription. In whisky it is quick and gives a drinker a long sleep. In a man with a bad heart and four whiskies already in him it can stop breathing. The county toxicologist can detect it by the Fujiwara test if told to look. Nobody tells him to look until after Lambert's death.
- **The law.** The Displaced Persons Act of 1948 and its 1950 amendment let in a limited number of refugees with sponsors. Many were left out. A seaman who deserts ship in a US port without discharge can be deported; one who is listed as "discharged ashore by mutual consent" is legal for a few weeks. The Paper Crew racket put names on crew lists and had them "discharged" in port. Anyone on the list can be traced, deported and, for those whose papers were forged, prosecuted. That is why the book must not be handed to the police.
- **The union.** Longshoremen are hired each morning at the Shape Hall by "shape-up": the men stand in a ring and the hiring boss nods or lifts a toothpick. Ferrante controls that nod. The coast's big longshore union ran union-controlled dispatch halls by 1952; Local 41 is a rump local on one old pier where the shape-up survived, which is part of why it is so easy to corrupt.
- **Police.** Radio dispatch is called "the wire". Detectives work in pairs. A coroner's physician, not a medical examiner, signs deaths in the county. An autopsy is done only when ordered.
- **Licence.** Private investigators are licensed by the State Board of Licences. A complaint can suspend a licence pending a hearing, which takes ten days.
- **Money.** A fin is five dollars, a sawbuck ten. A night clerk earns $38 a week; a cab ride across the city is 60 cents; a decent hotel room is $4.50 a night; a good lunch is 90 cents. Ruth charges $15 a day plus expenses, and is behind two months on rent at $42.

**Glossary (recurring terms, objects and customs).**

1. **The Ninth** / **the Mezzanine**: the hidden floor. Staff who know it say "upstairs". Quill alone calls it "the Mezzanine", an old Calder Club word.
2. **The Frieze**: the terracotta cornice that is the ninth floor's face.
3. **The dial**: the brass arc and needle above the cab door; the ninth stop is blank.
4. **The plug**: the painted brass disc on the car panel below the "8" that covers the keyway.
5. **The service key**: a long brass key with a square head, stamped OTIS INSP.
6. **The Heifer**: the Calder Point foghorn, a low two-note moan every forty seconds in fog.
7. **The Estate**: the Calder Estate Trust, owner of the hotel and the pier; its public face is Gilbert Swain.
8. **Local 41**: Longshoremen's Local 41; **the Shape Hall** is its hiring hall.
9. **Shape-up**: the dawn hiring ring.
10. **Tally clerk**: a Customs or Harbor Commission clerk who counts cargo and persons against the manifest.
11. **Manifest**: the ship's list of cargo, crew and passengers; the original is the master's copy.
12. **The Paper Crew**: the name Ferrante's men gave the racket; the people were "cases" in the Estate's receipts and "jumpers" in the union's.
13. **Slip 3**: the third berth on Pier 6's south side, where the *Esther Vane* landed.
14. **The wire**: police radio dispatch; Ruth's old world.
15. **The carbon**: the carbon copy of the March 1948 dispatch log with Rafferty's order and Maher's 11:14 p.m. call.
16. **The Pacific Room**: the Harbor's ballroom.
17. **The Cormorant Room**: the Brine Street jazz club.
18. **St. Raphael box**: the offertory box at St. Casimir's for "travellers", which Stefan used for the first rescues.
19. ***Pod Twoją Obronę***: the prayer book, a black leather *modlitewnik* with a brass clasp and a cracked spine, printed in Kraków in 1911.
20. ***Na górze***: "upstairs" in Polish, the ledger's word for time on the ninth.
21. ***Do Domu***: "to the House", the ledger's word for the Estate's fee.
22. **Sorkin's**: Sorkin's Pharmacy on Front Street, whose prescription number is on the brown bottle.
23. **The Beacon**: the evening newspaper.
24. **Rx 41872**: the prescription number that convicts.
25. **A case**: freight, in the Estate's receipts; the unforgivable word.
26. **Toll ticket**: the operator's slip with start and end time of a long-distance call.
27. **The Selkirk**: the Selkirk Building, Ruth's office.
28. **Opłatek**: the Christmas wafer; Helena starts breaking it from the first Sunday of Advent.

## Naming palette

Port Calder in 1952 is a working port of immigrants. Draw names from: Polish (Szymanski, Wozniak, Kowal, Dziedzic, Sobczak, Okonski), Irish and Irish-American (Dolan, Rafferty, Maher, Nugent), Italian (Ferrante), Chinese-American (Fong, Lau), African-American (Lambert, Hightower, Tolliver, Odessa), Greek-American (Karras), Jewish (Sorkin), English (Quill, Swain, Plum, Calder, Pomeroy). Keep initials distinct among major characters (R, T, F, Y, E, A, L, P, G, H, B). New minor names must not repeat an initial already used by two or more named characters in the same chapter, must not appear in the overused-names list in `scripts/chapter-check.mjs`, and should sound like real people, not fantasy.

## Cast

All speech examples are indicative; writers should vary them, not repeat them.

### Ruth Anne Szymanski ("Ruth"; "Rutka" to her mother; "Ruthie" to Dolan when he wants something; "Miss Szymanski" to everyone else)

- **Age**: 36 (born 11 February 1916 in Port Calder).
- **Look (never change)**: five foot ten, long-boned, a little stooped from sitting in a wire room; a streak of grey in her dark hair at the right temple; her father's steel Waltham watch worn on her left wrist with the face turned inward.
- **Speech**: laconic and dry. She repeats an address or a fact back as a dispatcher does ("Say again. Third and Hake. Third and Hake."), counts things aloud under her breath (stairs, doors, minutes), and answers a question with a question when she wants time. Mostly short sentences. Example: "Say again. A dead man, on the ninth floor, of an eight-floor hotel. Mr. Wozniak, I need that in smaller pieces."
- **Wants**: rent, a client who pays, and to be right. Deeper: to stop being the woman who kept the carbon in a drawer instead of doing something with it.
- **Fears**: being owned by someone, and that her silence about 1948 will one day hurt a person she can see.
- **Secret**: she kept the 1948 dispatch carbon, with Rafferty's order and Maher's call, and for four years has used it as private insurance (it got her a licence, in effect: Dolan signed her application). She also knows more about what happened at Pier 6 that night than she has told anyone.
- **Arc**: from keeper as hoarder, someone who holds a paper in order to have power, to keeper as guardian, someone who holds a thing so that others are safe. She gives the carbon away and keeps the book.
- **Habits**: she notices hands and shoes first. She walks everywhere because she can't afford a car; she hums nothing; she smokes Luckies but no more than six a day (she counts). She keeps a .32 Colt in the desk and does not carry it.

### Tadeusz "Teddy" Wozniak

- **Age**: 54 (born 1898 in Łódź; came to Port Calder in 1920; hired as bellhop at the Harbor 1931; night clerk since 1939).
- **Look (never change)**: small and stooped, with white eyebrows like moth wings; a gold tooth top left; neat hands that square pencils and blotters without thinking. He carries a little tin pillbox of nitroglycerin tablets and rattles it when anxious.
- **Speech**: formal, precise English with a Polish lilt; "please", "if I may", "forgive me". Apologises for what he is about to say. Example: "If I may, Miss Szymanski, I am a man who counts the keys at night. I am not a man who dreams."
- **Wants**: to know who killed the man on nine, and to keep Bronia and the hidden people out of it.
- **Fears**: the Immigration; the loss of his sister; being thought mad.
- **Secret**: he helped Stefan run the ninth from 1946, knew Maher at once, lied to Ruth about it, was kept in ignorance that Quill was the Estate's head, and wrote the last entries in the book. He also lied about Yvette's visit.
- **Arc**: from a man who hides to a man who will be believed, and finally a man who, by confessing, spends himself to keep the names safe. He dies in custody on 22 November.

### Detective Sergeant Francis Xavier "Frank" Dolan

- **Age**: 44.
- **Look (never change)**: broad, freckled forearms and thinning sandy hair; a nose broken twice; a plain gold wedding ring worn on a chain round his neck since his wife Moira died in 1950.
- **Speech**: slow Irish-American cadences, deflecting jokes, "Ruthie" when he wants something, wooden matchsticks chewed in the corner of his mouth. Example: "I'm a policeman, Ruth. It's a very small job. They give you a badge so you'll stop asking for a bigger one."
- **Wants**: to make lieutenant before his daughter finishes school; to be a decent man.
- **Fears**: the Pier 6 ninety minutes coming out.
- **Secret**: in March 1948 he took $300 from Captain Rafferty's hand to keep patrol cars away, spent it on his wife's surgery, and asked Ruth to alter the log.
- **Arc**: from avoider to witness. He reopens the case, arrests Teddy by the book and regrets it, and testifies against Rafferty and himself.

### Yvette Marchand (born Irena Wolska)

- **Age**: 31 (born 1921, Lublin).
- **Look (never change)**: very pale, a low coil of dark hair, grey-green eyes, a small crescent scar on the chin; white cotton gloves on stage and sometimes off it.
- **Speech**: low voice, a stage French she overuses ("mon cher"), Polish consonants coming through when she's angry. Example: "Marchand. Like people who sell things. Write it however you like, Miss Szymanski, the police do."
- **Wants**: her page torn out of the world. She has been paying Maher $40 a month.
- **Fears**: deportation; the sound of a particular kind of knock.
- **Secret**: she lived nineteen days on nine in October 1949, came on the *Pacific Tern* under the name of a Lyon laundress whose papers she bought; she went to 807 at 12:10 on the night of Maher's death to buy her page, got no answer, and saw an old man in a dark gown carrying a tray.
- **Arc**: from running to staying long enough to speak, then leaving on her own terms.

### Everett Plum

- **Age**: 58. Councilman (Seventh Ward) re-elected 4 November; president of the Calder Pier & Terminal Company.
- **Look (never change)**: round, pink, dyed ginger hair, a diamond stickpin, a damp upper lip he dabs with a folded handkerchief.
- **Speech**: oratorical, "friends", mispronounces long words with confidence. Example: "Miss Szymanski! A daughter of the working classes and an alumna of our fine police family!"
- **Wants**: to be mayor.
- **Fears**: a headline.
- **Secret**: he takes $100 a head; he knows "Maher" (he slips and says it) and did not know about the killings.
- **Arc**: a vain man who is, for once, more frightened than vain; resigns.

### Ambrose Quill

- **Age**: 63 (born 1889, Dorset; came to Port Calder 1911 as secretary to the Calder family).
- **Look (never change)**: tall and narrow; silver hair parted exactly in the middle; pale blue eyes; long yellow-white hands; always an ebony cane with a silver collar and a rubber tip as clean as a new pencil eraser. He wears charcoal suits with a high collar, and at night a dark red quilted dressing gown.
- **Speech**: unhurried, complete sentences, an English schoolroom courtesy; says "my dear" to everyone; calls the ninth "the Mezzanine". Example: "My dear Miss Szymanski, a hotel is a machine for being forgiven in."
- **Wants**: the House of Calder and the building kept standing.
- **Why he signed**: the Estate's insurers insist that the Trustee sign for every item received into storage, and Quill, an auditor to his bones, cannot bring himself to falsify a receipt. His honesty about paper is the flaw that hangs him.
- **Fears**: bankruptcy, which to him is not money but the death of a family.
- **Secret**: everything. He needs no one else's secret; he is the Estate. He mixes his own green-black ink to an old Dorset formula; it dries to a rust brown.
- **Arc**: he does not change. He is the one fixed point; Ruth changes round him. He says little at the end and never explains.

### Lucille Fong

- **Age**: 34 (born 1918 in Port Calder).
- **Look (never change)**: small, round tortoiseshell glasses, a pencil always stuck in her hair, a chipped front tooth, immaculate handwriting.
- **Speech**: fast and funny, switchboard phrases ("Number, please", "Please hold"), sardonic. Example: "I'm a records clerk, Ruth. I know where everything is. That's not the same as wanting to tell you."
- **Wants**: to be made Chief Clerk of the Harbor Commission (a man was always put over her).
- **Fears**: that her father's papers could be looked at.
- **Secret**: her father Walter came as a "paper son" in 1909 under a purchased family name (his true surname was Lau, which she has never said aloud); and, once, to save a cousin, she altered a Commission record. She understands exactly what the book is.
- **Arc**: from careful to bold; she is the one who tells Ruth to keep it. She worked on the police switchboard in the wire room with Ruth from 1941 to 1948.

### Aldo Ferrante

- **Age**: 51.
- **Look (never change)**: thick forearms; the top of his left ring finger missing; a hat brim always snapped down; peels an orange with a pocket knife in one long spiral while he talks.
- **Speech**: soft, courteous menace, a trace of Calabria. Example: "Your father was a good man, Ruth. I carried his coffin. Remember I carried it."
- **Wants**: the union, and his take; deeper, to not be the man who sold people.
- **Fears**: the Immigration, and the Estate, whose face he has never seen.
- **Secret**: he was Maher's first blackmail victim; he truly believes he saved people.
- **Arc**: from threat to informant; indicted.

### Josiah "Joe" Lambert

- **Age**: 58 (born Mobile, Alabama; at the Harbor since 1930).
- **Look (never change)**: short, bowed, scarred hands from cab ropes and grease, a gold cufflink on his Sunday shirt; the service key on a shoelace under his collar.
- **Speech**: slow, courteous, "Miss" and "sir", a hum of "Abide with Me" when thinking. Example: "That cab's got moods, Miss. I just keep her company."
- **Wants**: to atone for carrying the body.
- **Fears**: Quill's displeasure, and his wife Odessa's.
- **Dignity**: deacon at Mt. Zion A.M.E., saving for a house on Fir Street; he knows the building better than anyone and is the only man there who has never once been asked his opinion of it. He is never servile in private; "Miss" and "sir" are the job's manners, and he drops them when he calls Ruth.
- **Secret**: he carried Maher down. He was told it would protect the people upstairs.
- **Arc**: he tries to speak and is killed. (Second body.)

### Pearl Hightower

- **Age**: 49. Head housekeeper.
- **Look (never change)**: tall, upright, gold spectacles on a chain, a ring of forty keys that she never lets chime.
- **Speech**: precise, unhurried. Example: "I do not guess, Miss Szymanski. I count."
- **Wants**: to keep her staff and the upstairs people safe.
- **Fears**: being turned out.
- **Secret**: she fed the ninth for six years. She noticed the dent that was not in the bedspread.
- **Arc**: from silent to ally; she lets Ruth in by the kitchen on the night of the certificate.

### Gilbert Swain

- **Age**: 41. Attorney, attorney-in-fact for the Estate and Plum's counsel.
- **Look (never change)**: hair oil, a scent of bay rum, cufflinks shaped like small anchors.
- **Speech**: speaks in rhetorical questions. Example: "Would I be here if there were anything to hide, Miss Szymanski? Would I pay you five hundred dollars to look?"
- **Wants**: to be rich and clean.
- **Fears**: Quill, a little, and juries.
- **Secret**: he has run the Estate's errands for years, delivered the key and the note to Maher, and does not know Quill killed anyone.
- **Arc**: red herring, then witness.

### Helena Szymanska

- **Age**: 70. Ruth's mother, widowed 1941 (Anton, stevedore, killed by a slipped sling on Pier 6).
- **Look (never change)**: short and wide; a head scarf; forearms like rolled dough; reading glasses on a string.
- **Speech**: Polish and English mixed; imperatives; proverbs bent to her purpose. Example: "Rutka, eat. A detective who faints is only a woman lying down."
- **Wants**: Ruth married, or at least fed.
- **Fears**: the Soviets, doctors, and the first of the month.
- **Secret**: she can read the old Polish hand, and she hid a family of four in her own kitchen for a week in 1949, which she has told no one.
- **Arc**: she decodes the book, and says the thing Ruth needs to hear about what a keeper is.

### Bronisława "Bronia" Kowal

- **Age**: 62. Teddy's older sister; Stefan's widow.
- **Look (never change)**: small, in black, rimless spectacles, a mother-of-pearl rosary wound round one wrist, a black handbag with a steel clasp that goes everywhere.
- **Speech**: quiet, formal, Polish syntax in English. Example: "Teddy is a good boy. He is fifty-four and a good boy. Leave him be."
- **Wants**: to keep Stefan's book.
- **Fears**: that she has already lost Teddy.
- **Secret**: she has been carrying the whole racket in her handbag to Mass every morning for sixteen months.
- **Arc**: loses her brother; gives the book to Ruth; "A book wants a keeper, not a shelf."

## Relationships and conflicts

- **Ruth and Teddy**: client and investigator, each lying. Her loyalty runs to the truth; his runs to Bronia and the names. He wants her to find the killer and not the book; she cannot do the first without risking the second.
- **Ruth and Dolan**: eight years on the same wire, the voice she trusted before she knew his face. He asked her to falsify a log. She refused and kept the evidence, and neither forgave the other. He wants to be left alone; she wants him to be brave.
- **Ruth and Lucille**: switchboard sisters. Lucille will do almost anything for Ruth except lie about her father.
- **Ruth and Quill**: he is the best-mannered client she ever has; she likes him, which is how he works.
- **Ruth and Odessa Lambert**: Joe's widow, a seamstress and deaconess at Mt. Zion A.M.E., who gets the truth about his death only when Ruth brings his key back.
- **Quill and Teddy**: Teddy trusts him as a landlord of conscience. Quill uses that.
- **Ferrante and Plum and Swain**: three men pretending the Estate is someone else.
- **Yvette and Ruth**: Yvette will not be saved by anyone, then is.
- **Helena and Bronia**: parish women, rivals at the coffee urn, allies in the end.
- **Ruth and her mother**: a love that argues about food.

Every important want collides with another: Teddy's need to protect the names vs Ruth's need to catch a killer; Dolan's career vs the Pier 6 truth; Ferrante's survival vs Yvette's anonymity; Quill's House vs three hundred and eleven people; Lucille's father's secret vs the records she keeps.

## Voice guide

- **Viewpoint**: first person, past tense, Ruth throughout, narrating from some unspecified later time (never state when). No other viewpoint.
- **Register**: plain, wry, observant noir. Short declaratives with an occasional long, rolling sentence when the rain or a room gets her attention. She notices hands and shoes first, then clothes, then faces. She counts things.
- **Rhythm**: vary. Do not open consecutive paragraphs with the same word. Do not end every scene on an aphorism. About one simile per page, and make each one fresh and rooted in her world (wires, ledgers, laundry, streetcars, fish), never "like a cat".
- **Humour**: dry and frequent: one wry line a page; the supporting cast supply comedy (Pugh, Plum, Pomeroy, Helena, Karras). Grief and fear are written straight.
- **Dialogue**: about a third to a half of each chapter. Double quotation marks, `said` as the default tag, almost no adverbs. New paragraph for each speaker. People interrupt, dodge and answer other questions.
- **Period**: 1952. Slang sparingly and accurately: the wire, a fin, a sawbuck, flatfoot (once), on the level, a sap, the bulls (rarely), a tail, a shamus (a character may say it, Ruth won't), swell (rarely). No "dame". No "gumshoe". No modern idiom ("process", "closure", "gaslight", "okay" is fine, "trauma" is not).
- **Violence and romance**: never gratuitous. No romance plot; a possible warmth between Ruth and Dolan stays unspoken.
- **Retrospection**: Ruth may say "I found out later" at most once a chapter. Never "little did I know".

**Sample paragraph (voice only; not part of the novel).**

The Selkirk Building has an elevator that works on Tuesdays and a landlord who works on the first of the month. I took the stairs. Four flights is nothing if you count them, and I count everything; it's a habit I brought out of the wire room along with a bad back and a groove across my hair where the headset sat. Third floor, room 309, frosted glass with my name on it in gilt that had been cheaper than it looked. Somebody was already waiting on the bench in the hall, a small man holding a wet hat in both hands like a pie he'd been told not to drop. His shoes were good and old and had been resoled twice. His hands were clean to the second knuckle and no further, which meant he washed them every hour and was afraid of something. "Miss Szymanski?" he said. "Depends who's asking," I said, and let us in.

**Things this narrator would never write.**

1. A description of herself in a mirror.
2. "Gumshoe", "dame", "gat", "peeper", or any other stock noir word as a joke on herself.
3. A simile involving a cat, a spider, or a snake.
4. "I should have known" or "little did I know".
5. A character who explains his motive unprompted.
6. An em dash in every paragraph.
7. "The city was alive" or any sentence in which the city or the rain has feelings.
8. A sexual look at another woman's body or at Dolan's.
9. A word for a feeling where an action would do ("I felt afraid").
10. A summary of the previous chapter.
11. A joke at the expense of anyone's accent or name.
12. A diary, a letter or a recording that delivers the solution (the manifest is physical evidence, not an explanation).

## Motifs and recurring images

1. **Counting**: Ruth counts floors, stairs, paces, minutes. First (ch. 1) the floors from the street; payoff (ch. 14 and 26) fourteen paces and fourteen steps; last (ch. 28) she stops counting.
2. **Shoes and hands**: Maher's socks, Quill's clean overshoe soles, the cane tip, Fong's tape-measure hands, Ferrante's missing finger. Surfaces in every chapter; decides the case (ch. 3, 12, 22, 23).
3. **The Heifer**: the foghorn that gives the novel its pulse every forty seconds; used as a clock in ch. 1, 4, 10, 18, and a timer in the climax (ch. 26).
4. **Paint and plaster**: covering a thing over. The wet plug (ch. 2), the black louvres (ch. 7), the painted-over hoistway door (ch. 15), the fire (ch. 27).
5. **The needle between numbers**: the dial's blank stop. Opens the book and closes it: a new ninth button.
6. **Keys and Pearl's ring**: who may open what. Lambert's shoelace key (ch. 12), Pearl's forty keys that never chime (ch. 2, 14, 24).
7. **Names**: false names, chalk first names, ledger true names, a man who writes a false name in a holy book. The theme of the whole novel.
8. **Rust-green ink**: Quill's ink: the visitor pass (ch. 2), the hatband note (ch. 16), the manifest signature (ch. 24).
9. **Bread and kitchens**: Helena's bread box, the ninth's kitchenette, Teddy's bakery: the places where people are kept alive.
10. **Rain on tin, rain on glass**: the weather as the soundtrack; never a mood.

## Ground rules that must stay consistent

**Distances and times.** Hotel to Pier 6: 4 blocks, 6 minutes. Hotel to the Cormorant Room: 3 minutes uphill. Hotel to Hake Street: 20 minutes on foot or the Number 14 streetcar (9 minutes, 7 cents). Selkirk Building to the hotel: 8 minutes. Cab from L to 8: 38 seconds; to 9: 44 seconds. The corridor on nine: 14 paces. The hidden stair: 14 steps. The pit: 7 feet. The Heifer sounds every 40 seconds in fog. Ruth walks everywhere and has no car.

**The cab.** Eight buttons (L, 2–8). Certificate No. 4471 reads "Landings served: 9." The plug is repainted on the morning of 5 November (wet at 11 a.m.), but the key switch behind it still works. On Friday 14 November, after the needle swings to the blank during Ruth's visit, the switch is cut out and the keyway filled with brass; the car cannot reach nine after that. The hidden stair remains the only way in until the fire.

**Keys.** Lambert's key goes to Ruth on 11 November. Teddy's key stays in his watch pocket until his arrest, then is bagged as property. The Estate's key is returned to Quill's safe after Maher's death.

**Who knows what, and when.**

- Wed 5 Nov: Teddy, Quill, Lambert know the body was moved. Swain knows Maher was invited but not that he died.
- Thu 6 Nov: Ruth knows Delaney is Maher.
- Fri 7 Nov: Ruth knows the *Esther Vane* manifest is a retyped sheet and thirty-one men were fitted for sea.
- Sat 8 Nov: Ruth has heard two voices on nine; she does not know who.
- Mon 10 Nov: Ferrante tells Ruth Maher bled him.
- Wed 12 Nov: Quill tells Ruth he moved the body; Teddy tells her the shelter's history. Quill does not know Teddy has told.
- Thu 13 Nov: Ruth sees the ninth. Quill sees her car on his dial.
- Sun 16 Nov: Ferrante tells her the racket's origin; Bronia gives up the book; Helena reads it; Ruth knows the shape of the fraud.
- Thu 20 Nov: Dolan holds Teddy. Fri 21 Nov: Teddy confesses. Sat 22 Nov: Teddy dies.
- Tue 25 Nov: Ruth knows Quill's alibi is false and who bought the chloral.
- Wed 26 Nov, 3 a.m.: Ruth holds the manifest. Quill knows, because the needle moved on his dial.
- The refugees' names and the book are known to Ruth, Helena, Yvette, Bronia, and (eventually) Lucille. Nobody else may read them. Dolan never sees the book.

**Alibis.** Ferrante: at the Shape Hall with forty men and a radio, 8 p.m. to 3 a.m. Plum: in the Pacific Room until 1:30 a.m., home by 2. Swain: in the Pacific Room until 1 a.m., then dinner with the DA's secretary at the Elks. Yvette: at the Cormorant Room until 11:50 p.m., at the hotel at 12:10, back at the club by 1:10. Quill: left the party at 11:50 p.m., "retired" to 806; from 2:05 to 3:20 the toll ticket shows a call to Santa Barbara. Teddy: at the desk all night except 4:10–4:25 a.m. Lambert: in the basement boiler room, unobserved.

**Wounds and wear.** Maher died of chloral hydrate with alcohol and a weak heart; shoes off on nine, on in 807; his left sock has a hole at the heel. Lambert died of a blow to the left temple from the heavy brass oiler, then a fall; his hands have no grease. Teddy dies of heart failure at 3:10 a.m. on 22 November; his pillbox was in the property bag in the sergeant's office.

**Money.** Swain's retainer: $500 in an Estate envelope (watermark C.E.T.). The ledger fee: $400 a head. Ruth's rent is $42 and two months behind until the end.

**Weather.** Rain every day but three: it clears on Saturday 15 November (cold, bright), on Thursday 20 November (grey, dry) and on Thanksgiving Day. Fog on the nights of 11, 18 and 26 November.

**Wardrobe constants.** Ruth: her father's Waltham on her left wrist; a navy raincoat belted and creased; a felt hat with a short brim; flat shoes that can run. Quill: charcoal suit, cane, rubber tip. Pearl: her ring of forty keys. Teddy: the hotel's maroon jacket with a brass nameplate. Dolan: a brown hat and a matchstick.
