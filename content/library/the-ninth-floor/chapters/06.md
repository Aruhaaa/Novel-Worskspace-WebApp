# Victory at the Elks

The cake was four feet long and had been iced in the shape of City Hall, with the clock tower in white sugar and a dome of green, and in the dome somebody had planted a sparkler, which was throwing a small, crackling, indignant light over the whole banquet room of the Elks Lodge on Lamplighter Hill. The steps had come out a little crooked. The Mayor's window was a raisin. Two hundred people sat at long tables under paper cloths, working through creamed chicken on toast, and every one of them had one eye on the dome, waiting for it to catch the frosting alight.

"You're late," Gilbert Swain said at my elbow, "and in your best hat. Would it have hurt to choose a better one?"

"It's the only one that's dry."

"Then the Councilman will be proud of it." He had been waiting for me at the door of the banquet room, scented and glossy and agitated about the sleeves. He took my arm above the elbow, lightly, as a man takes a parcel he is not sure is his. "Come. He has asked for you by name. Do you know how many people the Councilman has asked for by name today? You and the caterer."

The room had oak panelling to the shoulder and, above that, the heads of elks, glass-eyed, looking down at the chicken. A pennant hung from every ceiling beam: the Friends of the Waterfront, in gold fringe. A raised platform at the far end held a long table, a lectern, a microphone on a stand and two loudspeakers shaped like bread bins. At the centre of the table, with his napkin tucked into his collar like a bib, sat a man I had seen on a hundred telephone poles and had never met.

Everett Plum was round, and pink, and gleaming. He had ginger hair that did not occur in nature, and a diamond stickpin the size of a pea, and a damp upper lip, which he dabbed at intervals with a folded handkerchief he kept in his cuff. He saw me coming. His whole face lit up like a window.

"Friends!" he cried, and the microphone, which had been waiting, shrieked.

The whole room winced. A waiter dropped a spoon. Plum held up both hands, in the manner of a bishop calming a congregation, and when the howl had died he leaned into the microphone again, at an angle, as if it might bite.

"Friends. I want you to meet someone special. Someone who's come up the hill today on a day of rain. A daughter of the working classes, friends, and an alumna of our fine police family! Miss Ruth Szymanski!" The microphone shrieked. "A woman of the waterfront!"

Two hundred forks stopped. A man at the next table, under a name card that said FRIEND OF THE LODGE, began to clap, and the room, unwilling to be left out, caught on. I nodded. I'm not built for standing in rooms while strangers clap at my family. I put my hand on the back of the nearest chair and kept it there.

"Don't smile," Swain murmured. "It makes him nervous."

* * *

There was a punch bowl on a side table by the cloakroom door, a cut-glass thing the size of a bathtub, and a woman beside it who had plainly been guarding it for some time. She was tall and thin and faded, in a mink stole that had been brushed the wrong way, with a hat that carried one dusty wing, and she was ladling punch into a cup with the concentration of a surgeon. She looked up and smiled with her whole narrow face.

"You're the girl from the police," she said. "Have a cup. It's the only honest thing in the room. It has rum in it, and it says so."

I took a cup. "You're Mrs. Plum."

"Cordelia. Everett says I should be Mrs. Councilman Plum, but it sounds like a patent medicine." She dipped her own cup again. "I'm told you're looking into something at the Harbor. I didn't hear it from anyone. I heard it from the chicken." She leaned closer, and a wisp of the wing's old dust floated off her hat. "I'm a Calder, you know. On my mother's side, or the wrong side of one. My mother's sister married a Calder. The Calders are all dead or in Santa Barbara, which is the same thing with better weather."

"Do you see them?"

"I get a letter at Christmas from a lawyer saying that I'm still a Calder. It's all I need to know." She tipped her cup at the platform. "Do you want to know about Everett? Everett thinks the harbor is a pond he owns. I've told him it's the Pacific Ocean. He says it's on a lease."

"Is it?"

"I'm the last person to ask. I once asked what a lease was, and he said, 'Cordelia, it's a thing you are not required to understand,' and I have been very restful ever since." She gazed at her husband with an expression that I found, to my surprise, was tender, and at the same time tired, like a woman looking at a clock she has long since stopped winding. "He's a good man to be married to. He doesn't hit. He doesn't drink very much. He just loves the sound of himself, and what's wrong with that? So does the church bell."

On the platform Plum had risen for his address, and the sparkler on the cake died with a last hiss as he began. It was a long speech. He told the Friends of the Waterfront that the harbor was the artery of our hopes, the very aorta of prosperity, and that under the right hand on the tiller it would be a mighty stallion. He thanked the voters of the Seventh Ward. He thanked God, and the Lodge, and the chicken. And then, with a smoothness that I would have admired if I hadn't been listening for it, he came round to the thing he had called me up the hill to hear.

"Friends, in the midst of our joy, on the very night of our triumph, this city lost a stranger. A guest at the Harbor Hotel passed away peacefully in his sleep. A tragic incident, on a night of joy. And I say to you, in the strongest possible terms, let us not be misconstrued. Let us not make a mountain out of a... a molehill of a mountain."

"Oh, Everett," Cordelia said, quietly, to her punch.

* * *

He found me by the cloakroom, afterward, because I had not been quick enough to be somewhere else. He came off the platform through a curtain with his handkerchief out, mopping, followed at three paces by Swain, who was walking like a man pushing a very large pram. Plum took my hand in both of his. They were damp, and warm, and soft, like a pair of poached eggs.

"Miss Szymanski. A woman of discretion. Gilbert says you are the soul of it. Such a business, you see, a terrible business, poor Maher, such a thing to happen at a time when..."

He stopped.

I've heard that stop on the wire a thousand times. It comes when a caller has said the one thing he meant to hold back and listens to it still ringing, and I let it ring. The handkerchief came up to his lip.

"Who?" I said.

"Delaney," said Plum. "Poor Delaney. From Portland."

"You said Maher."

"Did I?" The pink had gone up to the roots of the ginger. "My cousin has a hardware store in Sacramento. Maher's Hardware. I was thinking of a mishap in the... the paint department." He dabbed. "A terrible week for hardware."

"Everett," said Swain, from behind him, quite gently. "The photographer is waiting by the cake. He says the sparkler is out and you wanted it lit for the picture."

"The sparkler. Of course." Plum let go of my hand as if he'd just remembered where it had been. "Miss Szymanski, a pleasure, a privilege, I hope you will lunch with us again. Friends of the Waterfront are friends for life!" And he went, blown across the floor by his own momentum, with the handkerchief going before him like a flag.

Swain stayed. His anchors winked. He looked at me as one looks at a clock that has begun to strike thirteen. "The Councilman is an emotional man," he said. "He loves a good name. Would you hold it against him if he'd mislaid one?"

"The Beacon only printed Delaney," I said.

"Did it? I haven't looked." He smiled, and bowed from the neck, and followed his employer across the room to the cake.

The Beacon had printed the name Delaney and only the name Delaney, on page three, below a notice about a dog show. I had read it on the streetcar. The card with Declan Maher on it was in a manila envelope at the county morgue, and in Frank Dolan's head, and, I now understood, in a third place that I could not see.

* * *

I had meant to leave then. I'd got as far as the cloakroom for my coat, a long, dim room of hooks with a ticket girl asleep behind the counter, when I smelled orange.

You can smell an orange the way you smell a match. I turned. At the back of the banquet room, by the double doors and under the glass eyes of a particularly stern elk, a man sat with his chair tipped on its back legs against the panelling, and he wore his hat. The Elks have a rule that hats come off indoors. Nobody had told Aldo Ferrante, or everybody had.

I knew the hat first, the dark snapped brim. I knew the way he peeled the orange, the coil of skin hanging from the blade in a single ring and turning. I had left him standing in a laundry door in Anchor Alley two hours ago, and he'd beaten me up Lamplighter Hill, which a man with a car can do. He wore a dark overcoat, open. His forearms, where the sleeves were pushed up, were as thick as bedposts. The hand that held the orange was the left, and it lacked the top joint of the ring finger, cleanly, as if it had been cut off with a ruler.

Two men sat beside him with their hats on, too, and neither was eating.

He let the chair down onto its four legs without hurry and rose, and took the hat off, which was the only courtesy he made to the Elks. His hair was black and thick, cut short. He had a broad, handsome, tired face with a nose that had met something, and he smiled at me with an air of such warm, personal welcome that I almost looked behind me.

"Anton's girl," he said. The voice was soft. There was a trace of Calabria in it, in the way he laid the words down one by one. "I have seen you from a distance all my life, and never had the honour. You are very like him. Around the eyes."

"I'm Ruth."

"Ruth." He tried it. "Your father was a good man. I carried his coffin. There were six of us and I had the front left, and it was a heavy coffin for such a small man." He held out a segment of the orange on the flat of the knife. "Please. From Florida. Everything good comes from somewhere else."

"I don't like being given things."

"Everyone likes it, Ruth. It's why they get so angry about it." He ate the segment himself. "Remember that I carried it. It is a thing I like to be remembered for, and there is not very much else."

"Mr. Ferrante," I said. "Were you in Anchor Alley today?"

"Was I?" He considered this. "I am a man who likes a walk. The tailors there are the finest on the coast. I do not wear their coats, but I admire the principle." The orange turned in his hand. "I will tell you something, Ruth, because your father had a right to hear it from somebody. Pier 6 is a bad place for a woman who sits in the dark."

"I don't sit much."

"No? I heard you sat very still for nine years." The knife moved round the fruit, taking a long thin curl, and he did not look at it. "It is a great gift, to be still. A rare one. I would not throw it away on a pier."

A cheer went up at the front of the room as somebody relit the sparkler. The photographer's flash went off, and for the width of a blink the whole banquet hall stood out white, two hundred faces, a hundred elk's-eyes, a cake in the shape of a building. When my vision came back, Ferrante had put his hat on and gone through the double doors with his two silent men, leaving the peel on the arm of his chair in a loose bright spiral, like a spring that had come out of something.

* * *

I stayed in the Elks long enough to be photographed standing next to a cake. Then I walked down Lamplighter Hill in the rain, past City Hall, which was a good deal less sweet in stone, and at five o'clock I climbed the Selkirk's stairs, found the superintendent in his basement beside a boiler that sounded like a dying cow, and paid him eighty-four dollars of Mr. Swain's money. He wrote out a receipt on the back of a seed packet. I kept the packet. It had a picture of a very hopeful beet.

I ate a sandwich at Tommy's. I walked along Front Street as far as the hotel and stood under the awning of the shop opposite, and the Harbor stood there in the wet with its curtains drawn, eight rows of windows under the fourteen feet of terracotta waves, and nothing on any of them to say what it was hiding. At nine I climbed the stairs again, because a thing isn't known until it's typed, and because the party line goes quiet after eight, and because I had a day's worth of facts and no desk in the Flats to put them on.

My office door was unlocked.

I locked it every night. I lock it with a key on a loop of string that I keep inside my glove, and I had turned it at five. The glass with its gilt letters was dark. I pushed the door with two fingers and it swung inward on its hinges without a sound, and I stood on the threshold of room 309 and counted what I could see by the light from the hall.

The filing cabinet had four drawers and all four stood open. The folders from the bottom two lay on the floor in a long, even drift, as though a man had poured them out of a pail. The desk drawers had been pulled out and set on the desk in a row, each upside down. The chair was on its back. And from the filing cabinet, the typewriter case grinned at me with its lid thrown wide and its Royal portable still sitting in it, untouched, with the felt lining flat and the wire of the ribbon spool undisturbed.

I did not take the machine out. I lifted a corner of the felt with my thumbnail, in the dim, and slipped a finger under the board. The carbon lay where I had put it back, folded in four, soft as cloth. Whoever had been through my office had looked in the case for a typewriter and found one. They had not looked for anything underneath it. That told me they'd come for something that fit in a drawer.

The black address book with the rubber band was gone from the blotter. It had my mother's number in it, and Lucille's, and Dolan's, and the night desk at the Harbor, all written in pencil, in my own hand. A thing like that you lose without knowing how much of yourself it held.

I picked up the chair. I turned toward the desk, to put it down, and saw what was lying on the middle of the blotter.

An orange peel. One long, unbroken spiral, a coil of bright skin, laid out on the green felt with the white side up, in a perfect round. The cut at the end was clean. It had not been dropped, or thrown. It had been placed, with the care of a man setting down a hat in a church, in the exact centre of the blotter, so that I couldn't miss it, by somebody who had turned my office inside out and then taken the trouble to be tidy about the one thing he wanted me to see.

Somebody who wanted me to know he'd been polite.
