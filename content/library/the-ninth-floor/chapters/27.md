# The Cornice

The paint on the louvre was blistering.

I watched it from the kitchenette floor, where the air was, a hand's breadth above the linoleum: a black skin on a slat of fired clay, a yard over my head and out of my reach, rising in small bubbles like the skin on boiled milk and splitting one by one, and under every split a point of orange the size of a pin. It was the only window the ninth floor had. Somebody in 1929 had built a card room in the roof of a hotel and painted the vents black on the inside so that the lamps would not show in Front Street, and in twenty-three years nobody had thought of anything better.

Behind me the fire had the Club room, and the corridor, and the dormitories on either side of it. I did not turn to look. I knew the sound, a long ragged roar like a flag in a gale, with a counterpoint of small ticks and pops that were the iron cots letting go of their paint. The smoke had come down off the ceiling to the height of a standing woman's chin, so I stayed low. It was not black, as I had expected. It was brown, the colour of strong tea, and it tasted of lamp oil and of my own hair.

I got up on the bench. The enamel mugs stood along the back of it with their names inked on their sides, and they had begun to sweat. I put my back to the wall and my flat shoe to the lowest slat, and kicked.

The clay went in, three slats and then four, a rain of black flakes, and the iron frame held. I kicked the frame. It squealed on its pins. I kicked it again, and the whole panel left the wall in one piece and dropped out into the fog, and for as long as it takes to say it I heard nothing at all; then, a long way down, a flat clang in the yard. Cold air came in at the hole like a hand, and the fire behind me stood up to meet it.

The hole was two feet by eighteen inches, with the frame still standing round it. I wound my mother's grey scarf round my right hand and took the iron in my left, bare, because there was not enough scarf for both, and the iron burned. I will not describe it. I will say that I did not let go, which is a different thing from being brave.

I went through on my side, one shoulder and then the other, with the ledger in my coat catching on the frame, until I lay along a ledge on my belly with my legs still inside. Then I drew them out. A tongue of flame leaned from the hole after me and went up into the fog.

The ledge was two feet wide, wet terracotta, sloped a degree outward for the rain, with a fish on every foot of it. Below me there was nothing. I mean that as a measurement: eight floors of it, and the cobbles of Ferry Slip Row at the bottom, and a fog thick enough to lean on, so that if I fell I should at least be spared the sight. I stood up, which took three tries, and put my face to the Frieze and my arms along it. The fish were carp, or something near them, a foot long, with their lips pursed in relief, and each had a lip a hand could close on. Between them rose the terracotta waves, ridged like a washboard and no use to anyone.

I went east. I did not know how far I thought the corner was.

I counted the fish. It was not a habit that night. It was a handrail. Hand over hand, shuffle, one; the lip of the next, shuffle, two. By nine the louvre beside my face had begun to glow. At fourteen a slat spat a flake of burning paint past my ear. At twenty the whole band behind me lit, from the inside, the louvres going from black to dull red to orange one after another down the south face, so that I edged along the rim of a lampshade with the colour coming through the fog onto the back of my neck, and the Frieze I had stared at from the kerb for three weeks stood out before my eyes in perfect relief: the waves, the fish, my own fingers closing on a lip. In the wall the roar went up a tone.

At thirty-one the ledge turned a corner, and my left hand found two iron staples set in the clay, wet as dock rope. I am aware of what number that was. I did not remark on it to the fish.

The ladder was the eleven rungs I had climbed on the eighth of November in the rain, with a lantern talking behind a louvre. I could not close my hands on them. I hooked my forearms over each rung instead and let my feet find the next, like a woman going down a well with a sack of flour in each arm. Eleven. My shoes met the grating outside the eighth-floor end window. It had been an eventful month, I thought, and I was not sure which of us had come further, the grating or I.

The bell began while I stood there. Not the electric kind. A hand bell, brass, swung by a woman who had swung it once a year in a drill for twenty-six years and never once for real, and who swung it now as though she had been waiting for the chance.

I went down the fire escape with my forearms on the rail. At every landing the window beside me was lit, and in the window someone was being walked briskly to the stairs: a maid in grey, a guest in a dressing gown and a hat, an old lady carrying her teeth in her hand, each shepherded by one of Pearl's girls in a white apron with a clipboard, from the top down, with a bell. Pearl told me afterward that she had begun with nine. "There was nobody on nine, Miss Hightower." "I know that," she said. "It is the habit of six years." Ninety-one steps, I had counted going up, and I counted them going down, and the answer was ninety-one, which was the first thing that had gone right all evening.

The yard was full of people. Kitchen hands in white, maids in grey with their coats thrown over, thirty guests in dressing gowns and furs and hats, standing in the order Pearl had given them, in a fog the colour of a lampshade. Above them the light well was a shaft of orange cloud. In the kitchen door, with a clipboard in the crook of one arm and the ring of forty keys silent in her fist, stood Pearl herself, counting heads under her breath.

The service door beside the laundry chute opened on the yard as I reached the last landing, and a tall man in a dinner jacket came out of it with a cane over his forearm and walked into the crowd at the pace of a man leaving a restaurant where he has dined well. Nobody looked at him. Everyone was looking up. A tall old gentleman leaving a fire is the most natural thing in the world; there is always one. He turned toward the mouth of the alley and Ferry Slip Row, and I let go of the last rail, and dropped the last six feet, and landed in front of him.

He cleared his throat, once. For the first time in my acquaintance with him, he did not do it twice.

His eyes went to my face, which I had not seen and which must have been something, and then down to the front of my coat, where five pounds of green cloth made a square bulge under the belt. I watched him read it. I think it was the only time I ever saw him lose a figure.

The cane came up in his right hand, the silver collar level with his shoulder, as a man lifts his stick to hail a cab or tip a hat. It stayed there for a count of two. Then it came down.

"Mr. Quill."

Dolan stood in the mouth of the alley with his hat on the back of his head and no matchstick. Karras was at his shoulder with the notebook put away at last, and two uniformed men behind them with the fog on their capes. He was looking not at us but up, over the roof of the kitchen wing, where the fog above the light well glowed like the inside of a lantern and the Frieze stood out in it, a band of orange light over eight rows of dark windows. Nine, if you counted it. Dolan counted it.

"I count nine."

"The plans say eight, Sergeant," said Mr. Quill.

"Plans lie."

Karras stepped round me and took Mr. Quill's wrists, one and then the other, the way one takes a pair of gloves from a shelf, and the cuffs closed. Mr. Quill allowed it. He held out his hands as a man holds them out to be measured, and the cane, which had been over his arm, slid off it and struck the cobbles with a small clean sound and rolled and lay at my feet in the light from the kitchen door. I looked at the tip. A perfect disc of grey rubber, flat and round and dry, as unmarked as the day he bought it.

"Ambrose Quill," said Dolan. He said it as he had said Teddy's name in the lobby, reading out a bill he had not run up. "I'm holding you for the deaths of Declan Maher and Josiah Lambert, and for the setting of a fire in the Harbor Hotel with a woman in it, and for whatever else Mr. Barnwell finds by morning. You can have a lawyer. I'd get one that isn't Swain."

Mr. Quill said nothing at all. Not a word that meant nothing, either.

Pearl crossed the yard with the clipboard against her chest and the keys silent in her fist, and stood in front of him. For twenty-six years she had been invisible to him, and she let him see her. "Mr. Quill," she said. "Forty-six, with the kitchen. Every one counted out by me. I shall count them in." She turned back to her door. He had no answer, and I do not think he expected one.

"Seven-thirty," I said to Dolan. "You said seven-thirty."

"It's eight minutes to eight." He looked at my hands. His face did what it had done on my landing at five in the morning, once, and stopped. "Pardoe signed on his sister's porch step with a pencil at twenty to. I've got it in my pocket. It's the best paper I ever carried, and it came half an hour late."

"The law's always late, Frank."

"I know," he said. "It's what I'm for."

I took the green ledger out of my coat and gave it to him. The corner was scorched. "The Estate's receipts," I said. "Footed. I believe it balances."

Dolan took it in both freckled hands. Mr. Quill, between Karras and the constable, looked at his own ledger in the sergeant's grip, and then not.

Lucille Fong came round the corner of Ferry Slip Row at a trot, in a man's overcoat that touched her ankles, with a second coat folded over her arm. Her glasses were pushed up on her forehead and one end of a scarf was trailing. She stopped when she saw the yard, and the glow, and me.

"You said eight," she said. "It's four minutes to. I'm early." She held up the coat. "Pa says you are to put your arms in it and not tell him about it. He cut it the day you came to lunch. He said it was a professional embarrassment to leave a left shoulder like that to Sears."

It was grey herringbone, lined in black sateen, and it fit. It fit in the left shoulder, which cannot be said of any garment I have worn since I was nine. Lucille put it round me, because my arms would not go into sleeves, and buttoned it to the neck as if I were six, and then she saw my hands.

"Ruth."

"They'll heal."

"Ruth." She had her mouth shut hard, and the chipped tooth showed on the lip. "I'm going to sit down." She sat on an upturned bucket in a yard full of strangers with her father's overcoat bunched about her, and pulled her glasses down onto her nose, and watched the fire with the fixed attention of a woman reading very small print.

* * *

I went round to Front Street through the people in dressing gowns, to see the hotel from the kerb where I had stood on the fifth of November and counted its floors.

The brigade had come up Front Street with its bell going, Engine Company Four, two lines run to the hydrant at Ferry Slip Row and a third up the ladder truck to the cornice. They had been at it in six minutes from the first bell, by my father's Waltham. I looked at the dial twice. He had been right about that, too.

The Heifer sounded. I counted forty out of habit, and was glad, in a plain way, of the habit.

A hose played along the Frieze in a long white arc. The louvres, which had been red, went black again where the water touched them, and then, one after another along the band, the slats gave up their frames and fell, steaming, into the street, like tiles off a kitchen wall. Each one took a name with it. I knew which, and I stood in Walter Fong's coat with my hands wrapped in Lucille's handkerchief and my face wet and watched them go.

I will say what I felt, because a book that leaves it out is lying. I was glad. A wall of chalk names is evidence. Evidence is photographed and numbered and carried into court on an easel, and every Rūta and Halina in the Port Calder directory would have been a name a clerk could look up and a man from the Service could find. The fire was thorough, and I was grateful. And while I was grateful I thought of a small hand holding a stub of chalk to plaster at the height of a child's coat hook, and it did not help.

At about nine the ash began to come down. It came out of the glow where the Frieze had been, grey flakes as light as moth wings, turning over and over through the fog onto the roofs of the cars and the shoulders of the firemen and the hat brims of the crowd. A boy near me put out a hand and caught one. It was a small curl of burned paper with a ruled line across it, from a saints' calendar or a card-room ledger or a child's drawing of a train; nobody will ever know. He looked at it and blew it off his palm, and it went up and came down again on Front Street with the rest.

It fell all that night, soft and grey, like paper snow, and there was not a word on any of it.
