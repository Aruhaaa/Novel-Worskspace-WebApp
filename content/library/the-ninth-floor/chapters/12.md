# The Pit

A policeman's flashlight swung across the wall of the pit and found a rat.

The rat did not run. It sat on a bracket of the guide rail at about the height of my knee, with its forepaws together like a deacon at a graveside, and looked into the beam with the settled calm of a tenant who has been in residence longer than the landlord. The patrolman holding the light said a word Father Dziedzic would not have liked. For some seconds nobody in the boiler room of the Harbor Hotel did anything but consider the rat. Then Detective Karras, at the foot of the pit ladder, said "Excuse me" to it, quite politely, and set his tape measure down on the concrete.

It was twenty past twelve. Teddy had telephoned Central Station at five to, in the voice of a man reading a telegram to himself, and they had come: a patrol car, a second car, a photographer who turned the pit into a white room six times over and went away, and Dolan and Karras in the coats they had worn to the parade. I stood at the sill of the pit door with one hand on the iron. Dolan stood at my shoulder with his hat on the back of his head and his matchstick motionless. He had not said a word to me yet. He had looked at me and then at the pit, in that order, and I understood that the order was the message.

Karras measured. Seven feet from sill to floor; a ladder of seven rungs, slick where the oil off somebody's boots had been trodden onto them; a squat iron buffer, its top edge bright where something had struck it. He wrote it all down in a notebook held shut by a rubber band, which he took off and put back on for each entry. He was twenty-nine, with clean fingernails and a Greek profile that had been wasted on the police, and he had brought Teddy the grey blanket off Lambert's cot for his shoulders and called him "sir" three times, which was three more than anyone had called Teddy all night.

Dr. Lyman Ostrander came at half past twelve in a camel overcoat over striped pyjamas and carpet slippers, smelling of sleep and Sen-Sen, and went down the ladder one rung at a time like a man testing a bath. He was seventy and had signed forty years of death certificates, and he looked at Joe Lambert for about as long as it takes to unscrew a pen.

"A fall," he said, to the pit at large. "The rungs are wet with oil, the man has a can in one hand, he misses the fourth. Left temple against the edge of the buffer plate. He would not have known it." He straightened with a hand on his knee. "I'll sign in the morning, Sergeant. Accidental."

"Doctor," I said from the sill. "His hands."

They all turned. A flashlight beam came up the wall and across my face, which I thought unfair.

"What about his hands, Miss...?"

"Szymanski. Look at them."

He looked, because he was an old man and polite. Lambert's hands lay open on the concrete, palms up, white across the knuckles with old cab-rope scars. There was grease in the creases, years of it, where soap had given up. The fingers were clean. The palms were clean. Beside the right hand the brass oiler stood upright, and Karras, kneeling, tipped it toward the light and said, "Full, sir. Right to the spout."

"He hadn't started," said Dolan.

"He'd come down to do the guide shoes. Those rungs are as slick as a fish, Doctor. A man who slips off a slick rung grabs at it. His palms would be black to the wrist." I could hear my own voice, flat as a plate, the voice I had used on the wire for nine years when someone at the other end of it needed a fact and not a feeling. "I took calls from men who'd fallen off roofs and ladders and a church once. They all say the same thing. I grabbed."

"Miss," said Ostrander, with great kindness, "I have seen a great many dead men. They do not follow a pattern."

"Doc says a fall," said Dolan, and took me by the elbow.

He walked me back through the pipes as far as the boiler, where the heat came off its brick flank like a hand, and let go.

"You were here at midnight," he said. "The night clerk phones it in at five to, and at ten past he tells Karras that Ruth Szymanski was standing at his elbow when he found it. Why were you standing at his elbow, Ruth?"

"Mr. Wozniak asked me to look at the cab. He's had it in his head since Wednesday that somebody's been at it."

"At midnight."

"He works nights. Midnight's his noon."

"And Lambert?"

"I wanted to ask him whether the guide shoes hum."

Dolan looked at me. He took the matchstick out and looked at that instead. "A friend of Mr. Wozniak's asks you to look at a lift in the dead of night, and you come, and you find the porter, and the first thing you do is get a good view of his hands." He put the matchstick back. "You were never any good at it, Ruth. It's the one thing I always liked about you, and the one thing I asked of you once."

I said nothing. Behind him, at the foot of the stair, Karras had opened his notebook to a clean page. I watched him write a single word and underline it. I couldn't read it upside down at that distance, but I would have bet the Waltham it wasn't cab.

"Teddy," said Dolan, over my shoulder. "Is that right? Did you ask her?"

Teddy sat on the cot in his blanket. His eyes came to me, once, and went away. "I did, Sergeant. The car has behaved strangely since the fifth. I thought that a woman of Miss Szymanski's judgment..." He stopped there, for want of an end to it, and I owed him one.

That was when the stairs spoke. Someone was coming down them from the lobby, slowly, with the swish of quilted silk, and a cane with a rubber tip was set on each tread like a pencil being put down.

At the foot of the stair Ambrose Quill stopped. He wore a dark red quilted dressing gown tied with a cord, and soft leather slippers, and his silver hair was parted as exactly as if a professional had done it and set it. The cane lay over his left forearm like an umbrella. On his right cheek stood a single tear, which he did not wipe. I believed it. I've believed worse.

"Sergeant Dolan." He cleared his throat twice. "They woke me only now. I came as soon as I could stand. Poor Joe. He has been with this house since 1930."

"Mr. Quill. I'm sorry for your trouble."

"It is the house's trouble, Sergeant. Joe was the house." He looked across the pipes at the open pit door, and I watched the tear travel to the corner of his jaw. Then the pale eyes found me. "Miss Szymanski. You are here before me again. I wonder whether you would do me the kindness of taking tea in the morning. Nine o'clock, in 806. There is something I ought to say, and I would rather say it in daylight than in a cellar."

"Nine," I said.

"I am obliged." He bowed from the neck, to Dolan, to Teddy, and to the dead man he could not see, and went back up the stair, the quilted silk going before him.

Dolan watched until the silk was gone. "Ten years I've known that man," he said, "and I've never once seen him in a dressing gown."

"He's allowed to wear one in his own hotel."

"I know it." The matchstick went round. "Go home, Ruth."

* * *

I slept four hours in my clothes with my shoes on the floor beside me and Joe Lambert's key under the pillow, a thing I'd have been ashamed of in a child. At nine I was in the Harbor lobby again, in daylight, with the fog burnt back to the water. Dorrie Nash stood behind the brass rail with no gum in her cheek and her eyes pink to the lids.

"He gave me a peppermint every Monday," she said, to nobody, when she saw me. "He called me Miss Nash." It was the only thing I ever heard her say in that building that wasn't a rule.

The car was running again. I rode it up with a bellhop who studied the floor between us like a man at a graveside. The plug under the 8 was dry now, a cream coin that anyone could have pried up with a nail. Over the door the needle swung across its arc and settled on the eight a hair short of the mark, and past it the brass ran on another inch and a half to the tick with no number on it.

Quill opened the door of 806 himself. He was in the charcoal suit and the high collar, with the cane over his arm, looking like a man who had sat up all night and had it pressed afterward. The room was a sitting room like a captain's cabin. A narrow bed made tight as a drum stood behind a lacquered screen. There was a round table at the window with the tea things laid out, two chairs, a shelf of black cloth ledgers like the ones in the office downstairs, and in the corner a squat green safe with a vase of bronze chrysanthemums on top, as if to apologise for it. The window gave on the ferry slip and the Sound beyond, grey on grey. Over the door hung a brass arc with a needle, a sister of the one in the cab, and below it, screwed to the plaster, a small brass toggle under a hinged glass cap, like the fire alarm in a school corridor. On a hook behind the door hung a dark red quilted dressing gown. It had been on him seven hours before. I looked at all of it left to right, the way I look at any room, and sat where he showed me.

"Tea," said Quill, and poured. It was tarred rope again. "I owe you an apology and a correction, Miss Szymanski, and at my age I find they are very nearly the same animal. On Wednesday I told you that the Harbor has eight floors. It has nine."

"You said the plans, the licence and the Board of Underwriters would tell me eight."

"And so they will. They have been telling it since 1926." He cleared his throat twice before he went on. I wrote it down. A hotel full of men with colds in a wet November, but I wrote it down anyway, on the back of the pink notice, under Estate and Marchand. "The Mezzanine was never put on the licence. It was the Calder Club once, in the dry years, a card room for old Mr. Calder's friends. After Repeal it was shut. I have kept the key to it for twenty years."

"Mezzanine."

"Forgive me. The ninth floor, as I suppose I must call it now." He had said it the way my mother says Hake Street, without noticing there was another way to say it. "A habit of the old days."

"What happened on it on Tuesday night?"

"I do not know." He set the pot down. "That is the plain truth, my dear, and I should be glad if you would believe the other plain truth with it. Teddy telephoned my room at ten past four. I came. A man helped me. We found Mr. Delaney on a cot in the Mezzanine, dead, and I knew that if it were known the hotel would be closed by Friday and sixty people turned out of their places: the maids, the kitchen, Miss Hightower, a porter who has been here since 1930. So I moved him. I put him in a bed he had paid for, and I told Teddy there was no ninth floor, which was cruel, and I told you the same, which was merely necessary. I did it for them. I would do it again." Click, click. "It is a curious thing to find that one cannot go on telling a woman what she does not believe."

"The man who helped you."

"I will not name him. He is past being hurt by it, but I will not hang his name on a nail."

"Joe Lambert."

"I did not say so," said Quill gently, and drank his tea.

I let a gull cross the window. "You told the police Mr. Delaney's heart."

"I told them what they would accept. Dr. Ostrander says heart; Dr. Ostrander says a great many things. But Joe has oiled those rails for twenty-two years, and I do not believe he came off a ladder in the twenty-third." He turned his cup a quarter turn by the saucer. "A man has died in my house, and I think a second. I would like you to find the person who did it. I will pay you fifteen dollars a day and your expenses, and you may tell the Sergeant whatever you judge best."

"I'm working for Mr. Wozniak."

"Teddy cannot afford you for long. The Estate can."

"The Estate's lawyer paid me five hundred dollars last Thursday to say there was no ninth floor."

Something went over his face, very fast, and was folded and put away. He sighed. "Gilbert," he said. "Yes. I am only the Estate's auditor, Miss Szymanski. Mr. Swain holds the pen, and sometimes he holds it too tightly. I count what I am shown."

"And what are you shown?"

"A great deal less than you suppose. Think it over, my dear. Drink your tea."

I drank it. It was good tea. He talked for ten minutes about the Dorset churchyard where his mother lay and a spaniel he had owned in 1904, and I listened, and liked him, and watched the glass cap on the toggle over his door take the light from the window and give it back.

* * *

In the corridor the oxblood nap had been brushed that morning, in long stripes, by one of Pearl Hightower's girls. Across the stripes, from the door of 806 toward the lift, ran a line of small round dents, evenly spaced, each the size of a half dollar. I counted them. Eleven. I looked along the line for the odd one, as you look for the odd man in a queue, and there wasn't one. They were as round as coins.

I stood there with Joe Lambert's key in my pocket and a thin cup's worth of tea going cold inside me. I put my hand in and found the brass cold. It had been warm all night, from him and then from me, and I couldn't have told you when it stopped.

I liked Ambrose Quill. I stood in his corridor and noted that, as you note a draught, and could not have said why it worried me.
