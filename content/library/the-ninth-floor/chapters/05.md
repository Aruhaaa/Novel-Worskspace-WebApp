# The Empty Folder

"Number, please," said Lucille Fong, and brought down a rubber stamp on a form without looking up.

I had no number. I stood at the brass grill of the counter in the Harbor Commission's records room, on the ground floor of the Customs Annex at Front and Cutter, with the rain still on my hat, and watched the top of her head. A pencil stuck out of the dark hair above her left ear, sharpened that morning to a needle. Behind her, rows of green steel shelves ran back into the gloom under a sign that said NO FOOD NO SMOKING NO EXCEPTIONS, and in front of her stood a wire basket of forms, which she was working through with the blank, metronome pleasure of a woman playing a hand she knows she'll win.

"Please hold," she said, and stamped another.

"I'm holding."

"Not yet you're not. I can't hear you holding." She lifted her face at last. Small, round tortoiseshell glasses, a chipped front tooth, and eyes that had lain in wait behind the lenses for me since I came in the door. "Well. If it isn't the woman who left the wire room and took the good chair with her."

"It was a terrible chair."

"It was a terrible chair that I got for nothing from the Water Department, and you walked off with it in June of 'forty-eight in a taxi." She came to the counter and looked me up and down. "You look like you've been sleeping in that hat."

"It's my best hat."

"That's what worries me, Ruth."

We had shared a headset for seven years, her left ear and my right, in a room with no windows on the third floor of Central Station, and it left us with a way of talking in which neither of us has ever once been polite. Lucille Fong is thirty-four. She is the Senior Clerk of Vessel Records, and she will be the Chief Clerk of the Harbor Commission the day the Commission stops promoting men called Pruitt over her. She has never said a word against Mr. Pruitt. She says "Mr. Pruitt" the way other women say "the weather."

Behind her a pale young man in a green eyeshade, with a frightened pimple on his chin, was shelving folders so slowly that every one of them was a conversation. "Mr. Tibbets," Lucille said, not turning. "There's a drawer in the back that needs looking at. Go and look at it from a long way off."

"Which drawer, Miss Fong?"

"Any drawer." He went. She leaned on the counter. "Now. Whatever you want, it'll cost you lunch."

"I want a man named Declan Maher. Customs tally clerk, let go September of 1950. I want to know what the word irregularities was covering."

"I looked him up before you came," Lucille said. "I'm a woman of foresight. He telephoned the Commission twice in October, and both times asked for Vessel Records, and both times the call went to Mr. Tibbets, who told him I was at lunch. I'll want to talk to Mr. Tibbets about his judgement." She took the pencil out of her hair, wrote on a slip, and put it back. "Tally Clerk, Grade Three. He counted what came off the ships and checked it against the paper. Dismissed for irregularities. That's what they say when they can't prove a thing and can't stand the smell of it. Is he your trouble?"

"He's dead."

"I'm sorry. Was he nice?"

"I don't know yet."

"That's the trouble with the dead." She slid the slip over. "What else?"

"A date. Sunday night, the fourteenth of March, 1948. Pier 6. What came in."

She didn't move, and the way she didn't was a sort of answer. The rain ticked on the high windows. "March of 'forty-eight," she said, in a lower voice. "That's your month."

"It's a date."

"You left in June. I thought about that for a long time, you know. I thought about it on the streetcar." She waited for me to say something, and when I didn't, she drew a breath through her nose, and the clerk came back into her face like a shutter coming down. "Sunday the fourteenth. I don't need the berth book for that, which means I'll look in it anyway. Hold the line."

She went back among the shelves. I heard a drawer, a long pause, and a drawer again. She returned with a green folder under one arm and her glasses pushed up her forehead, which is how she wears them when she is angry.

"There was one ship at Slip 3 that night. Liberty ship, American flag, the *Esther Vane*, in from Antwerp by way of Halifax, docked at nine ten p.m., sailed on the tide the Tuesday. Master, a Captain Oakes." She laid the folder on the counter and opened it. Five tabs, printed in the Commission's blue: ARRIVAL. CREW. MANIFEST. TALLY. CLEARANCE. "Look at that and tell me what's wrong."

I looked. Behind the first tab lay a single sheet. Behind the other four tabs lay nothing at all. The cardboard dividers stood in their folder like empty chairs in a hall.

"There's no manifest."

"There's a tab called MANIFEST with nothing behind it. The master's copy goes to Customs, but we're supposed to hold the Commission's copy. It should be here. It isn't. Somebody took it, or nobody ever put it in." She tapped the one sheet. "And that. That's the arrival sheet. Read it."

The sheet was typed. At the top was the ship's name and the time of docking, and beneath that the crew: seventy-one men, tallied by rating, deck and engine and steward. At the bottom, in the margin, someone had written a note and a figure in pencil, and the note said that thirty-one of them were "discharged ashore by mutual consent."

"Thirty-one," I said.

"A Liberty carries forty. Forty-one if the cook has a nephew. Nobody loses thirty-one men in a night. That's a mutiny or a lie, and a mutiny would have been written up." Lucille took her glasses down and put them on. "And look at the paper."

I'd been looking at it without seeing it, which is how paper works. The Commission's own sheets were on buff stock, thin and pulpy, the kind that smells of glue. This one was white bond, heavier, with a watermark that showed when I tilted it to the high window. It had been typed on a different machine, too. I know typefaces the way other people know hymns. This was an Underwood pica, and the capital E sat a hair low on the line, the way an old friend's coat hangs lower on one side.

"Typed somewhere else," I said. "Then slipped in."

"Typed somewhere else, then slipped in. At night, I'd say, by somebody who has a key and a very good opinion of himself." Lucille closed the folder with the flat of her hand. "I can't take it out of the room, Ruth. I can't even let you take notes in the room with Tibbets breathing. But I'll let you put your thumb on the number so you remember it."

I put my thumb on the pencilled 31. It felt like nothing, like paper.

"While I've got you," I said. "The Harbor Hotel. Is there a 1926 building permit in this place?"

"The Commission holds permits for anything inside the pier-head line, for the fireboat easement. The Harbor's inside it by about four feet, God help it." She narrowed her eyes. "Why do you want a hotel?"

"To count the floors."

"You always did count." She wrote that on another slip. "I'll find the permit. I can't show it to you here either. Give me a day, and I'll bring a copy, in an envelope, in my handbag, like a smuggler, and you'll owe me a slice of pie at Tommy's."

"I owe you the chair."

"You owe me the chair." She took her coat off a hook. "Lunch. I said it'd cost you."

* * *

Anchor Alley is off Front Street north of Cutter, a lane three people wide where the rain came straight down between the roofs and nowhere else. It was the old Chinese quarter, and what was left of it was a few blocks of tailors, herbalists and a steam laundry whose vents breathed out a warm, soapy cloud. Number 14 had a window with a half-curtain, three bolts of serge in it, and a hand-lettered sign in gilt on black: FONG & SON, TAILORS TO THE SEA.

"There's no son," I said.

"The Son is aspirational." Lucille opened the door. "Dad has me."

"You're a clerk."

"I'm a clerk who can hem."

A bell rang over the door, and the shop took me in like a warm sleeve. It smelled of hot wool and camphor and a steam iron. Along one wall ran a shelf of bolts, serge and gabardine and a dark blue melton for pea coats, and beyond them a long cutting table scored with chalk, and two dress forms in half-made jackets, standing at attention. At the table, with a tape measure hung round his neck like a stole, a man in a waistcoat was marking a length of grey cloth with a sliver of tailor's chalk. He was sixty-six, thin and upright, with cropped white hair and a pair of half-glasses low on his nose, and he did not look up until he had finished the line.

Then he looked at me, and it was an odd thing to be looked at. He did not meet my eyes. His gaze went to my shoulders, down my arms to my wrists, across my chest, down my back as I turned a little, which I hadn't meant to do, then to my feet, and it came back to my face with the air of a man who has read a long letter and approves of the handwriting.

"Thirty-seven inch chest," he said. "Thirty-six sleeve. You stoop a little, and the left shoulder is lower, because you carry something on that side."

"A handbag."

"A bag with a book in it, I would say, or a notebook." He nodded at the belted navy raincoat. "Who cut this?"

"Sears."

"Sears has never met you." He said it to the chalk. Then there was a pause as long as a stitch, and the corner of his mouth moved. "I apologise. That was rude to Sears."

Lucille groaned. "Dad. This is Ruth. Ruth Szymanski. The telephone Ruth."

"The one with the chair." He put down the chalk and inclined his head to me, a small, precise bow, and said, in a clear, slightly formal English that had not a rough edge anywhere in it, that he was Walter Fong and that I was welcome in his shop. He did not shake hands. His hands, I saw, were spotless to the wrist and scored across the first two fingers of the left with a hundred tiny white lines, the scars of a lifetime of needle.

We ate standing up at the cutting table, on newspaper, from a paper sack Lucille had fetched round the corner: pork buns, hot enough to hurt, and a pot of tea on a gas ring. For a while nobody said anything useful. Walter Fong talked about the weather, which he held to be the fault of the Navy, and about Eisenhower, whom he considered a tolerable cutter and no sort of tailor.

"Dad," Lucille said. "Ruth is looking for a man named Maher. He's dead. He worked on the piers."

"I did not know him."

"And she wants to know about a ship in 1948."

"The *Esther Vane*," I said. "A Liberty ship. Thirty-one of her crew were discharged ashore on the night of the fourteenth of March. Seventy-one men on the Commission's sheet, and thirty-one of them let go at the gangway."

Walter Fong set down his tea.

"Thirty-one," he said.

"You know the number?"

"I know it because I made them." He turned to the shelf, to where the pea coats hung, dark and square, and he laid one palm on a sleeve. "I was called to the shop on a Sunday evening, in March. A man came to the door, a man with a clerk's voice, a very smooth voice, a voice that says *sign here* and means *do not read it*. He wished thirty-one pea coats and thirty-one watch caps, fitted, that night, at a pier. He paid in cash. I took the coats down on a handcart, with the boy from the laundry to push, and my tape and my chalk, and I fitted thirty-one persons in a shed by a lantern."

"Persons," I said.

"Men." He looked at the sleeve. "Most of them were men."

"Most?"

"In my trade, Miss Szymanski, you do not look at the man who pays cash for thirty-one coats. That is the first rule of the trade, and it is a good one. I did not look at him. I looked at shoulders." A little dryness came into his voice a beat late, like the second note of a bell. "I was told not to ask what they were for. I did not. But I will tell you that not one of them spoke to me, and several of them held out their arms for the measure afterward as if they had been taught to do it by someone much worse."

I did not trust myself with the pork bun. "Do you remember what he looked like? The man who paid?"

"I did not look at him." He lifted his eyes, now, to mine, and kept them there. "I kept the order book. I keep all of them. Every coat I have made since 1912 is in a book in the back, with its measurements and its price and the name of the man who wore it, or the man who paid. If you should need those books, they will not be going anywhere. I have never thrown away a measurement."

"That's the best thing anyone's said to me this week."

"It is not a high standard." He picked up the tea.

On the wall behind the cutting table, where the steam from the iron had been dulling it for years, hung a framed certificate. It was an old thing, gilt on black, with a border of lines and a row of tall characters across the top, and under them an English translation typed on a card: THIS IS TO CERTIFY THAT LAU WAI-LOK HAS SERVED HIS APPRENTICESHIP IN THE GUILD OF MERCHANT TAILORS AT CANTON AND IS FOUND SKILLED. A date, 1903. The name on the sign over the door outside said FONG. The name on the wall said Lau. I looked at it for about the time it takes to turn a page.

"Ruth," Lucille said, from slightly too close. "Have another bun. You're about to say something and you'll be sorry. Have a bun."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"Good. Then eat."

Her father looked at her. He did not say anything either, and in the pause the iron ticked, and the rain went on drumming in the lane. I took the bun. I have spent my life learning how much to leave out of a conversation, and it seemed a poor day to forget.

* * *

The rain had slackened to a drizzle by the time I went out. Lucille stayed behind to be scolded in two languages, and I stood in the lane under the shop's awning to turn up my collar. The steam laundry across the way breathed its soapy cloud over the cobbles. A boy went by with a barrow of wet sacking. And in the arch of the laundry's loading door, perfectly still, stood a man in a long dark coat and a snapped-brim hat, with the brim pulled down in front so that his eyes were a rumour.

He was peeling an orange.

He did it with a pocketknife, a thick one, held in a thick right hand, and he did it without once looking at it. The peel came away in a single spiral, a long coil of bright skin growing longer and longer and turning slowly on its own weight, and all the time his face, under the brim, stayed pointed across the alley at me. The lane was three people wide. I could have thrown a bun at him. He didn't hurry, and he didn't hide. He had the air of a man who has all the afternoon, and has chosen you to spend it on.

I held his look for a count of ten. Then the coil reached the end of the fruit, and he let it go.

It dropped from his hand to the wet cobbles whole, and lay there in a coil, as neat as a new telephone cord.
