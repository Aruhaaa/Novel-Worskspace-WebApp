# Over the Bakery

At eight o'clock Mrs. Sobczak's ovens came up to heat, and the wall of the stairwell at 1131 Hake Street turned warm under my hand, like a flatiron left standing on a shelf. The whole stair smelled of rye and caraway and the sour of the starter. Downstairs, through the glass of the shop door, I could see her in a white apron to her ankles, shaping tomorrow's loaves on a floured board with a hand on each end, as if she were wringing necks. Eighteen steps to the landing, one of them a board that sounded a note. A card was tacked to the door: T. WOZNIAK, in a small square clerk's hand that I would have known anywhere by now.

He opened before I knocked. He had heard me on the stair, he said, and he had known the step, and I wondered when I'd begun to be a step.

His two rooms were one room and a closet. The front room held a table with an oilcloth cover, two straight chairs, a hot plate on a shelf with an enamel kettle on it, and a crucifix over the door whose Christ had been dusted that morning. A shelf of Polish books ran along one wall, the spines cracked white, a missal at one end and a paperbound *Pan Tadeusz* at the other with its back broken. Through the closet door I could see a narrow iron bed made so tight you could have bounced a coin off it. He wore a clean collar and his waistcoat, and the jacket with the brass nameplate hung on the back of a chair, ready for eleven o'clock the way a man's good shoes are ready on the mat.

"Please. Sit." He gave me the chair that faced the window and took the other himself. "I have coffee, but it is a Polish coffee. It will be an ordeal."

"I'll be careful."

He made it anyway, in a glass, with the grounds still floating on it, and set it in front of me on a saucer, and I drank it, and it was an ordeal. Above the shelf hung a photograph in a frame of black wood: a heavy man in a sexton's long black coat, with a face like a locked door and hands as large as shovels, and beside him, coming to the height of his shoulder, a small woman in rimless spectacles, holding a handbag in front of her with both hands. Neither one was smiling. They looked as if they had been told not to.

"Who's that?"

"My sister's husband." He didn't turn his head. "Dead."

"And the lady?"

"My sister."

I let that lie, the way you leave a lamp on in a room you may need to come back to. I took out my notebook, which was the pink notice, and laid it flat on the oilcloth.

"The man you found wasn't named Delaney," I said. "His name was Declan Maher. He worked for Customs as a tally clerk until the autumn of 1950, when they let him go."

Teddy Wozniak's hand had reached for the pencil that lay on the oilcloth beside a blotter, a green one, folded in the corner where he must have written his letters. The pencil was lying square to the blotter's edge already. His fingers moved it a quarter inch to the left, and then back.

"Maher," he said.

"Say it as if you'd heard it."

"I have heard it now, from you." His eyes were on the pencil. "I checked in a Mr. Delaney of Portland, and he signed the book as Delaney, and paid three nights in advance, in cash, which is why I recall it. I believed what was in the book. A night clerk who doubted the book would have no work."

"Had you seen him before Friday?"

"No."

"Never?"

"Never, Miss Szymanski. I have a memory for faces. It is the only property I own."

"Then tell me why a man you'd never seen wrote my name on your blotter."

He had an answer for that, and I saw it come up from somewhere low, the way a bucket does, with some effort and a little spilling. "On Monday night, while I made up the keys, Mr. Delaney asked in a general way whether I knew a private detective. He said, an honest one. I said I did not. I have no use for such persons. He said," and the gold tooth showed, apologetic, "then I know one. And he wrote your name. A woman who had been on the police wire. I did not ask how he knew of you. It is not a clerk's business to ask a guest how he knows things."

"And you came to me because a stranger told you to."

"I came to you because a stranger who was dead had told me you were honest. There is a certain kind of recommendation that cannot be improved upon." He moved the pencil a quarter inch to the right.

I sipped the coffee, which fought back. "I'll ask you again how you got to nine."

"I will tell you when I know I can."

"That isn't an answer."

"No." His hand went to his waistcoat. The tin rattled, a dry little fall of seeds, and he took the hand away again as if it had been caught at something. "It is a promise. I do not make many. May I ask one thing in return, Miss Szymanski? Please do not go near the cab. Mr. Quill will take it as an insult, and he is a man who is hurt by distrust. He has been a friend to me."

Mr. Quill had also been very kind to me, and I'd noticed the cane. I thought of the round of cream paint on my thumb, and of the shoes in their bag, and of five hundred dollars in a drawer on Cutter Street that I hadn't mentioned either. We sat at an oilcloth table, the two of us, each with a hand held back under it. It had the look of an honest transaction.

"I'll stay out of the cab," I said, which was not a lie in the tense I meant it in.

He bowed his head. Below us, in the shop, Mrs. Sobczak slammed a tray, and the floor hummed under our feet, and settled.

* * *

My mother's kitchen is four doors down from the bakery, at 1127, up one flight, over a Lithuanian cobbler who sings. She had kept supper for me since six, and told me so on the landing before she opened the door. A short, wide woman in a head scarf with arms like rolled dough and her reading glasses on a string round her neck, Helena Szymanska can say more with a lid than most people do with a sermon, and the one she had on the pot was a reproach.

"Rutka. Sit. Eat. A detective who faints is only a woman lying down."

"I haven't fainted since 1931."

"You are thin since 1931." She put three cabbage rolls in front of me in a sea of tomato, a heap of potatoes with dill, and a plate of cucumbers in sour cream, and stood over them like a customs officer. "Eat first. Talk second. The Americans do it the other way and look what it gives them."

I ate. Cabbage rolls are not a food you talk through. The kitchen was warm from the stove and the one lamp, and the window over the sink had gone silver with steam. The Philco on the shelf was off, which meant she had been saving something up. She waited until I had put away two of the rolls.

"So. A general."

"Mama."

"Thirty-nine states, the radio says. A general is a man who asks other men to die, and then writes a book about it. I would have voted for the other one, the professor, the one with the hole in his shoe. A man who admits to a hole in his shoe is a man who can be fed."

"You did vote for him."

"I voted for him twice. In my heart." She sat down across from me and picked up her darning, a brown sock stretched on a wooden egg, and I thought of a sock with a hole in it, lying on a steel table, and put down my fork for a second. "You were at Sobczak's," she said.

"I was visiting a client."

"Four doors away you could not come up for a glass of tea first?" The needle went in and out. "A client with eyebrows."

"You saw him."

"I see everything on Hake Street, Rutka, I have a window. A little man in a hotel jacket. He buys a rye on Saturdays and sits in the last pew at the six o'clock and goes out before the blessing. I have never heard him say a word to a living person." She bit off the wool. "Wozniak. Yes? Tadeusz Wozniak."

I had not said the name. I looked at her, and she looked at the sock.

"Bronia Kowal's brother," she said. "Everyone knows. You don't know?"

"I don't know a Bronia Kowal."

"You know nothing. You were a policewoman, and you know nothing about the street where you sleep." She said it kindly, which was worse. "Bronia Kowal, at the church. Small, black, glasses without a rim, a rosary on her wrist, and always the handbag, with both hands, as if it would run off. Seventeen months a widow. Her husband was Stefan Kowal, the sexton. He kept the St. Raphael box, God rest him."

"What's a St. Raphael box?"

"What do they teach you? Raphael is the saint of travellers. By the door of the church there is a box for the travellers, for Masses, for the poor sailors off the ships. Stefan Kowal emptied it every Friday and never said where it went." She crossed herself, with the needle still in her hand, so that she drew a small silver cross in the air. "A saint, that man. With a temper. He once chased the bishop's driver down Eleventh Avenue with a candle snuffer, because the man parked on the church steps. It was a Christmas. The snuffer was brass."

"Did he catch him?"

"He caught the car. He broke a lamp." She smiled, briefly, a thing that goes across my mother's face like the shadow of a bird across a window. "Who is dead, Rutka? Is somebody dead?"

"A man in a hotel. A guest. The police say natural."

"And what do you say?"

"I say I'm not yet paid enough to say."

"Hm." She went back to the sock. After a while she said, in a different voice, to the wool, "The Kowals were people who kept their doors open. That's all. Eat your potatoes."

I ate my potatoes. It struck me over the dill that she hadn't asked me one question I would have asked in her place, and that my mother is a woman who asks every question there is.

* * *

At twenty past eleven I stood alone at the sink, with the dishes done and the lamp turned down, looking out of the window the way I do when something won't sit still in my head. Helena had gone to bed at ten with a candle and a prayer for my future, the future part rather long. The window over the sink looks north, across the back yards of the Flats, the tarred roofs, the chimneys, the clotheslines hung like loose telegraph wire, and the rain had thinned to a mist the colour of dishwater. Past the roofs the ferry slip showed its red lamp. And beyond that, ten blocks off, set down on the edge of the water with nothing near it as tall, stood the Harbor Hotel.

I knew it by the shape. In the dark it had no windows to speak of, only a cliff of brick with a few lit squares, the night people, the late guests, a lamp that stayed on in an office on the second floor. Eight rows, I thought, and then did not think, because I was already counting from the bottom with a finger on the glass, and I came to the eighth, and above the eighth there was the black band of the cornice where no floor should be.

A light moved in it.

It was small and yellow, no brighter than a lantern held low, and it came out at the Ferry Slip end of the black band and slid along it from left to right, a little behind the slats, so that I saw it only in pieces, through the louvres, bar by bar, like a hand running along the keys of a piano. I counted. Eleven seconds. At the far end, toward Front Street, it stopped, and for a moment the whole row of slats stood out in faint dull lines, as if the cornice had been drawn on the dark in red pencil.

Then it went out.

The night clerk of the Harbor Hotel was at his desk by now, with his key in his watch pocket and his pillbox in his waistcoat. At ten I had seen him from the front window, going down Hake Street in his raincoat with a lunch tin under one arm, and he had turned the corner for the Number 14 without once looking back. Whoever had a lantern on that floor was not Teddy Wozniak. And whoever it was had gone, with the patience of a man doing something he did every night of his life, along a row of black slats from one end of the building to the other, as if inspecting them.

I stood at the window until the water in the sink had stopped draining. Out on Calder Point the Heifer sounded, two notes, the second lower, and the tin of the roofs took up the sound and gave it back to me as a kind of shudder in the glass. It came again at forty. The band above the eighth floor stayed black the whole time.
