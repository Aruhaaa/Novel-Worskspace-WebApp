# Under the Hatband

The Police Benevolent Association Band, eleven men in braided tunics, three of them Lutherans, played "The Wearing of the Green" for the retirement of a man who had been born in Dayton, Ohio, and it was the first dry night in two weeks. A moon like a clean dinner plate stood over Calder Sound. The Pacific Room's tall windows were open at the top for the cigar smoke, and the cold came in at them and met the chicken.

I sat at table six in the one dress I own that isn't black, which is navy wool and had last been to my father's funeral, and the hat I'd worn for Plum. Dolan sat on my left in a dinner jacket that had been rented, with the tags still on the lining, and a gold ring on a chain tucked out of sight under his shirt. On his right sat a lieutenant from Traffic and his wife. Between the pickled beets and the roast capon I learned a good deal about the department's pension plan, none of it cheerful. Karras sat at the next table, eating very neatly and watching the door like a boy who has been told not to.

The room held three hundred. I counted the heads of the men I knew and then, to be honest, the exits. At the head table beneath a banner reading GOD BLESS OUR DEPUTY CHIEF sat Desmond Rafferty himself, sixty years old and built like a mail chute, with white hair cut square across the nape and a face the colour of rare beef. On one side of him was the District Attorney, Hollis Barnwell, a thin careful man who cut his capon into equal pieces and ate them in order. On the other was Everett Plum, pink as a ham under the lights, the stickpin winking, passing his handkerchief across his lip every forty seconds, so that I began to time it. Gilbert Swain sat three seats along with his back turned to the room. Far down the table, in a white dinner jacket and a white carnation, his cane laid across his knees, Ambrose Quill was explaining something to Mrs. Barnwell with a gentle forefinger, and she was nodding as though to the Gospel.

At the table behind the band, with an orange on his plate that no one had given him and a hat on the chair beside him, Aldo Ferrante was peeling it.

Rafferty rose for the toast. He spoke for twenty minutes. He told us what forty years of service had taught him, which was chiefly that a good policeman gets to the corner before the crowd, and when he came to the line about the women who had served in the radio room in the early days, and how a brave voice on the wire had kept more men alive than a company of bayonets, he found my eye across the room and lifted his glass.

"Ruth Anne Szymanski," he said. "Where is she? The best voice on the wire. I told Frank, you'll bring her back one day."

There was a patter of applause. I had my hands in my lap and my father's watch against my wrist, and I put the two together until the clapping stopped. The man at the head table was smiling at me, and I knew the order he had given on a March night in 1948, because it had come down a line to me by way of a sergeant, and I had typed it, as I know my own address. *Cars out of the area, 2300 to 0030.* I smiled back at him, the way you do to a dog you intend to go on living beside.

"Smile," said Dolan beside me, without moving his lips. "Then eat something."

"I'm smiling."

"That's what you do when you're not."

The band struck up something I would rather not have been told was the Port Calder Police Anthem, and the room rose, and Ambrose Quill, who had come down the aisle against the crowd with the cane over his arm, like a man carrying a lantern through a fair, put his long hand lightly on my sleeve.

"Miss Szymanski. You must let me show you the one handsome thing in this room."

It was a photograph on the piano. The Pacific Room's baby grand stood by the stage with its lid down, and on it, in a silver frame the size of a tea tray, a family had been arranged on a lawn under a monkey-puzzle tree: a stout old man with a watch chain, a lady in a hat, two women in white with parasols and the same long chin. Quill held the cane before him with both hands.

"Mr. Hallam Calder, in 1913, with his wife and his nieces. I stood behind the photographer and held the plate." He cleared his throat twice. "The ladies in white are Miss Honor and Miss Verity. They are in Santa Barbara now, the last of the line, God keep them. They have not seen Port Calder in twenty years. Miss Honor is somewhat deaf, I'm told. She writes me at Christmas in a very large hand."

"They own all this?"

"They own, my dear, a name." He smiled at the glass. "The rest is a trust."

His shoes were black and flat and clean to the welt, and the rubber tip of the cane had come to rest on the carpet between them, a perfect round, the colour of a new eraser. I looked at it long enough for him to notice me looking, and he turned the cane a quarter turn on its tip and went on smiling.

Plum caught me at the cloakroom corridor. He came out of nowhere, sweating, the handkerchief in one fist and a water glass in the other that rattled against his teeth.

"Miss Szymanski! Friends!" There was no one else there. "A moment. I want it understood. Whatever happened at my little celebration on the fourth, it was, it was a conflagration of circumstance, you follow me, an inadvertical thing, and I had no part. A pier is a lease. I have never lifted a pencil in the matter of a berth. Two men, Miss Szymanski, two! The papers say a third man will be asked questions. I am a man of the people, and I ask you, would the people stand for it?"

"Mr. Plum, nobody's said you did anything."

"No. No!" He dabbed. "That's what frightens me. My wife says I haven't the brains to do harm, and I take it as a comfort, and tonight I find I can't hold a glass." He lifted it; the water slopped over his cuff. "You see?"

I did see. A man who could not hold a tumbler steady could not have held a decanter, nor a secret, and when he went back into the Pacific Room I put him down in my head as the worst conspirator in the history of the Seventh Ward, which was a distinction.

* * *

Dolan found me at ten, when the District Attorney was making his own toast. "Coat," he said, and walked me round the back of the band into the men's cloakroom as though we'd come to collect his. The attendant, a boy in a bellman's jacket, looked at the ceiling and left the cubicle. It smelled of wet wool, bay rum, and the hair of three hundred men's hats.

"I wasn't going to show you tonight," said Dolan. "I'm showing you tonight because I think by morning it'll be a different thing."

The evidence envelope came out of the breast pocket where I had seen it that afternoon. He held it flat, with his thumb across the mouth. Inside was a grey felt hat, and inside the hat, when he turned back the sweatband, a strip of ruled paper no wider than a wallet-card, folded in four.

"Maher's," he said. "Pugh bagged it on the Thursday and nobody opened the bag. Friday, after Lambert, I did." He unfolded the slip with two fingers. "It was under the sweatband, where a man keeps a bill he means to remember."

*NINE. HALF PAST MIDNIGHT. BRING IT.*

Block capitals, thick-nibbed, in a brown that went on to the edge of red where the pen had pressed. The paper had been torn from a pad. I held it under the cloakroom lamp.

"Ink like old blood," I said. It was a phrase that had been lodged in my head for a week and I didn't ask where from. "Bring what?"

"What does a Customs man bring? A paper."

*Paper does not walk off, Joe.* I stood in a cloakroom with my mouth shut. The dry voice in the Frieze had said it, behind a louvre, in the rain.

"Who wrote it?"

"Block letters. Cheap pad. Could be a school child." He put the slip back in the hat and the hat back in the envelope. "That's not all. You told me about the jar."

"Ostrander's."

"I asked the lab Wednesday, after the pit. They had it Thursday and finished this morning. Chloral hydrate. Knockout drops, Ruth. Enough to put a man to sleep for a day, or to stop a bad heart that had four whiskies to carry." He did not look at me. "He was drugged, Ruth. Somebody put him to sleep, and he never woke up."

"It's a homicide."

"It's a homicide if Barnwell says it is. I put it in front of Rafferty at four o'clock. Rafferty said it'll keep till Monday." He laughed, once, without any air in it. "Retire first. Murder after."

"Frank."

"Hold on." He put a hand flat against a coat-rail. "I've got to ask you something and I'd rather ask it with my back turned. Have you still got it?"

I knew what he meant. We had never once said the word in eight years.

"Yes."

"Burn it."

It sat in the cloakroom between us like a third man. Beyond the wall the band began a waltz.

"No."

"He's out the door at midnight with a clock and a pension, and in two days he's a man in a rocking-chair. You could have used it any time in four years, Ruth."

"That's why I didn't."

"You could ruin the pair of us."

"I know what I could do. I've known it every day. I haven't done it." My voice was quiet and not steady. "It's a piece of paper, Frank."

"It's four years of both our lives."

There was nothing more to say, and we said it for a while. He turned his back to me and leaned his forehead on his forearm against the coat-rail, among the hats, with the tags of the rented jacket showing at his collar, and I stood and looked at the broad freckled hand that held the envelope. I wanted to put my own over it. I did not, and neither of us was ever going to say that I had wanted to.

"Come on," he said at last. "They'll have missed the beets."

* * *

At a quarter to one Swain's Packard pulled away from the hotel kerb, and I let it go as far as the corner.

The Packard turned north up Front Street. I knew it would; there was nowhere to go from the Harbor at that hour but the pier, and I'd rather lose a man in four blocks than follow him for a mile. I walked. Six minutes, by the Waltham, past the shuttered mouth of Anchor Alley and the dark Customs Annex, with the cold coming off the Sound and my breath showing, and not a whisper of fog. The Heifer was silent. It sounds on thick nights only, and this one had stars on it like salt.

I turned into Capstan Row at the Shape Hall, dark on its corner, and went down the Row for two blocks to where it ends at the pier gate. Beyond the last warehouse, in the lee of a wall, stood a stack of wooden cable reels each taller than a man. I got behind them. The pier ran out into the water in front of me, planked and empty, with a single bulb in a cage burning above the gate, and under it the Packard stood with its lights off and its engine ticking as it cooled.

Swain was by the door of the car with his collar up. His anchors caught the bulb whenever he moved a hand, and he moved it a good deal. Opposite him, with his hat brim snapped down and his hands in the pockets of a long dark coat, Aldo Ferrante stood still.

"You ask a great deal," Ferrante said. "Courtesy of the hour, Mr. Swain. I'm out of my bed."

"The Estate has asked me to deliver this." The envelope in Swain's hand was cream, and I would have bet my rent on the watermark. "It is a mark of its goodwill."

"How much?"

"Five hundred."

Ferrante did not take it. "Two men dead," he said, "and you bring me an envelope."

"The Estate does not wish further incidents."

"The Estate." He said it softly, as a man says the name of a bad dog he's been told is friendly. "Who is the Estate? Show me a face."

"A trust has no face, Mr. Ferrante." The envelope wobbled. Swain's voice had been rising since he began, and I heard the exact place where it ceased to be a lawyer's. "It's a, it's an arrangement. I only sign. Do you imagine I choose?"

"Then it's got no neck."

The word sat in the cold air by the pier. I felt it from behind my reels, and I'm fairly certain Swain felt it in his collar. He put the envelope in Ferrante's coat pocket himself, since Ferrante's hands stayed where they were, and backed to the car with the quick stiff steps of a man who has found the stairs dark. The Packard started on the second try. It turned the corner into Front Street and its taillamps went down toward the hotel like two coals.

Ferrante waited until the sound had gone. Then he turned, not quickly, and looked at the stack of reels, and did not look at the wall of the Shape Hall or the black water or the pier.

"Fish market," he said to the dark. "Four o'clock Sunday. Come alone, or don't come."

I didn't answer. I knew better than to speak to a man who had said *don't come.* He stood a moment more, and I heard a small sound I had last heard in an office with St. Joseph on the wall: the click of a pocketknife opening. It closed. It opened. It closed again, evenly, like a man counting something to himself.

I counted eleven. Then his boots went away on the planks, and the knife went on, a long way into the dark.
