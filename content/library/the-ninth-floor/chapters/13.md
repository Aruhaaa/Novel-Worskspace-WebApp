# What Teddy Left Out

Teddy Wozniak stood at the hot plate with his back to me, watching a pan of milk the way other men watch a horse they have money on. He had hung the maroon jacket on its chair, and in his shirtsleeves and waistcoat he looked narrower across the shoulders than he did behind a desk, like a boy sent to the tailor in his father's clothes. He hadn't asked what I would drink. He had put the pan on when he heard me on the stairs, all eighteen, the board that sang giving its note, and I had come in and sat at the oilcloth and let him.

"My sister's remedy," he said to the wall. "Hot milk. For grief, for fever, and for Russians."

"I've had the shock, Mr. Wozniak. I had it last night, in a cellar. I'd like to get to the rest."

"Yes." The milk climbed the side of the pan. He lifted it off the ring half a second before it went over, and the half second cost him, because I heard the pan chatter against the rim of the glass as he poured. Below us in the shop Mrs. Sobczak dropped a tray, and the floor hummed and settled. "Please. Drink. It gives me something to look at while I decide."

I drank. It had a skin on it. I took the skin out with the spoon, and he was kind enough not to see.

"Mr. Quill gave me tea this morning," I said. "Room 806, nine o'clock. He told me the Harbor has a ninth floor."

Teddy had been about to sit. He stopped with a hand on the chair back, and I watched the colour go out of his face from the forehead down, the way water leaves a basin: first the brow, then the cheeks, then the long upper lip, until the gold tooth was the only thing in it still carrying light. Then he sat.

"He told you."

"He called it the Mezzanine. A card room in the dry years, he said. He's kept the key to it for twenty years."

"He said he would never." It came out in a whisper, and he heard it, and tried again at speaking pitch. "Forgive me. In six years, if I may, Mr. Quill has not once said that word in my hearing. He said to me a single time, in the winter of 1947, that he had a great respect for the tank room above the Frieze, and did not wish to be told what he had no need to know. I took it as a promise. I believed it would hold to the end of his life."

"He also told me he moved Mr. Maher. Himself, with another man, after you telephoned. To save the licence and sixty jobs."

"That is what I supposed." His hands lay on the oilcloth and found no pencil, and settled for the corner of the blotter, which they squared. "When he told me that morning there was no ninth floor, I thought: he is closing the door on it. He is a gentleman, and he is protecting us, and he cannot say so. I have never been so grateful to a man. And now you say he speaks of it over tea."

"He asked me to find out who killed them. Maher and Joe."

"Of course he did." There was relief in it, and the relief was what worried me. "He loved Joe."

I looked at him loving Quill. You can't argue with that across an oilcloth. The pillbox in his waistcoat made its dry fall of seeds, and he laid a hand flat over it, the way one covers a cough in church.

"Mr. Wozniak. On Thursday you told me you would say how you got to nine when you knew you could."

"Yes."

"Do you know it now?"

He looked at the crucifix over the door. "A man is dead who wished to tell you something and was not given the time," he said. "I should prefer not to be the second. If I may, I will tell it in order, because it is the only way I am able. If I stop, please do not help me."

I turned the pink notice over and wrote nothing on it.

He told it the way he might have said a rosary, with his eyes on his hands. In December of 1946 his sister's husband, Stefan Kowal, sexton of St. Casimir's, had come up the bakery stairs at midnight with a man, a woman and two girls who had walked off a Greek freighter that afternoon without a paper among them and with nowhere in Port Calder to sleep that a priest's housekeeper wouldn't count. The photograph on the shelf looked down on us while he said it, the heavy man in the sexton's coat with a face like a locked door. "He had a temper like a kettle," Teddy said, following my eyes. "It boiled, and then he wept, and then he carried your coal. In June of last year it stopped his heart in the vestibule of the church, at sixty-one. He is the one who ought to be telling you."

Teddy had been fifteen years at the hotel by then, seven of them on nights. He knew a linen cupboard at the east end of the eighth floor with a padlock on it and no guest ever inside. He put the family in it for nine nights, with Pearl Hightower carrying soup up under the pillowcases in a laundry basket. On the ninth night, with one of the girls coughing, he did what he had not done in the fourteen years since he first found it out. He pushed on the back wall of the shelves, and it gave.

"A stair," I said.

"A stair, and at the top of it a floor." He lifted a hand and put it down. "That winter there were four. By spring there were nine more, and Stefan was carrying iron cots up the service stairs one at a time with a laundry tag tied to each, and Joe Lambert was taking the car up on his rounds with a basket of folded sheets that was heavier going than coming. Pearl fed them. Joe kept the car. I kept the desk, and the stairs, and said nothing to anyone for six years." The tin rattled. "Three hundred and eleven people, Miss Szymanski. All with names."

"You counted."

"I did not need to count. It is written down." He shut his mouth on the end of it, a second late, and I let the words go by like a streetcar I meant to catch at the next stop.

"Maher," I said.

"Mr. Maher was Stefan's friend at the Customs. A tally clerk who could not add, he used to say, whenever he wished to add one more name for us." Teddy turned his glass a quarter turn on the oilcloth. I had watched another man do that with a teacup that morning, and I put the thought down where I could find it. "He was kind to us for two years. Afterward he was something else. I will not say what, of a dead man, in my own kitchen, with his body not yet in the ground."

"And the key."

"There are three. Joe's, which you carry. Mine." Two fingers touched his watch pocket. "And the one Mr. Quill keeps in his safe, which to my knowledge has never been out of it. At the lobby panel, beside the call plate, there is a keyhole you would take for the electrician's. I turn it and the car comes down from wherever it is, like a dog called in. Inside the cab you lift the painted plug with a thumbnail. Mr. Quill's man painted it over that morning, but paint is only paint. You put in the key, press the eight, and hold it to a slow count of three. The car goes on past eight for six seconds more. And then it stays." He looked up. "It is deaf to the lobby, you understand. It stays on nine until somebody turns a key at the panel to fetch it home, and all that while the needle sits in the dark. That is what I saw."

"Joe told you it was sulking."

"Joe said the car was sulking and the guests should use the service lift. In twenty years Joe did not lie to me, and that night he looked at his shoes and lied, and I let him. Somebody had gone up with a key. I thought it was he. I thought he had his reasons."

I let that sit between us a while. Everyone who knew the way up to that floor was in this room, or in the ground, or in a building I could see from the window.

"Then tell me why you lied to me," I said. "On Thursday, at this table, you had never seen him before Friday."

"Because of a thing my sister keeps."

"A book."

His eyes came up. They were pale brown, wet at the rims, and for the first time since I'd known him there was no apology anywhere in them.

"Black," I said. "A brass clasp. She held it against her chest at the Gospel as if somebody might take it off her at the elevation."

"You see hands."

"Hands and shoes. The face comes last."

"Then you will understand me when I say you must not ask what is in it." He drew a breath through his nose. "If you read it, Miss Szymanski, you will burn it. Not today. But you will."

"I don't burn things, Mr. Wozniak." It was out before I'd looked at it, and I thought of a typewriter case on a filing cabinet, behind a door with a new Yale lock.

"No," he said, studying me with something that was not quite hope. "No, I did not think you did."

I tried the next door. "A singer called Marchand stood across Front Street from your hotel for ten minutes just after midnight on election night. White gloves. Then she crossed over."

His hand left the blotter. "Who saw her?"

"A counterman at Tommy's. I saw her myself on Saturday, in a dressing room, with a snapshot of a low ceiling and some coat hooks stuck in the mirror."

He closed his eyes. "Ten past twelve. She asked for Mr. Delaney by name. I told her he had retired, which a guest does at midnight, and she said then she would leave him a note, and I looked down at the register so as not to see which way she went. The Councilman's people were coming out of the Pacific Room by then, forty at once, wanting their coats and their bills. When I looked up she had gone. I did not see the lift. I did not see her come down." He opened them. "I told the Sergeant no one had asked for Mr. Delaney. It was the second lie I have told a policeman in my life, and I told it as though I had been practising."

"She was one of yours."

He said nothing. In a dispatcher's ear that is an answer. A line that stays open with nobody on it is still a line.

"Say again where everyone was," I said. "Midnight to four. In order."

He gave it to me the way he gave a guest his keys, one at a time, laid on the counter. Joe in the boiler room, he supposed. Pearl at home by then. The party emptying until past one, the Pacific Room staff carrying out chairs. "And Mr. Quill retired at ten minutes to twelve. I saw him cross the lobby from the party. I did not see him again until I rang his room at ten past four."

"You rang him. He was in his room."

"If you are thinking what you are thinking, you may spare yourself." It was the first sharp thing I had heard him say, and it turned his ears pink. "At five minutes past two the operator rang the desk. Santa Barbara for 806. I put the plug in myself and wrote it in the toll book, the time and the room and Miss Calder's name. He telephones the elder niece when he cannot sleep. She is seventy-eight and deaf, and he is obliged to shout. I saw the lamp for 806 burning on the board at a quarter to three, when I looked up at the dial, and dark at half past three when I looked again. I thought: poor man. He has no one else to talk to at that hour." He nodded at the door. "The book is on the desk. You may see my handwriting."

I wrote *2:05, Santa Barbara, 806* on the back of the notice, and under it, because I'd have put down his weight if he had offered it, *lamp 2:45, dark 3:30.* He watched me do it with the look of a man who has gone to the dentist and been told it was nothing.

By the Waltham it was twenty to eleven when I stood up. The hotel had given him the evening for Joe, he said at the door. A relief clerk named Okonski had the desk until midnight, and Teddy had spent an hour of the afternoon at Mt. Zion with the deacons, a white man in a maroon jacket among forty deacons, who had given him coffee and not asked why he had come. I hadn't asked either. He told me anyway, as a man pays a debt in small coins.

I went out onto the landing and started down. He came after me as far as the top of the flight and put his hand on the banister, a rail polished by the palms of everyone who had ever hurried on those stairs.

"You took Joe's key," he said. It wasn't a question.

I stopped on the fifth stair. "Pearl told you."

"Pearl told me the shoelace was not on him when they carried him up. And you put your hand to that pocket whenever someone says nine. You are not a woman who touches her pockets." His own hand went to the watch pocket and came away. "He wore it twenty years. He lent it to no one, not to me. It is yours to carry now and I do not ask for it. If you must go up, then go. I would rather you did not see it empty."

"Empty."

"They are all gone since October. The last family left on the seventeenth. There is only the furniture, and what they wrote on the walls."

I went down three more. The board that sang sang under my shoe, and he winced for it, as though a plank were a person he had to answer for.

"Miss Szymanski."

I looked up. He stood at the head of the flight in the light of the bare bulb, small and stooped, the white eyebrows pinned over his eyes like a pair of moth wings.

"Don't go up," he said.

I waited. Below us a tray went down on the bakery floor, and the whole stair hummed with it.

"If you go," said Teddy Wozniak, quite quietly, "count the steps."
