# Pod Twoją Obronę

My mother's reading glasses hung on their string against her chest, and she had pulled the kitchen lamp down on its cord until it hung a foot above the oilcloth like a surgeon's, so that the table lay in a bright round pool and everything else in the room had gone to brown. On the table, in the middle of the light, with its brass clasp the colour of weak tea, lay the book.

"Sit," said Helena. "Not there. There. You will hold the lamp straight and you will not touch."

"I'm not a child."

"You are a woman who reads with her head. Sit."

I sat. It was eight o'clock on Sunday night, and the rain had come back about four in the afternoon and settled in against the window over the sink as though it had a lease. My mother wiped her hands on her apron, one and then the other, and put on the glasses, and set a thumbnail against the clasp. It opened with a small, dry, definite click. I had heard that sound once already that day, in a coat pocket, against a key.

The front leaves were print: the prayer from which it took its name. *Pod Twoją obronę uciekamy się.* "Under your protection we flee," said my mother, "Holy Mother of God. Do not despise our petitions in our necessities." Her finger went along the line. "Every Pole knows it. A man says it on a ship."

She turned past the Litany and the Stations to where the print ended, and the paper went blank and ruled, and the writing began.

It was in pencil, small and upright, in the hand of a man who had gone to school until he was twelve and had made up for it since. The columns were ruled by a thumbnail and the edge of a hymn card. Names; a place; a ship; a date. "Dates by the saints," said my mother. "Of course. A Pole knows the Feast of Saint Lucy before he knows Tuesday." She read, and put it into English a line behind, at the speed of a woman buttering bread.

*Nowak. Józef, Maria, Zosia, Ania. A Greek ship, the Aegina. On Saint Lucy, 'forty-six. Nine days upstairs. Offering, nothing.* Under it, in a smaller pencil, as if added after: *Pan Bóg zapłać.* "God will repay."

"He wrote that for himself," I said.

"He wrote it for a Pole in the next room who had no money." She turned the leaf.

I counted. It's what I do when other people read aloud. There were twenty-eight lines to a page, and each line held a name, a ship, a saint's day, a number under *dni na górze,* days upstairs. "Where from?" I asked, over a line that said *z Przemyśla,* and I tried it. "Premissle."

"Przemyśl." My mother did not look up. "Pshe-mishl. Like a man sneezing politely in church. Say."

"Pshemishl."

"The Americans have ruined you." She turned another leaf. *Dąbrowska. A widow and a boy of six. Saint Joseph's day.* Her voice dropped a note and went on at a steady pace, because she was reading, and it is hard to cry through a list. Some lines had a word in the margin I couldn't catch. *Chora.* Sick. *Chrzest.* A baptism. And once, in letters that had been pressed hard enough to dent the page: *urodzona na górze.* Born upstairs.

"Now," she said, and her voice changed. "Here is spring."

The columns had altered. In the ruling of the page for March of 1948 Stefan had drawn two new lines in a harder pencil, and headed them, in capitals so small they might have been written with a pin: OFIARA. DO DOMU. My mother put her finger beneath each. "The offering. And to the House."

"Say that again."

"*Do Domu.* To the House." She lifted a shoulder. "It is not a word that means anything. It is a house. The man has written, to the House, a hundred and fifty. And beside it, in the other column, four hundred." She ran her finger down. "Four hundred. Four hundred, one hundred and fifty. Four hundred, one hundred and fifty. Rutka, from this page they pay." She turned over, and I saw that the page opposite was written in a single strong line across two columns, the pencil gone black from being gone over twice. She read it silently. Then she took off her glasses, and pressed the bridge of her nose between a finger and a thumb, and put them on again, and began it from the start, like a woman who has dropped a stitch.

"*Okręt Esther Vane. Na świętej Matyldy. Trzydzieści jeden dusz.*"

"Thirty-one," I said.

"Thirty-one souls." She said *dusz* with her whole mouth. "The ship *Esther Vane,* on the feast of Saint Matilda, the fourteenth of March. A soul, Rutka. They count cattle by the head. He is a sexton, and he writes souls." She turned the next leaf and the next, and I saw the names run down the page in his pencil, thirty-one lines, and a fee against every one. "Four hundred each. A hundred and fifty to the House."

I did the sum on the notice. Thirty-one at four hundred was twelve thousand four hundred dollars. Thirty-one at a hundred and fifty was four thousand six hundred and fifty, which is what a night clerk at thirty-eight dollars a week earns in two years and four months. It went to a house, in a night. I put the pencil down. At the foot of that page, under the thirty-first line, there were three words in the same upright hand, and they were not a name. My mother crossed herself before she read them.

"*Panie, przebacz mi,*" she said. "Lord, forgive me."

We were quiet for a time. The rain tapped at the pipe. I remembered what a man had said over a steel table that morning, wiping his hands on a cloth: *I never thought of it as selling.* I was looking at the arithmetic of a sexton who had.

She read on through 'forty-nine more quickly, a name to a breath. In February her thumb stopped on a line I could see, upside down, across the table: *u Sz., 7 dni, 4 dusze.* She did not read it aloud, and she did not look at me. I looked at the line and then at the lamp, as one looks at a stranger's face on a streetcar, and said nothing, and in a moment the page turned. It was the second time that week I had watched my mother not answer, and I had been taught better than to put the question.

The last leaves were another hand. I knew it at once. It was small and square, with a line under every figure and the sevens crossed, and I had seen it on a desk blotter at the Harbor Hotel and on a card tacked to a door in Hake Street. *Łukaszewicz, five persons. Third October. Left the seventeenth.* There was nothing under it but a line ruled to the foot of the page and the smooth white of the leaf.

"Teddy," said my mother.

"He wrote them out."

"He wrote them out to the end." She closed the clasp and put her hand flat on the book, as you do on a loaf you're not sure is done. "Three hundred and eleven."

I looked at the leaves, the eleven pages of twenty-eight lines and a short twelfth, and I didn't count them. I knew what the sum would be.

* * *

At ten there was a knock. It was soft, three and then one, the knuckle of a very light hand, and I'd taken two steps toward the door when my mother, without lifting her eyes from the book, said:

"That is Stefan's knock."

I stopped. "Whose?"

"Three and one. The parish knew it. Open the door, Rutka. Do not use the chain."

The woman on the landing had rain running off her hat and one white glove. The other hand, bare, held a cardboard suitcase tied with string, and the nails on it were bitten to the quick. She looked past me down the hall into a bright kitchen where an old woman sat under a lamp with a book, and the grey-green eyes went wide, and I saw a thin crescent scar on her chin go white.

"Mon cher," said Yvette Marchand, in a voice with no stage in it at all. "A sergeant came to the club at eight. A big one, with a matchstick. He asked where I was on Tuesday night. The Deacon said, on stage. The sergeant said the club's book had me leaving before the half hour, and the Deacon said he does not keep a book on a woman's cigarette." She drew a breath. "Then he looked at me, and said *Mm-hm,* and gave me a street."

"Come in," I said.

She came as far as the kitchen door and stopped. The book lay on the table in the pool of light, with its clasp shut, and I watched her see it. She didn't scream and she didn't speak. She sat down hard on the nearest chair with the suitcase in her lap, and the one glove went to her mouth.

My mother took off her glasses. She said something in Polish, very low, a long sentence with a shape like a question and the sound of a hand on a head, and from behind the white glove, in a voice that came from a good deal farther back than Lublin, Yvette answered. Not in French, and not in the careful English of a club on Brine Street. The consonants of her mother tongue came out hard and wet, and she was a girl of twenty-eight on a landing with a hook-rail, and she was crying. My mother stood up. She said another sentence. Then the two of them were holding on to each other over the book, a short wide woman in an apron and a tall one in a wet coat, and neither one made any sound but the sound of the breath going in, and I stood in my own kitchen on the wrong side of a glass door, with the only Polish I had, which was *how are you.*

I put the kettle on. It seemed to be what the room required.

It took a long while. When they let go of each other, my mother wiped her face on her apron without embarrassment and opened the clasp, and she turned to the year 1949 as one turns to a street one lived on. "Here," she said. "Find yourself, child. You will know your own page."

It took Yvette less than a minute. She laid her bare finger on a line and it shook; my mother read it out, slowly, so that I could have it in English.

"*Wolska, Irena. Parowiec Pacific Tern. Saint Ursula, 1949.* That is the twenty-first of October. *Nineteen days upstairs. Offering four hundred. To the House, a hundred and fifty.*"

"I paid with my mother's rings," said Yvette. Her voice was level now, and I found it harder to listen to than the other. "She had three. I gave him the three, in Antwerp, a man with a hat. I had a laundress's papers from Lyon. I was a Madame Marchand, who washed sheets." She looked at the pencil. "I am in a book. I am written down in the name I was given, and in pencil, and someone has put a line under it."

"In your own name," said my mother.

"In the one I will not say." She shut her eyes. "Forty dollars a month, Miss Szymanski. That is what he asked. From the first of July, 'fifty-one, in an envelope left at Mrs. Abbott's for Mr. Declan Maher. Sixteen times. Six hundred and forty dollars." The scar stood out. "I sing French songs in a basement for scale. I paid it. I told myself it was rent."

"And the fourth of November?"

"I had three hundred dollars in my stocking. Not for him. For my page. I thought, he has a list, he is a man who keeps lists, and I will buy it out of the world, and he will tear it, and I will be a woman with no past at all." "I left the stage at the eleven o'clock and I stood across the street from the hotel for ten minutes, I think. I could see the lobby. Mr. Wozniak was at the desk. I asked for Mr. Delaney. He told me Mr. Delaney had retired, and he looked down at his register, so as not to see me."

"Where did you go?"

"Up. By the stair. I did not want the lift, with its brass, and the little needle. I went to the eighth floor and I found 807, and I knocked, as one knocks on a door one hopes will not be answered." The voice thinned. "And no one did. And while I stood there in the corridor with my hand on the knob, I saw a man go away from me down to the end, toward the window. From behind. In a dressing gown."

I felt my own hands go flat on the oilcloth. "What colour?"

"Dark. The light in that corridor is brown. It shone a little at the shoulders. Quilted, I think. He was tall, mon cher." She looked at me. "Taller than Mr. Wozniak by a head and a half. White hair, or the light. He carried a tray, with glasses on it, two, and something tall that caught the light. I heard them, a little chime, like water."

"How old?"

"Old. A neck like a bird's. But he was walking faster than an old man ought." Her gloved hand turned over in her lap. "He went to the last door on the right and he did not try the handle. He had the key out already. He put it in, and went in, and shut it, and the light under it did not come on."

"Did you know him?"

"I never saw his face."

I thought it over, the way I'd have thought over a call that came in on a bad line. Teddy, who was a head shorter than that man by any measure. Joe, shorter yet, and dead. Pearl, who was tall, and a woman, and would not have chimed a glass in her own kitchen. I said it under my breath, as I do, with the fingers of my left hand tapping the oilcloth: Teddy. Pearl. Joe. Quill. Four people with a key to the door at the end of the corridor, and three of them could be crossed off by a tape measure.

I did not say the fourth. I thought of a man in a window drinking tea, sixty-three years old and clean to the welt, who had folded his hands on the head of a cane and asked me to find whoever killed his porter. I thought of a telephone call to Santa Barbara at five past two, written in a night clerk's hand in a book, with a lamp burning on a board. A dressing gown at ten past twelve is only a dressing gown, and the man in it might have carried a nightcap up a stair and gone down again to his telephone. I had a back and a tray and a shine at the shoulder, and the word of a woman who had never in her life been believed by a policeman.

"Who have you told?" I said.

"Nobody. They do not ask a woman like me what she saw. They ask what she is." She looked at the white cotton glove in her lap. "I tell you because Madame cried."

"I'm not a policeman."

"No," said Yvette. "You are something that keeps a thing."

My mother put Yvette to bed in mine, with a hot-water bottle and a nightgown of her own that came to Yvette's shins. Then she took the bread knife from the drawer, wiped the blade on her apron, and carried it into her own room and put it under her pillow.

"Mama."

"Tonight I am a woman with a knife. In the morning I am a woman with bread. Sleep, Rutka."

I didn't sleep. At a quarter to two the house was quiet, the rain had thinned to a drip from the gutter, and I sat in the kitchen with the lamp pushed up out of the way and the book in front of me, and read it as best I could with the words I had. A hundred little pencil lines. I found the leaf of the *Esther Vane* and the thirty-one lines and the four words of the Lord's forgiveness, and I turned the leaf back to the one before it, the first one with the new columns, and held it to the light.

There was a note in the margin, so faint that the lamp had missed it. Pencil, very small, at an angle, written by a man who didn't mean to be read. Three words and a letter, and I could read them without my mother, because every Polish child learns one of them before she can tie a shoe and the other is the same in any language.

*Dom = Hotel. T. mówi.*

The House is the Hotel. Teddy says.

I sat with the sentence for a good while. A hundred and fifty dollars a head had gone to the House, and the House was the building on Front Street with a Frieze and a night clerk and a fern. Plum's wife's cousins, a man with a fish knife had told me. A trust has no face, a lawyer had said, with a catch in his voice. I held the book against my chest, as a woman had held it at the Gospel, and decided that in the morning I would telephone Lucille Fong at the Harbor Commission and ask whose name stood on the paper for the pier.

A board creaked behind me. My mother stood in the door of her room in her nightgown, with her hair down her back in a grey plait as thick as a mooring line, and the glasses on their string, though she had no business with them at that hour. She had not been asleep. She looked at the book under my hands and then at me.

"A name is a small thing to carry," said Helena Szymanska. "Carry it."
