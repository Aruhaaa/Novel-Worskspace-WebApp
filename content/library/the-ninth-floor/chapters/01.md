# The Blank on the Dial

The radio on the cigar counter in the Selkirk lobby said General Eisenhower had carried thirty-nine states and Governor Stevenson had conceded in the night. The man behind the counter was sorting pipe cleaners by length and gave the news the attention you give a stranger's cough. A landslide, the announcer said, and then said it again, as if the word might not hold the first time.

The elevator wore a card tied to its gate with string. I took the stairs, fifty-two of them counting the half-landings, and I do count the half-landings. On the third floor somebody had tacked a pink paper to the frosted glass of 309, square across the gilt of my name, so that the door now read PRIVATE INQUIRIES and, above that, PAY OR QUIT. The Selkirk's bad news used to come on white. I decided the pink was a courtesy.

Eighty-four dollars, it said, two months at forty-two. I had eleven dollars and forty cents, which I knew to the penny, having counted it on the kitchen table at six and again on the Number 14 streetcar in case it had changed.

There was a man on the bench across the hall, and he had been there long enough to leave a small lake under it. He was small himself, and stooped, past fifty, with white eyebrows that stood off his forehead like the wings of a moth. Under an open raincoat he wore the maroon jacket of a hotel, a brass nameplate on the breast: T. WOZNIAK. He held his hat by the brim in both hands and turned it a quarter inch at a time, and every time it drifted off true he turned it back. I look at hands first, and shoes. His hands were small and tidy, the nails cut square. His shoes were black, thick in the sole, made for standing, and wet to the laces.

"Miss Szymanski?" He rose. I looked down at the parting in his hair, which had been combed with water and then rained on.

"That's the name under the pink," I said.

"Forgive me. I was told that you open at eight."

"I open when I get here." I peeled the notice off the glass with my thumbnail, folded it in four and put it in my coat pocket. "Come in out of the wet, Mr. Wozniak."

Room 309 holds a desk, two chairs, a filing cabinet with a typewriter case on top of it, and a window onto Cutter Street with rain running down the glass and a dark stain on the sill where some of it got in. The radiator knocked three times and stopped. I hung up my hat, lit the first of the six Luckies I allow myself in a day, which was early for it, and sat. He would not sit until I had. Then he perched on the front of the client's chair with the hat on his knees.

"If I may," he said, "I will tell it in order. It is the only way I am sure of it. But I must say the worst first, or you will think I am coming to it slowly on purpose."

"Say the worst."

"Last night, Miss Szymanski, I found a dead man on the ninth floor of the Harbor Hotel."

"Say again."

"On the ninth floor."

"The Harbor's got eight. I've walked under it all my life."

"Yes." A gold tooth showed, top left, in an apology. "That is what Mr. Quill says also."

I took the pink notice out of my pocket and turned it over. It was the only paper in the office I owed money on, and the back of it was clean. "Smaller pieces," I said. "Who are you, and who is Mr. Quill?"

"I am the night clerk. Eleven to seven, thirteen years at the desk and twenty-one in the building. I began as a bellhop. Mr. Quill is the resident auditor. He keeps a room on the eighth floor and speaks for the Estate that owns us. A gentleman." The hat went another quarter turn. "Last night was the Councilman's party for the election, in the Pacific Room. A great many men who were very happy. At ten minutes to one, the guest car stopped answering the buttons."

"Stopped."

"People pressed, and the lamps lit, and nothing came. I telephoned the basement and spoke to Lambert, the porter. He said the car was sulking and would come down when she was ready."

"She."

"Lambert has run her twenty-two years. He says she has moods. The few guests still about took the service lift, which is slow and smells of linen. Most were not in a condition to mind." He drew a breath through his nose. "At ten minutes past two I looked across the lobby at the dial above the lift doors. One can see it from the desk." He held up two fingers an inch apart. "A half circle of brass, and a needle, and the numerals one to eight. Beyond the eight there is a stop with no numeral. The needle was sitting on it."

"Past the eight."

"On the blank."

"What did the lobby clock say?"

"The lobby clock runs three minutes slow. I allow for it. Ten past two." He saw me look at him. "Everyone in the building allows for it. It is the one clock we trust, because it is wrong by a known amount."

I wrote that on the back of the notice, with the times. "And at ten past four?"

"The same. Two hours exactly. I am a man who counts the keys at night, Miss Szymanski. I am not a man who dreams." He did not smile at himself. "At ten past four I turned my key at the lobby panel and the car came down, and I rode up."

"How many buttons in the car?"

"Eight. L, and two through eight."

"Then how did you get to nine?"

His hand went toward his waistcoat and stopped. Something small and made of tin rattled in the pocket, a dry sound, seeds in a pod, and the hand came away empty. "There is a key."

"Whose?"

"Forgive me."

"What does it turn?"

"Forgive me," he said again, gently, as if I had asked to borrow something he had already promised away.

In the wire room I learned that a caller who won't give you the address will give you everything round it if you leave the line open. I left it open. "Go on. The doors open."

"On a corridor, very low. Then a long room at the end of it, with a ceiling a tall man could touch with the flat of his hand. I carried the flashlight from the desk drawer. There was a man on a cot against the wall, on his back, in his stocking feet. I looked for his shoes. I do not know why. His left sock had a hole in the heel, and the heel was very white." The hat made a half turn. "A man's heel is a private thing. I looked at it longer than I looked at his face."

"Dead."

"I am not a doctor. I touched his wrist. Beside the cot was a card table, and on the table two glasses. Tumblers. Empty."

"Two."

"Two."

I wrote 2, and went over it until the pen nearly went through the paper. "Then what?"

"I went down and telephoned Mr. Quill, as one does for a death in the house. He said to wait at the desk, and I waited. A little before six the police came, and they did not go to nine. They went to 807, where a guest named Delaney, of Portland, dried fruit, had been registered since Friday, and he was there on the bed with his shoes on and the spread across his legs. A doctor's man said it was the heart."

"The same man."

"I checked him in myself. I would know him anywhere." He studied the brim of the hat. "Afterwards Mr. Quill took me into the office behind the desk and gave me tea. Very kindly, and I believe he meant it kindly, he said the party had run until two, that there had been a good deal of noise and a good deal of rye, and that a man who has been on his feet for thirty hours sees things." The gold tooth again, and again not a smile. "I have been on my feet longer without once seeing a dead man."

The radiator knocked. I let my cigarette burn down an inch. "There are a dozen men on Cutter Street who'd take a job like this one, Mr. Wozniak. Most of them own better hats than mine. Why did you climb my stairs?"

From inside the raincoat he took a folded square of green blotting paper, opened it on my desk and squared it to the edge. His hands did that without consulting him. "This was the top sheet of the blotter at the desk. Mr. Delaney wrote on it on Monday night, while I was making up the keys. In pencil."

I turned it round. A night blotter keeps a week of other people's signatures, backwards and doubled, but the pencil stood clear of them in a clerk's neat hand with the sevens crossed. Ruth Szymanski. Selkirk Bldg., Cutter & Second. Rm. 309.

"He said that you used to be on the police wire," Wozniak said. "He said that you were honest."

I looked at the typewriter case on the filing cabinet, which is what I do when anyone uses that word. "I never heard of a Delaney."

"He was a very quiet guest."

"Dried fruit," I said.

"So he said."

My fee is fifteen dollars a day and expenses, and I told him so. He took a flat leather purse from his jacket, counted out a ten and two fives and squared them to the blotter. It was more than half of what a night clerk makes in a week, and I took it, which tells you what was in my pocket.

"Go home and sleep," I said. "I'll come to the Harbor."

"I cannot sleep. I will go back to the hotel. I have to stay near the cab."

"It's a box on a rope."

"Yes," he said. "But I would like to be near it, if I may." He put on the hat. On the stairs he took the steps one at a time, like a man who has been warned about a loose one, and the tin in his pocket rattled all the way down.

* * *

The telephone in 309 is on a party line with a dentist and a woman who sells corsets by appointment, and both of them listen. I walked along Cutter to the drugstore and shut myself in the booth, which had a folding door that would not fold and a seat that would if you sat on it. I gave the operator Central Station, Detective Bureau, and heard the clicks go through, a sound I had spent nine years at the other end of.

"Dolan."

"It's Ruth."

"I know who it is. You breathe like a woman counting." A wooden matchstick moved from one side of his mouth to the other. I could hear it against his teeth.

"Somebody died at the Harbor Hotel last night."

"Natural," Dolan said. "Heart. Room 807, where else would a man die in a hotel, Ruth?" He said it like a man repeating something he had already said once that morning.

"Who was he?"

"A guest. Out of town. I got the call at twenty past five and haven't sat down since, and Ostrander's man looked at him and signed. Heart, and a skinful. What's your interest?"

"Habit."

"Then break it. Go and find somebody's husband, Ruthie. They've all been up to something since the election."

The line went to a dial tone. I stood holding the receiver. Frank Dolan had been the voice in my headset for eight years before I ever saw his face. He had signed my application for a licence in 1949, and we had not talked about why. I had thirty-one dollars and forty cents in my pocket, a night clerk's twenty of it, and I had not told him about the needle, or the stocking feet, or the two glasses. It was a small thing to hold back. It came so easily that I noticed it.

* * *

From Cutter to Front the street runs downhill, and the rain came down it with me. A Number 14 swayed past toward the Flats with its windows steamed white. The air smelled of tar, diesel and the fish market, which on a wet day you can taste before you reach it.

The Harbor Hotel stands at the corner of Front and Ferry Slip Row, brick below and buff terracotta above, the colour of a boiled crab. I crossed to the water side, where the kerb is low and a bollard gives a woman something to stand beside, and I counted the way I used to count calls on the board, with a finger, under my breath. The lobby's tall windows, one. Two. Three. Rain went down my collar. Four, five, six, seven. Eight. Eight rows, curtains drawn on most, and above the eighth the cornice that the hotel's postcards call the Frieze: waves in terracotta with fish swimming between them, and over the waves a band of vents, louvred, slatted, painted black, like the gills of something very large. By eye I put it at fourteen feet. A storey is ten, and this was a storey and a bit. I had walked under it three thousand times. Nobody looks at the part of a building that is only there to finish it.

"Eight," I said aloud. Mr. Quill said so, and the city directory, and everyone in Port Calder who had ever looked up at the Harbor in the rain.

I started over, with my finger at the lobby windows, and this time when I reached the top row I did not stop. I went up through the waves and the fish and the black slats, and the number I got was nine.

Out on Calder Point the Heifer gave its first moan of the day, two notes, the second lower than the first. Somebody out there had decided the rain was thick enough to count as fog. I stood on the kerb with the water running off my hat brim and counted forty to see whether it would come again. It did.
