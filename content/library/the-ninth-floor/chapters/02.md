# Paint on the Brass

A man in a grey porter's coat was vacuuming confetti off the lobby floor of the Harbor Hotel, and he was losing. The confetti came in red, white and blue discs the size of dimes, and for every one that went up the hose another showed itself in the grout between the black marble squares and the white. Over his head a banner hung across the doors of the Pacific Room. PLUM FOR THE PEOPLE, it said, in red felt, except that the last E had lost its pin and swung by one corner, which left the people to PEOPL. Beneath it an arch of balloons had gone soft at the shoulders and shrunk to prunes, which I took for editorial comment.

The lobby clock said twenty-seven minutes past ten. My father's Waltham, which I wear with the face on the inside of my wrist, said half past, and I believed the Waltham. The clock has run three minutes slow since before the war, and the staff, as far as I could tell, kept time by it and arrived at everything three minutes late with perfect confidence.

The place smelled of floor wax and wet wool and a night's worth of cigars, and under that, from the direction of the lift, of something sharp and sweet that I put down to the hotel putting its face on after the party. The lift stood across from the desk with its doors shut. Beside the call button was a brass plate the size of a silver dollar, bored through for a keyhole, and over the hole one word, SERVICE, in gilt worn down to a rumour. I looked at it longer than a plate deserves. A man had told me that morning that he turned a key at the lobby panel, and here was the panel.

"Mind the hose, miss." The porter had switched off his machine to let me by. "They threw it by the bucket. Eisenhower's welcome to the country, but I've got the lobby."

Behind the brass rail the day clerk worked a wad of spearmint with the whole lower half of her face. She was twenty or so, in a maroon jacket cut for a bigger girl, with nails painted fire-engine red and bitten to the quick underneath. The nameplate said D. NASH. I laid a card on the desk and she read it upside down, and then the right way up.

"I'm a friend of Mr. Wozniak's," I said. "I'd like a word with him, or with whoever he answers to."

"Mr. Wozniak's off. He goes off at seven." The gum went round once. "He's a lovely man for a shift."

"Was he himself when you came on?"

"He's a lovely man for a shift."

"Say again," I said.

"A lovely man for a shift." Behind me the vacuum started up and swallowed my next two questions whole, and she answered them anyway, in the same words, a note higher. I admired it. I spent nine years at the end of a telephone listening to callers try tears, rage, the wrong address and a dead mother, and nobody had ever refused me as thoroughly as a girl with spearmint.

"Mr. Quill says anybody who asks about last night goes to Mr. Quill," she said at last, all in a rush, and lifted the house telephone. "It isn't about you. It's anybody."

"Who's Mr. Quill?"

"The auditor. He lives here." She said it the way you'd mention that the building had a roof. Into the receiver she said that there was a lady about last night. She listened and hung up. "He says come through, and mind the step."

* * *

The office behind the desk had been a cloakroom once and still had the hooks, which now held a brown umbrella and a calendar from a marine insurer. There was a roll-top desk, a green-shaded lamp, a gas fire, and a wall of ledgers in black cloth with their years painted down the spines in white. Over the fire hung an architect's elevation of the hotel in pen and wash: eight rows of windows under a deep cornice, and a pennant on the roof that nobody had flown since. A tea tray stood on the blotter. The cups were thin enough to read a newspaper through.

The man behind the desk rose when I came in, which men don't, and I noticed that before I noticed him. He was tall and narrow, with silver hair parted so exactly down the middle that the two halves of his head might have been issued separately. Charcoal suit, a collar high enough to disagree with the decade, pale blue eyes, and long hands the yellow-white of old piano keys. With the right one he drew an ebony stick off the edge of the desk, a silver collar at its head, and hooked it over his left forearm the way a waiter carries a napkin.

"Miss Szymanski. Ambrose Quill. Please." He cleared his throat, twice, a dry little double click like a man knocking at his own door. I wrote it down in the place where I keep such things. "You will take tea. It is the one thing in the building at this hour that I can vouch for."

It tasted of tarred rope, and I liked it, which I wrote down as well.

"I take it Mr. Wozniak sent you," Quill said, pouring. The tea made no sound at all. "No, don't trouble to deny it. He is not a man who can leave a building without telling somebody where he is going, and he left this one at a quarter to eight with a wet hat and the face of a man carrying a full cup."

"He asked me to listen to something he saw."

"Yes." Click, click. "I should like to say a word about Teddy before we come to what he saw. He has been in this house twenty-one years. I would trust him with my watch, my cheque book and my sister, had I a sister. I want that said first, because the rest will sound unkind, and is not."

"Say the rest."

"He has been on his feet thirty hours, my dear. A Councilman's victory is a labour in itself, and a man at the end of one sees things. At thirty hours I once saw my mother standing in the Pacific Room, and she has lain in a Dorset churchyard since 1923." He turned a cup toward me by its saucer. "Teddy believes he rode up to a ninth floor. There isn't one. The Harbor has eight, as the plans, the licence and the Board of Underwriters will tell you. I would offer you the plans, but they are dull. The elevation is over the fire, and you are welcome to count."

I counted. It's what I do. Eight rows of windows, and above them the cornice, drawn deep in terracotta wash with fish swimming in it; the architect had been proud of his fish. The cornice took up a fifth of the paper. I said nothing about that.

"He didn't tell me he thought he saw a floor," I said. "He said he rode up and found a man on a cot."

"Did he." Click, click. "Then he has told you in more detail than he told me. At ten past four he telephoned my room in some confusion, and I told him, quite firmly, I think, to wait for me at the desk. A little after five Lambert took the early papers up and found the unfortunate gentleman in 807, and sent for me, and I for the police. There was nothing in it that would trouble a coroner, and nothing did."

"Who was he?"

"A Mr. Delaney, of Portland. Dried fruit. He took the room on Friday." Quill spread his long hands. "I should be lying if I said I knew him. A guest is a hat and a coat to me, my dear. Teddy sees faces. I see the column the cheque goes in."

"And Tuesday night? Where were you?"

"Looking in on the Councilman's party for an hour, and retiring a little before midnight. Victory is a young man's exercise."

"Retiring where?"

"Eight, at the quiet end. I have kept a room in this house since 1938, which makes me its longest-serving guest and its smallest expense."

"I'd like to see the car," I said. "And the room."

"My dear Miss Szymanski, you may see whatever you like. Nothing in this building is locked to anyone who asks. It is the whole art of hospitality." He took a buff card from a drawer, and from a squat glass well of ink so dark it looked like a hole in the blotter he filled a steel pen and wrote. The line went on green-black. By the time he had blotted it the strokes were browning at the edges, and as I watched they went brown all through, the brown of old blood on a bandage. VISITOR, the card said. Miss R. Szymanski. Wed. 5 Nov. And under that, A. Quill, in a hand that ran uphill.

"Your ink changes its mind," I said.

"It has never been decisive." He blew across the card and gave it to me by its edge. "Lambert will take you up. He is the one man alive whom the car trusts."

Dorrie Nash's head came round the door to say that Lambert was at the lift. Quill's stick came off his arm and tapped the carpet twice, briskly, like a man putting on a hat for a photograph; when her head went away, the stick was back across his forearm as if it had never left. I had seen what I had seen. The tip in the second it touched the floor was as clean as a new pencil eraser. I noticed it the way you notice a stranger's good shoes, and put it in the drawer where I keep things that have no use yet.

* * *

Lambert waited at the lift with his cap against his chest, a short man, bowed at the shoulders, in porter's grey. I looked at his hands. They were scarred white across the knuckles by old cab rope, with grease worked into the creases where soap had given up years ago. His shoes were big and black and cracked at the toe, and blacked over the cracks. A black shoelace went round the back of his neck and down inside his collar. Some men wear a saint there. I didn't ask.

"Eight, Miss," he said, not as a question, and pressed the call button. The doors slid back.

The cab was oak veneer and brass, polished by someone who did it every morning at the same hour. The eight buttons ran in a row across a cream-painted panel beside the door, L, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, each stamped in its brass ring. Over the door a brass arc repeated the numerals along a curve, with a needle hung beneath it on a pivot. Past the 8 the arc ran on another inch and a half to a tick with no number at all. Nothing on the panel led to that tick. The sharp sweet smell was stronger in here, and I filed that too.

Lambert pressed the 8. The doors closed and he began to hum through his nose, slowly, "Abide with Me," with the patience of a man keeping a machine company. The floor pressed up through my shoes. One, I counted. Two.

"Mr. Quill says you found the gentleman in 807."

"Yes, Miss. A little after five."

The humming had stopped. I have taken a good many calls from people who saw the same thing. When two of them give you the same sentence, down to the "little after," it isn't corroboration. It's rehearsal. Lambert's eyes stayed on the dial and his hands hung in front of him, one holding the other, the way hands do in church.

A framed card hung on the back wall of the cab at eye level, its glass dulled by the thumbs of people reading it. CERTIFICATE OF INSPECTION, it said, STATE DIVISION OF ELEVATORS, No. 4471. Passenger, traction, Otis. Capacity 2,500 lbs. I read it as I used to read a log, top to bottom and then the part someone has typed in. Landings served: 9. And under it, on a line of its own, in the same black ribbon: Floors open to public: 8. A signature at the foot, H. Pomeroy, Inspector, and a date in March. Twenty-two, I counted. Twenty-three.

"Landings served, nine," I said. "Floors open to public, eight."

"The fellow in 807 read that, Monday." Lambert said it to the dial. "Stood where you're standing. Read it a good minute, like a letter from home. Then he laughed, and said it was the only honest thing in the building."

"Did you ask him what he meant?"

"No, Miss. A porter's not asked what a guest means. He's asked for the floor."

At thirty-eight the needle crept round the arc and settled on the 8 a hair short of the mark, as if it had considered going on. The doors opened.

The corridor was oxblood carpet and brass numbers, with a window at the far end gone small with distance. Lambert said that he would hold her, that 807 was on the left, and that Miss Hightower was in there.

"Is that good or bad?"

"It's Miss Hightower, Miss," Lambert said, which was an answer.

* * *

A linen cart stood outside 807 with a green chenille spread folded on top of it. The room was what four-fifty a night buys: a double bed, a walnut dresser, one window on Ferry Slip Row, a radiator ticking as it cooled. The police had taken the man and his things, and what remained was a mattress in striped ticking and two women taking the rest of the bed apart. The smaller was a girl with her hair in a net, who tucked a pillow under her chin with the stillness of someone listening to a thing she has been told she didn't hear. The other was tall and upright, in a grey frock with a white collar, gold spectacles on a chain, and on her hip a ring of keys, forty or so, gripped in her left hand against her thigh so they couldn't speak. I started to count them and she shifted her grip. Not one chimed. Her shoes were black, laced, and resoled by someone careful.

"This room is not available," she said, without turning round.

"I'm not renting it. Mr. Quill gave me a card."

She took it by one corner, read the front, turned it over and read the blank back, and returned it the same way, as though the ink might still be wet. "That is Mr. Quill's hand. I am Hightower. Housekeeping."

"I'm told a man died in here."

"A guest was taken ill in this room. I have it to turn by two."

"Were you here when they found him?"

"I was sent for at a quarter past six, to see that the room was fit for the police. I am sent for." She shook out a sheet.

"What did you see?"

"A bed." She folded the sheet, corner to corner. "Twenty-six years I have made the beds in this hotel, Miss Szymanski, since the week it opened, and I tell every girl who comes to me the same thing. A person who dies on a bed leaves a dent in it. The dead are heavier than the living; they do not hold themselves up. There was no dent in that spread. It was drawn smooth, and he lay on it like a folded sheet." She set the sheet on the cart and squared its edge. "He had been laid there."

"Laid there by whom?"

"I do not guess, Miss Szymanski. I make beds." She did not raise her voice. She shut the subject like a drawer, and I heard the runners go. "Kasia, the other pillow."

"I'd like to see the corridor," I said.

"It is a corridor."

"I have a card."

Pearl Hightower looked at the card in my hand, and at me, and turned back to the bed. I took it for leave.

Past 807 the corridor ran on for two more doors and ended at the small window onto the light well. To the right of the window a cream door was lettered LINEN. Someone had fitted it with a padlock, a brass one the size of a hen's egg, in a hasp whose screws were bright, and below the hasp a few pale curls of wood lay on the skirting where nobody had yet swept. Hotels lock their linen, since towels walk. Hotels don't often do it with a padlock still shining from the shop. I didn't touch it. In the glass of the window I could see 807's doorway behind me, and Pearl Hightower standing in it, not working, her fist closed hard on the keys.

When I came back to the lift Lambert had gone from it. The car stood open at eight with its lamp lit and nobody in it. I stepped inside, and the doors, having no orders, stayed where they were.

Teddy Wozniak had said there was a key. I had spent a morning among people telling me what they wouldn't say, and a key was the one thing so far that had told me something, because a key is a fact with a hole in it. I had found one hole already, downstairs, in a plate marked SERVICE. A key that could call this car down to the lobby could send it somewhere else, and that would want a hole in here too.

I read the panel the way you read a face you're not supposed to be reading. Eight brass buttons in a row, the cream enamel round them, no keyhole anywhere. I put my nose close. The sharp sweet smell was strongest at the right-hand end, under the 8, where a round of metal the size of a half dollar sat flush in the panel. It had been painted the same cream as everything else, and you would have taken it for the head of a rivet if the paint had gone clear to the rim. At one edge a hairline of brass showed. I laid my thumb on it.

The plug didn't move. The paint did. It gave under my thumb like the skin on cold custard, and when I took the thumb away a perfect round of cream had come off on it and the brass showed through, dull and old, a good twenty-six years older than the paint. The Waltham, turned in on my wrist, said one minute past eleven. Wet paint at eleven in the morning, on a button that the plans, the licence and the Board of Underwriters said was never there to paint.

I worked my thumbnail into the hairline at the rim, for a better look.

"Don't, Miss," Lambert said, quietly, from the doorway.
