# Pills in a Paper Bag

The holding corridor at Central Station smelled of disinfectant and wet wool, and most of the wool was mine.

Father Dziedzic had told the turnkey I was a parish visitor, and Helena had supplied the hat: black felt with a short veil, kept in tissue for funerals and the visiting of the sick, which made me look like a woman in an advertisement for regret. The turnkey was a man named Brophy with a bologna sandwich in one hand and a ring of keys in the other, and he had the air of someone who has watched a great many people arrive and had never once seen them leave in the order they came.

"Five minutes, Father. Does the lady carry a knife?"

"She carries a rosary," said Father Dziedzic, which was true. My mother had put one in my hand at the door.

It was Friday, a little after six, and raining again. The priest was a round weary man of sixty-six who had known everything in his parish for thirty years and told it only to God, and he walked down that corridor with his black bag in front of him like a man pushing a pram. Teddy's cell was the third on the left. They had taken his tie, his belt and his laces, so that he sat on the iron bunk like a man in a barber's chair, in shoes that wouldn't quite stay on. His waistcoat was buttoned wrong by one button. When he saw the stole come out of the priest's bag, he said something in Polish, very quietly, and the priest answered, and then looked at me.

"I will ask you to wait where you cannot hear," he said.

I went to the end of the corridor and counted the bricks in the wall at the level of my eyes. There were forty-one in the course. Behind me the murmur went on in Polish at the pace of a man reading a long list slowly, and once it stopped altogether, and I did not turn round. A pipe knocked somewhere under the floor. Brophy ate his sandwich in tidy bites and watched the ceiling.

When Father Dziedzic came out his face had gone the grey of old dough, and for a moment he took hold of my wrist the way one takes hold of a stair rail. "He has told me what he has told me," he said. "Of that I can give you nothing. I will tell you only what I saw, which is a man at peace who is not at peace." He let go. "Go in. I will wait here, and I will say the rosary, and the turnkey will pretend it is a hymn."

"Miss Szymanski." Teddy stood, because he could not do otherwise, and his shoes slipped at the heels. "You have come in a hat."

"I've come to talk you out of something."

"Then you have come too late, and I am sorry for it, because you are the only person I ever paid to be right." He indicated the bunk. I didn't sit. "Mr. Quill came yesterday."

I said nothing. I would have given the Waltham for him not to say the rest.

"At five minutes past four. He brought a rug, which they would not let him leave. He sat where you are standing, with his cane across his knees, and he was very sorry. He said, 'My dear Teddy.' He said the police had a bottle and a key and a locker and a clerk who found the body, and that no jury in the world would look further. He said the Estate would be generous about counsel. And then he said that Bronia's papers were not in order."

"Her papers are in order. She's been a citizen since—"

"Since 1931, on the affidavit of a man whom Stefan paid forty dollars to be a witness. A clerk's cousin." Teddy squared the edge of the blanket with two fingers. "Mr. Quill knows his name. He knows the date. I did not ask how. He seems to know everything that has ever been written on paper. It will go to the Service, he said, with a letter from the Estate's lawyers, and Bronia will be sixty-two years old on a ship to a place that has no record of her. He said it so kindly. He said it as one tells a man the time of the last train."

"He's bluffing. The minute a paper goes to the Service the Estate's name is on it too. He can't touch her without—"

"He is not lying, Miss Szymanski." Teddy looked at me as one looks at a child who has said the sky is green. "He is the hotel. A hotel does not lie. It remains."

I did not like the sentence then, and I was to like it a good deal less.

"Think what it is," he said. "If I am the man, there is no other man. No one climbs to the Frieze to look for another. No one reads in cupboards. The names stay in the book, and the book stays in the oven." A flash of gold tooth showed. "I heard about the oven. It is the only arithmetic I have ever been good at. One life, against three hundred and eleven and a sister."

"Quill wins."

"Mr. Quill wins nothing. The book wins, if you still have the book." He leaned toward me. "Burn it. Please. If it is burned, nobody is on a list. Nobody in the world is on any list."

"No."

"Rutka. If I may—"

"No." It came out harder than I meant. "I don't burn things, Teddy. I keep them. It's the only trade I've got."

He sat back. After a time he nodded, as a man nods at a bill he has been expecting. "Then keep it. A keeper is not a murderer. I have been a keeper six years, and for a few weeks I will be the other thing, in the newspapers. I think I shall manage it."

His hand moved to the pocket of his waistcoat, found it empty, and came away. "They have my pills in a bag."

"I'll see to it."

"It is nothing. Thirty years I have the heart." He rubbed finger and thumb together in the air, the small rattling gesture I'd watched across a fortnight and six rooms. "It is only that at night, when I lie down, I like to hear them in the pocket."

Brophy was jingling his keys at the far end. Teddy rose again. "You counted the steps?"

"Fourteen."

"The sixth is loose."

"I know. I stood on it."

"Good," said Teddy Wozniak. "Good." And that was the last thing he said to me that was meant for me alone.

* * *

I stayed. Father Dziedzic went back to his parish and I sat on the long bench in the Bureau under the clock with the rain on the window and my hat on my knee. At five to nine they brought him down between Karras and a turnkey. Swain was there already, with a leather case and a wet umbrella and a smell of bay rum that reached the bench. Dolan stood against the wall with a stenographer's pad open in front of a young man who looked frightened of it.

Through the glass of the interview room I watched it done, as one watches a play in a language one knows. Swain laid down three typed pages. Teddy read all three, with his lips moving slightly on the long words, and squared the pages on the table when he had finished. Swain unscrewed a fountain pen of his own and held it out. Teddy looked at it, and at Dolan, and said something, and Dolan gave him a police pencil with the eraser chewed. He signed at the foot of the third sheet in a small, upright hand, and I saw the pencil go down for each letter of the name.

Swain's face was wet and glad. Karras was looking at the floor. Dolan was looking at the signature as if it were a thing he had once owned.

Afterward I caught him in the corridor. He had his hat in his hand and his coat half on. His face was the colour of the wall.

"Frank. His pills."

"What pills?"

"The tin. In the bag with his wallet. Nitroglycerin. He has a heart, and he needs it at night."

"They're in the bag in the sergeant's office," said Dolan. "I'll see to it."

"Tonight."

"Tonight, Ruthie."

The desk sergeant leaned out of his cage. "Telephone, Frank. It's the old man. Says it'll keep till you've sat down, but he doesn't think it'll keep."

"Rafferty?" Dolan looked at me, then at the stairs. "Wait," he said. "Go home, Ruth. I said I'll see to it." He put his hat on crooked and went through the swing door at the end of the passage, and I heard it flap behind him twice and stop.

I sat on the bench another quarter of an hour. He did not come back. The desk sergeant, when I asked him, said the sergeant had left for the night, and was it a message? I said no. Property was in a locked office with a locked cupboard in it, behind a door I could not open, and I stood and looked at the door for some time. Frank Dolan had never in his life said *I'll see to it* without meaning it. That was the whole trouble with him. He meant everything, and he finished about half.

I went home. I went up the fourteen stairs, and at the top I stopped and said, aloud, to the closed door, "He'll see to it." And then I went in and did not sleep.

* * *

At five in the morning there was a tread on the stairs. I had known it for eight years, the weight and the pause on every landing, and I knew it this time before it reached the second floor because there was no matchstick in it. I stood in the kitchen in my stockings with the lamp lit and heard it come up the last fourteen and stop at the door.

I opened it. He stood on the landing with his hat in both hands, turning it by the brim.

"Ten past three," he said. "Brophy found him. On his side, with his knees drawn up, like a man trying to keep warm. Ostrander says the heart. Ostrander always says the heart." He turned the hat a full circle. "The bag was on the sergeant's shelf, Ruth. I looked at it at four. The tin was in it."

"Frank."

"I had a call. Rafferty. It went on, and I—"

I shut the door. I did not slam it. I closed it quietly, as one closes the door on a sleeping child, and stood with my palm flat on the wood and heard him stand on the other side of it. After a while he went. I counted him down. One. Two. Three. The weight and the pause on the landing. Seven, eight. He had a bad knee on the stairs that he'd never told anyone about. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen. Fourteen, and the street door's brass handle, and its long groan, and its shut.

By afternoon the Beacon would have it in eight words on page one, HOTEL CLERK DIES; CONFESSED SLAYING TWO, over a column about the price of turkeys. It was the tidiest thing anyone ever said about him.

I went on counting. Fifteen, I said. In the kitchen the book lay in the pool of lamplight on the oilcloth where my mother had left it, with its clasp shut. Sixteen. Behind me a board creaked, and Helena stood in the doorway of her room in her nightgown with her rosary in her fist, and her lips moving, not yet stopped. Seventeen. Eighteen. I was counting stairs that had never been built, and I knew it, and I could not stop, and I was in the twenties when she said it.

"Rutka," said my mother, very quietly. "Stop."
