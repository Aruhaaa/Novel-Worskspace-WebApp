# The Shape-Up

Three hundred men stood in a ring on the cobbles outside the Shape Hall at a quarter to six, in a fog so thick that the lamp over the door was only a stain, and their breath rose from the circle in one grey column, so that from the top of Capstan Row the whole assembly looked like a pot on a low flame.

I stood at the edge of it in my father's overcoat. It hangs on a hook behind my mother's door, brown wool with a patch on the left elbow that she set herself in 1938, and I had taken it down at five that morning while she was pretending to be asleep. She let me. It smelled of camphor and of a pipe that has been dead eleven years. With one of his caps pulled down over my hair I passed, from ten feet off, for a tall stevedore with a bad back, which is more or less what I am.

Nobody talked. They stood in peacoats and sheepskins with their cargo hooks hanging from their belts, the young ones shouldering toward the inside of the ring and the old ones drifting out to the edge, where I was, and every pair of eyes pointed at the same door. In it, under the dead lamp, with his hat on and a toothpick in the corner of his mouth, stood Aldo Ferrante.

I had heard about the shape-up and never watched one. A man raises a hook or touches his cap; the boss looks around the ring; the toothpick moves a quarter of an inch. A man who has been standing on one leg for twenty minutes puts his foot down and goes through the door with his hook already out. The toothpick moves again. A second man. A third. It took the best part of half an hour to hire the Slip 3 gang, and in that half hour I watched a hundred families' week decided by a sliver of pine. The men who weren't picked did not leave. They stood where they were, the way people stand at a streetcar stop after the last car.

"You're in the wrong line, mister," said the old man at my elbow.

He was seventy, with white stubble and a face like a boiled ham and one eye gone milky. He squinted at the cap and the coat, and down at the shoes, which were mine, and up again to the patch.

"Szymanski's coat," he said, very low. "That's Helena's mending. You're Anton's girl."

"Everybody says so this week."

"Then stand where he can't see you, Anton's girl." The good eye went to the doorway and came back. "He's seen you. He saw you from the corner."

The toothpick moved. This time it wasn't at a man in the ring. Two men inside the door came out across the cobbles, and the ring opened for them with the quiet of a thing arranged, three hundred men studying their boots and nobody looking at me at all, which is a kind of loud.

"Mr. Ferrante's compliments, missus," said the first, a large young man with the manners of a boy who has been told to open doors. "He says it's no morning to stand in the wet."

* * *

The back room of the Shape Hall was low and warm and smelled of coal smoke and oranges. It held a potbellied stove, a desk with the hiring ledger open on it, a radio on a shelf with its dial lit, a calendar from a macaroni importer showing a girl with a basket, and in the corner a plaster St. Joseph, patron of workers, with a votive lamp burning red before him and a dead cigar laid across his hands, as an offering or a joke. Ferrante sat behind the desk with his hat on, peeling an orange in one long spiral. The ledger was ruled in columns and filled with names in a clerk's hand. I read three of them upside down. Skubic. Okonski. O'Hearn.

"Anton's girl in Anton's coat," he said. "Sit, Ruth. The chair is clean."

"I'll stand."

"Then stand. It is a good coat. I was with him when he bought it, at Gelb's, in 'thirty-eight. He wanted the one with deep pockets, for his lunch and his dignity. He thought a man could carry both." The peel turned and lengthened. "A segment?"

"No."

"That is the third time. A woman could starve on principle."

"You sent two men across a ring for me. I'd say it was your morning, Mr. Ferrante."

"Aldo. We are going to be a long time acquainted." He cut the orange in half and ate neither half. "You are going to ask me if I killed a clerk. You came to ask, and you stood an hour in the wet working up to it. Ask."

"Did you?"

"Declan Maher." He laid the name down like a card he had been holding all night. "They tell you it is a sin to speak ill of the dead. I am Calabrian, and we consider it a sin not to. He bled me, Ruth. Two years. A hundred a month, and a hundred and fifty at Christmas, because Christmas is a time of giving. Last week he came into this office in a good suit with a worried face and asked for two thousand dollars in a lump, and I told him I would have to think."

"What did he have on you?"

"He knew what I am. Not all of it. Enough." The knife stopped. "A man in my position has things on a pier. A man like Maher counts them, as a boy counts stamps."

"Where were you Tuesday night?"

"Here. With the returns. Forty men and a radio, eight o'clock to three in the morning, and not one of them left to make water, because in this hall a man does not leave during Ohio." He nodded at the shelf. "Ask any of them. Ask the radio, if you like. It will tell you what time Ohio went, and forty men will tell you who cheered."

It was a good alibi, and he knew it was a good alibi, and it was forty men deep, every one of whom drew his week from the end of that toothpick. I wrote it down in my head, with that last part underlined.

"Stay out of Pier 6," Ferrante said. It came out gently. "Your father died on my pier. I paid for the funeral. Remember that I carried it."

"You told me on Friday."

"It bears repeating. It is the one good thing I have, and I do not like to see it spoiled." He wiped the knife on a handkerchief and folded the blade. "The tide at Pier 6 is not for sitting in. You will hear a thing, and then you will hear another thing, and then you will find you have been sitting in it for years."

The young man with the manners walked me out through the ring. Nobody looked at me this time either. The toothpick had moved again as we crossed the cobbles, and a man behind me put his foot down, and I did not turn to see whose week it was.

* * *

The Selkirk's elevator was out again, so I heard Dolan before I saw him. He climbs a staircase the way other men sing: loudly, and so that you know where he is. Fifty-two steps, counting the half-landings, with a stop at the third floor to rest against the radiator and say something to it. By the time he reached 309 I had the new Yale open, the kettle on the gas ring and my face arranged.

"Ruthie."

"Frank."

"I brought doughnuts." He held up a paper sack gone translucent in two places. He was breathing through his mouth. The matchstick was in its corner and the brown hat in his hand, and the collar of his shirt stood open on the chain with Moira's ring, which he doesn't tuck in on days when he has stairs.

"I can see them. What do you want?"

"Can't a man bring doughnuts?"

"A man can. You never have."

He sat in the client's chair, which creaked, and laid the sack on the blotter where an orange peel had been on Friday. I thought he might look at the place. He looked at the door.

"New lock."

"Old tenants."

"I didn't know you had tenants."

"That's the point of a lock, Frank."

He chewed the matchstick. "Two hundred men saw a woman in Anton Szymanski's coat walk into Ferrante's office at six this morning. I had four phone calls before eight o'clock, and one of them was from a priest."

"Which priest?"

"I'm not a man who tells you which priest." He opened the sack and held it out, and the smell of hot sugar went through the office like an amnesty. "Eat. Listen. Rafferty retires on the fifteenth."

"Saturday."

"Saturday. He's got a gold watch in a drawer and a dinner at the Harbor Hotel, God help us all, and a wife who's picked out a bungalow at Santa Cruz. Forty years a policeman, and he'd like the last five days to be quiet. He doesn't want a story about a dead clerk in a hotel the week he leaves."

"A natural death isn't a story."

"That's what I told him."

"Did he believe you?"

"He believed me like he believes in the weather. He doesn't think I made it." The matchstick moved. "The lab went over 807. Nothing. A maid's prints, the dead man's, a Gideon Bible nobody opened. It's a sad little business, Ruth, a man dies in a hotel bed."

"Did anyone ask the lab to look at anything else?"

"Like what?"

"Like a jar on Dr. Ostrander's shelf. Pugh says it's what a man had in his stomach, and nobody's asked for one since the war."

The matchstick stopped.

Dolan said nothing. His big freckled hand, which had been lying on the arm of the chair, left it and travelled, as if without him, across the three feet of floor to the filing cabinet, and came to rest flat on the corner of the typewriter case, the way you lay a hand on a dog you aren't sure of. We both looked at it. Neither of us said what was under the felt. Then he took the hand back, as a man takes his hand out of a pocket he has put it into by mistake, and held out the sack again.

"Doughnut," he said.

"Thank you."

"I'm a policeman, Ruth," he said, to the wall above my head. "It's a very small job. They give you a badge so you'll stop asking for a bigger one." He put on the hat. "Stay out of Capstan Row. And don't take any more calls from priests."

"You said you weren't a man who told me which priest."

"I'm not," said Dolan, and went down the fifty-two steps the way he had come up them, loudly, so that I would know where he was.

* * *

Gundersen's Drug on Cutter has a soda fountain with eight stools and a telephone booth whose folding door doesn't fold. At ten to six I was on the fourth stool with a grilled cheese sandwich and the Beacon, which had found room for a map of tomorrow's parade route on page one and a missing Pomeranian on page three, and for Declan Maher or Delaney or anyone at all at the Harbor Hotel nowhere.

"Szymanski!" Mr. Gundersen leaned over the counter in his bow tie with a fountain pen in every pocket. "Telephone, in the booth. The tall one, they said. I've only the one tall one."

The receiver was warm from someone's hand. I shut the door as far as it would go.

"Szymanski."

"Miss Szymanski." A man's voice, slow and low, held close to the mouthpiece, like a man carrying a candle cupped in his hands. "This is Lambert. Joe Lambert, the porter at the Harbor. I took your card off Dorrie's desk. Your office line has a lady on it who sells corsets, and she told me where you take your calls. She told me a good deal else."

"Mr. Lambert."

"I can't stay on. They'd..." He stopped himself. I heard him breathe, and behind the breath a machine was ticking, something with gears. "I carried him. I'm the one carried him down. I can tell you from where and to where and who said do it. I won't say it on a wire. Tomorrow night, after the parade, down by the boiler. Midnight. Come to the Ferry Slip door and ring the kitchen bell."

"Why me?"

"The gentleman in 807 told me you were honest. I don't know about honest. I know I haven't slept since Wednesday, and I'm a deacon on Sundays." There was a silence, and in it I heard what he had left out. The Miss was gone, and the sir, and every manner the job puts on a man. It was the voice of Joe Lambert with his collar open. "Midnight. Bring nobody."

The line clicked and went to a tone.

I stood in the booth with the tone in my ear and the door half open on the fountain and the smell of grilled cheese. Out on the Point the Heifer began, low, and the fog took the sound up and carried it over Cutter Street and down the hill. I held the receiver until the tone gave up and became a buzz, and then I held it some more.
