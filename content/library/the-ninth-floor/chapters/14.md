# A Floor Nobody Built

The key went into the hole the way a tongue goes to the gap where a tooth was, without being told where to look. Nobody had painted that hole. Whoever dabbed the cream over the plug on the fifth of November had painted what could be seen, and a brass keyway under a half dollar of dry paint is not a thing that can be seen until a woman splits her thumbnail down the middle prying the coin up, which I had done a minute before, in the cab, with the door closed and the light on and Teddy Wozniak's card on the desk outside saying he was away.

It was one in the morning by my father's Waltham, three minutes to by the lobby clock, which runs slow and has never been ashamed of it. I had walked from Hake Street in rain that came down so straight you could have hung washing on it. The Harbor lobby was empty in the way a church is empty on a Tuesday: the fern in its brass pot, the marble gone dull under one lamp, the desk with its blotter squared and its pencil laid parallel to the edge. The jacket was gone from the chair. The card said NIGHT CLERK AWAY FROM DESK, PLEASE RING, and beside it stood a brass bell. I didn't ring it. A bell has a way of fetching people, and I hadn't come to be fetched.

Above the lift door the lobby repeater showed L. Inside, the Certificate of Inspection hung where it had hung when Joe Lambert read it out to me, in oak and brass and a glass that gave me back my own hat. *Landings served: 9. Floors open to public: 8.* I looked at the eight buttons, L and two to eight, and at the numbers on the arc over the door, and at the plug that was no longer a plug but a hole with a key in it. Then I pressed the 8 with my left hand, and with my right I turned Joe's key a quarter turn to the right, and I counted.

One. Two. Three, slowly, the way you count when the number is a promise.

The car rose, with the small shudder at the second floor and the hum in the cables that comes up through the soles of the feet. I counted that too, because I count everything: thirty-eight seconds to the eighth, where it ought to have stopped. The needle crossed the numeral 8. The car did not stop. The brass ran on another inch and a half to the tick with no number on it, and the needle followed it, and sat. Six seconds. The shudder came again, softer, and the door slid open on the dark.

I'd bought a nickel torch at Gundersen's for a dollar nineteen and meant to charge it to Mr. Wozniak. I didn't switch it on at once. I stood on the sill with the cab light laying a yellow slab across the floor in front of me, and smelled it: cold lamp oil, chalk, laundry soap, mouse, and under those the ghost of boiled cabbage and wet wool, which is the smell of a great many people having once been afraid in the same place and trying to make it smell like supper.

I stepped out. The floor was felt, a thick grey runner laid over the boards the way a piano teacher muffles a practice room. I lifted my hand and my palm lay flat on the ceiling without my arm straightening, plaster, dry, cold as a plate. That told me where I was better than any needle could. Then I put the torch on and counted paces under my breath with my left hand trailing the wall.

Fourteen. At the thirteenth the ceiling dropped, where a steel girder crossed under the plaster and brought it down to six feet and an inch or two; I ducked it without being told, as people must have done before me, and the corridor widened by a yard to make room for a door on each side. On the right-hand wall ran a rail at the height of my knee, with small brass hooks screwed into it every foot. One of them held a mitten. It was red wool, a child's, with the thumb darned in green.

I had seen that rail. I had seen it behind a woman in white gloves, in a snapshot stuck in a dressing-room mirror, under a ceiling that touched her head.

The plaster on the left was chalked.

I held the torch on it a long time. The names began at the height of a child's hand and went up as far as a tall man could reach, and they were in every hand there is: block capitals, a schoolteacher's copperplate, a scrawl by someone who learned his letters late. They were first names only. *Wacław. Halina. Józef.* Beside them, dates, in the order the Old World writes them, day and month and year, and here and there a small drawing. A cross. A star. A fish with a smile. *Rūta 3.XI.47. Andris. Oksana, 12/1/50.* Under a ship drawn in six strokes, with smoke going off the funnel in a curl, three names in a child's block letters: *JANEK. BASIA. ZUZIA.* Higher up, in a copperplate that leaned like a woman into wind: *Irena 21/10/49.*

I thought of a low voice saying *Write it however you like.* I thought of it and put it down.

Along one stretch of wall at the height of my shoulder, every name carried the same date. *Halina. Teodor. Marta. Józef.* I counted nineteen before the plaster went bare where someone had rubbed with a sleeve, and every one of the nineteen read 15/3/48. Monday the fifteenth of March. I know what day of the week it was, because the night before I had sat at a wire-room desk in a headset with a carbon sheet under my hands, and a man with a worried voice had asked me whether cars would clear the Pier 6 gate, and I had told him the wire had no information.

I had put my thumb on a pencilled *31* in the Harbor Commission, and a tailor had measured thirty-one people for coats by lantern, and here were nineteen of them, in chalk, with their names.

I don't know how long I stood there. The rain drummed on the terracotta outside, which from the inside is a flat rattle like peas dropped on a skin; every louvre in the Frieze had been painted black on the inside, and not a thread of street-lamp came through. The torch beam shook a little. I held the wrist with the other hand.

Declan Maher, then. A tally clerk with small clean hands who had been a friend to this wall for two years and afterward something else. He had bled Ferrante a hundred dollars a month, and Ferrante at least had a hall and forty men and a toothpick. What did he get from a woman with a stage name and no country? From a man who had written *Józef* on a plaster wall and gone to drive a taxi in Chicago? He had known three hundred and eleven names, and every one was a handle. And every one of them had a reason to want him quiet.

I had been hired by a man who sat across an oilcloth and mourned a dead stranger and never once said what the stranger was. I was to avenge a leech on the back of this wall. My client had lied to me from the first minute, with his pencil and his pillbox, and I could not have said he was wrong to. The question I'd carried up in the car was *who killed a man nobody knew.* The question on the plaster in front of me was a different one. *Who is keeping this, and who gets hurt if it comes out.*

I went on, because the alternative was to stay.

Five doors led off the corridor, three on the left and two on the right, and in the five rooms stood forty-two iron cots. I counted them room by room, nine and eight and eight and nine and eight, with the grey blankets folded to squares at the foot and pillows in bare ticking. A number had been painted at the head of each frame in white, and I recognised Pearl Hightower's hand in them before I could have said why: a numeral that had never been in a hurry. There was a kitchenette with a gas ring and a long table covered in oilcloth, a row of enamel mugs on hooks with a name inked on each handle, a copper boiler in a room with a drain in the floor. A calendar of saints' days hung by the stove. It was open at November, and someone had gone on turning the pages as the days went, to the thirteenth, Saint Stanislaus Kostka, and then stopped. On the wall beside it, at the height of my knee, was a pencil drawing of a train. It had a funnel and four wheels and a face on the engine. Whoever drew it had given it a passenger in every window.

At the far end the corridor opened into a long room, thirty-odd feet of it, with the card tables stacked against the walls in leaning towers under their green baize and a chandelier hung from the ceiling in a bedsheet gone grey, like a cow in a coat. In the east wall stood a safe the colour of old bottle glass, taller than I am, with a dial the size of a saucer and a line of gold lettering worn to a gleam too faint to read. The dust lay on everything. In the middle of the room somebody had pulled a single cot out from the wall and left it, and the mattress still held the long shallow dent of a man, as mattresses do. Not like a certain bedspread on eight.

On the third table from the door, which had been left unstacked, there were two clean circles in the dust. Each was the size of a tumbler. They stood a hand's width apart, and a week of settling had begun to take them back. I put my torch down level with them and looked along the baize. Nothing else had been set on it. Teddy's two glasses, then. Somebody had lifted them and carried them off, and nobody had thought to dust.

Behind me, along the corridor, the cables spoke.

It was a hollow knock in the shaft, a hum, and then the thin, descending whine of the car going home. I ran. Fourteen paces back, and I counted them, because that's what I do when I'm frightened. The car doors had shut. The shaft door was a blank brass-bound panel. On the wall beside it, where on any other floor there would have been a call button, there was bare plaster; nobody calls a car to a floor that isn't there.

I put my ear to the door. Far down the shaft, the weights were still moving.

I thought of the lobby panel, and a key I didn't have. Then I thought of a room I had sat in at nine o'clock that morning, drinking tea I'd not much wanted, and over its door a brass arc with a needle on it, and under the arc a small brass toggle behind a hinged glass cap. Someone had been sitting under that arc in the small hours, with a hand next to a glass cap, watching a needle come to rest on a blank.

I didn't say whose room it was. There was no one to say it to, and the corridor had heard worse.

Two men had carried a dead man from this floor to 807, one night last week, and they had not taken him through the lobby. I went back along the dust with the torch held low, as a dispatcher reads a street from a map, and I found where the dust was trodden into a broad path of feet, running from the cot to the east wall beside the safe. The oak panelling there had been laid in long boards, and one of the boards was an inch narrower than its neighbours, and there was a thumb-catch in it painted to match the grain. It swung inward on a hinge as silent as a church door.

The stair went down at once and steeply, bare wood on a steel stringer, no wider than a man's shoulders. I counted aloud. At the sixth step my foot came down on a tread that was not nailed, and it rocked, and I went forward with the torch beam leaping across the wall and caught the rail in time. After that I put my feet down like a woman crossing a creek. Fourteen. A door with a barrel bolt on my side of it, oiled, bright. I drew the bolt, and the door opened inward on a wall of sheets.

The linen cupboard smelled of bleach and lavender and hot iron, and it was lit by a single bulb on a cord. In front of the shelves, in a grey uniform with her back as straight as a shelf, stood Pearl Hightower. Her gold spectacles hung on their chain against her chest. In her left hand she held a ring of forty keys closed in her fist, and not one of them made a sound.

"I do not guess, Miss Szymanski," she said. "I counted the car going up."

"It's gone two in the morning, Miss Hightower." I switched the torch off. My hands wanted something to do, and I let them have a pocket. "I'd thought you'd be home."

"Mr. Wozniak telephoned my sister's house at eleven and asked would I sit up in the building tonight. He did not say for whom. He did not have to." She looked at my shoes, then at my hands, then at the dust on the knees of my stockings, and drew her own conclusions at the speed of a woman counting pillowcases. "I counted it going up, and I counted it coming down without you. At nine minutes to two, by the clock in this room. There is one way out of that floor for a person without a key, and I came and stood at the end of it."

"Who sent the car down?"

"I do not guess."

"You know."

"I know what time it was." The gold rims caught the bulb. "You have seen it, then."

"I've seen the hooks," I said. "And the wall."

She did not move her face, not by the thickness of a hair. But the fist with the keys went white at the knuckle, and stayed white. "They were children," said Pearl Hightower. "Some of them. I will tell you nothing of where they are now. Some are in Chicago and some in Spokane and some in the ground at Calvary, and I fed every one of them with my own hands for six years, and I should be obliged if you would not make me feed anything else on that floor. Come."

She took me by the elbow. Her grip was like a clothes-peg. She put out the bulb, and locked the cupboard behind us with a key she found without looking, and walked me down a flight of linoleum stairs that smelled of floor wax and onions, and along a passage behind the kitchen where a night cook's radio was playing a dance band very low to nobody. I had the sense of being returned.

"Miss Hightower. Teddy. Where is he?"

"In the boiler room, in Joseph Lambert's chair, with his thermos. He will not come up until you have gone." She stopped at the service door on Ferry Slip Row. Outside the rain went on, in the light of a single lamp. "Go home, Miss Szymanski. Go home and be sorry."

"I am sorry."

"Then you are halfway."

She opened the door with her right hand. The ring of keys she took in her left and held against her hip, closed in her fist, so tightly that the knuckles stood out like a row of pale buttons and not a single key touched another. I stepped out into the rain. She did not say goodnight. The door shut on the lamp, and behind it, as it had never sounded for six years, nothing chimed at all.
