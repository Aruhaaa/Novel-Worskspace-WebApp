# Requiem for a Night Clerk

Six men carried a small coffin up the nave of St. Casimir's, and the nave smelled of wet candles and wetter wool.

I knew five of them. Mr. Okonski, the relief clerk, who had Teddy's desk now and looked as though it had been left to him in a will. Mr. Sobczak the baker, in a suit that smelled of yeast. Hennessy the doorman. Deacon Tolliver in black, with no toothpick for once. At the front left corner, with his eyes on the sanctuary lamp and his clean fingernails white on the brass handle, Nick Karras, who had not been asked and had come anyway. The sixth was a man from the Shape Hall in a clean collar, whom nobody had asked either, and who looked at no one.

It was Monday, the twenty-fourth, ten in the morning, and the rain had settled in for the week. My mother had put Bronia in the front pew and herself beside her, shoulder to shoulder like a wall against a wind. They had been rivals at the coffee urn for twenty years and neither of them said a word about it. I sat in the fourth with Lucille, who had taken the morning off for an aunt who had been dying since March. Behind us Pearl Hightower held her key ring in her fist, so that not one of the forty could chime, and beyond her Dorrie Nash from the day desk sniffed into a handkerchief with a black ribbon pinned to it.

A wreath stood on an easel by the altar rail, white chrysanthemums on a frame the size of a wheelbarrow, with a satin ribbon across it in gold letters: THE HARBOR HOTEL. It took all the light there was. Beside it the coffin looked like what it was, a small thing, made for a small stooped man by somebody who had measured him by eye.

Ambrose Quill had the aisle seat of the second pew on the right. Nobody had reserved it; the sidesman had given it to him with a bow. He sat tall and still, with his hat on his knee and the cane across his forearm, a silver head parted exactly in the middle, and when the bell rang he went down on his knees without the small grunt a man of sixty-three allows himself. Kneeling puts the soles toward the pew behind. His were as clean as the day he bought them.

Father Dziedzic preached for six minutes. He said Tadeusz Wozniak had kept a door in this city for thirty years, and that when a stranger knocked, it had opened. He did not say which door. He looked once at the second pew, with his round weary face, and then at the roof.

At the back, in the last pew on the left, a woman in a black felt hat sat alone with her handbag upright on her knees. Lucille leaned over and breathed it into my ear. "That's Joe's widow." On each side of her lay a yard of bare oak. A church makes a shape of the people who aren't sitting next to you, and she had been given a good deal of it.

* * *

We stood on the steps afterward while the six men slid him into the hearse. Quill came down beside Bronia with his hat in one hand, and at the foot of the steps, where the gutter ran an inch deep in leaf mould and rain, he lifted the cane. He did not plant it. He held it level, clear of the wet, the way you hold a thing you don't want marked, and stepped across on his own good legs, and laid it over his forearm again. I saw the tip in the grey light, two feet from my face. A small round disc of grey rubber, flat and even and new. Not a scuff on it. Not a grain worn off one side.

"My dear Mrs. Kowal." He cleared his throat, twice. "May I?" And he took her hand in both of his. "It is the least a house can do. He was the best servant the Harbor has had in thirty years, and servant is a poor word. You shall want for nothing. You have my word."

"You are kind, Mr. Quill," said Bronia, who had been taught all her life to thank people. "The flowers were very large."

I wanted to put my hand over her mouth. I would only have been rude.

He turned. "Miss Szymanski. He was fond of you."

"He paid me."

"That is how the fond are sometimes obliged to show it." He inclined his head, as a man does to a woman he respects, and went off down Hake Street under a black umbrella held by Hennessy, tapping the pavement once, for the benefit of the church.

* * *

Calvary lies where the hill gives up and the Flats begin, so that the dead there drink the same water as the living. They had cut the grave in yellow clay and laid a plank across it. The rain came down the priest's stole in a single line. Six men lowered him, and Bronia let fall a handful of the clay, and it made the sound it makes for everyone.

Afterward she stood on the plank walk with her handbag held in both hands, though there was nothing in it now but a rosary, a handkerchief and the key to a door that had been kicked in. She did not look at me. She looked at the clay.

"Teddy was a good boy," she said. "He was fifty-four, and a good boy." It was the sentence she had given me over the urn, with the tense changed, and the change was the whole difference. Then, in the quiet, formal English she kept for the unbearable: "Keep it. Use it as you must. Not the names." And she let Helena take her arm.

At the cemetery gate Pearl waited beside the woman in the felt hat. They had the look of two people who have stood a long time without speaking and found it restful. She was small and straight, and her left forefinger had the callus of a needle.

"Mrs. Lambert," I said.

"Miss Szymanski. Miss Hightower told me who you were." Her voice was low and unhurried. "Joe thought well of that little man. He said Mr. Wozniak was the only one at the Harbor who said good night to the elevator."

I had Joe's key against my hip. It had been cold in my pocket for a fortnight, and I could not take it out in front of her; I had not earned the right to hand her a piece of brass with no sentence to go with it. "I have something of Joe's. I'll bring it to you, and the rest with it."

"Joe taught me to wait," said Odessa Lambert. "I'm good at it." A deaconess came with an umbrella and took her away, and the two hats went off together down the wet road.

Pearl let the umbrella turn the corner. Then, as if remarking on the weather: "Mr. Quill left the hotel at twenty minutes to four last Thursday, and came back at ten past five."

"Where did he go?"

"I do not guess. I count. He does not leave the building on a Thursday; in thirteen years I have known him to leave it on a Thursday never. He told the desk he had been to his dentist." The gold spectacles caught the light and let it go. "I know his dentist. His dentist is on Tuesdays."

* * *

Dolan's Plymouth stood at the kerb a hundred yards down with its engine running and the near window open an inch. I got in. The heater worked on his side only, and under the peppermint he had been eating there was rye, three drinks of it, I judged, by the care with which he took the corner.

"I didn't go in," he said. "I don't go into them. The last one I stood at the back of, a woman looked at me."

"Moira's."

"Moira's." He drove for a block. Then he took a folded square from inside his coat and laid it on the seat between us. A photostat, white on black: a ruled page headed CENTRAL STATION: VISITORS TO PRISONERS, Thursday, 20 November, eleven lines in eleven hands. I found the seventh. *4:05 p.m. A. Quill. T. Wozniak. Employer. Out 4:31.*

"Twenty-six minutes," I said.

"Karras pulled it. Took the book out of the sergeant's cage himself, on his own time, because it didn't look right to him. He's twenty-nine and everything looks wrong to him." Dolan chewed the air where the matchstick would have been. "He's been right oftener than I have. He handed it to me Saturday and I put it in my pocket. I've been carrying it around since like a stone in a shoe."

"Pearl Hightower says Quill went out at twenty to four and was back at ten past five. The desk was told the dentist."

"Whose dentist's on a Thursday?"

"His is on Tuesdays."

Dolan laughed without any sound, and then stopped. The windscreen wipers went over twice. "There's something else, and you'll want to hit me, so I'll drive slow." He did. "Nineteen-fifty. The Anchor, on Brine Street. It was the month Moira died. A man at the next stool, small, pale, decent, just let go by the Customs, who told me everyone's got a paper somewhere. And I said, the best paper in this town is in a typewriter case on Cutter Street." His jaw worked. "I said you'd kept the carbon of the whole of Pier 6. I said it to make him laugh. His name was Maher, and I never put it together till he was on a slab."

I looked at the road. A streetcar went by with its lights on in the day, the Number 14, with nine people in it who all had somewhere to be.

"I put a man in your path with a story I told to feel less alone," said Dolan. "He wrote your name on a blotter. That's mine, Ruth. Add it to the other."

"What other?"

"The ninety minutes. I'll testify. All of it, 1948, what Rafferty said and where I stood. I took three hundred dollars out of his hand in the lot behind Central, in an envelope, and I spent it on the surgeon. The city's plan wouldn't cut for what she had. She had two years more." He glanced across at me. "You're not going to say anything."

"I'm going to say you're an idiot."

"It's less than I've earned."

"It's what I've got." I watched the wipers. "Rafferty rang you Friday night."

"To tell me I'd done a clean piece of work. That Moira would've been proud." The Plymouth slowed, as though it had heard. "I stood at the sergeant's telephone for twenty minutes while the man who bought me for three hundred dollars told me what a fine policeman I was. And I forgot a tin of pills. That's the length of it. I've been trying to make it longer."

"I'm not going to say anything about the tin, Frank. Not today."

"No." He nodded, slowly, as at a sentence. "I'd like the carbon, Ruth. Not for me. It's the only thing in this town that says what Rafferty said, and when."

"Not yet."

"When?"

"When I've got something to lay it beside."

He turned into Hake Street and stopped at the kerb below the Szymanskis' stairs. The wipers went over once more and he switched them off, and the rain closed the windscreen like a hand.

I stood on the pavement until the Plymouth's tail light had gone round the corner of Eleventh. Then I said it aloud, to the gutter, in case the gutter had anything to add.

"A man who leans on a cane for twenty years wears the tip down on one side."

The gutter ran on toward the Sound. And I saw it again as it had been on the church steps in the grey light: a small round disc of new rubber, lifted level over the water, and not a mark on it.
