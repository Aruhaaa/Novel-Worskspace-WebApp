# Returning the Envelope

Through the frosted glass of Gilbert Swain's door a typewriter was whispering. The girl at the keys was one of those who say each word under her breath as she strikes it, and I had spent eight years learning to lift a voice off a bad line, so I stood on the fourth-floor landing of the Calder Building with an envelope in my glove and took down the first line of the letter before I knocked. *Dear Mr. Quill.* Then a comma, a bell, and the long rattle of the carriage going home.

Mr. Quill audited for the Estate. Auditors get letters. I put it in the same pocket as the other things I didn't understand yet, and knocked.

The girl was a Miss Tuohy, who had a cold and was nursing it with a lozenge and a grievance. She looked at my hat, then at the envelope, then at the clock, which said ten past nine, and told me Mr. Swain was in conference. I could hear Mr. Swain being in conference with a desk drawer. He came out before she had finished saying so.

"Miss Szymanski!" He held both hands out, palms up, the way a man does who wants you to see there is nothing in them. "Is it Monday already? And you've brought me my report."

"I've brought you your money."

His office was done in the colours of an expensive cigar: dark leather, a green-shaded lamp, law reports nobody had ever pulled down, and on the desk a bronze anchor the size of a fist, holding down nothing. The window looked across Cutter Street at the Selkirk, where it was raining on my name. I put the envelope on his blotter and he did not touch it. It was the same cream envelope, softer for a fortnight in my drawer beside a pink eviction notice. Seven of the Estate's fifties were still in it. The rest I had made up out of my mother's tin behind the flour, in ones and fives, and she had handed that tin across on Sunday night without a word, which told me what she thought of the Estate.

"Returning it." He considered the envelope as if the post office had played a joke on him. "Is that a conclusion, Miss Szymanski, or a mood?"

"It's a refund. You paid me to find that Mr. Wozniak was mistaken. He wasn't. I'd sooner owe you nothing, Mr. Swain. It saves on Christmas cards."

He sat. He turned one gold anchor cufflink a full turn on its pin, and then the other, which I had not seen him do in the Elks' banquet room or in my own chair, and I added it to the girl's whispering.

"Then let me be your friend instead of your client," he said. "It's cheaper. A complaint has come across this desk. From persons who prefer to be nameless, to the State Board of Licences, concerning your conduct at the Local 41 hiring hall on the morning of the tenth. A woman in a man's overcoat, among three hundred longshoremen at a quarter to six, asking questions of a union officer. They have asked me to put my name to it." He spread his hands again. "I haven't. Would I tell you if I meant to?"

"I think you'd tell me so I'd have a week to be sorry in. Who asked you?"

"The persons I act for."

"The Estate."

"The Councilman's lessors," said Mr. Swain, and glanced at the door to the outer office, where, quite distinctly, the typewriter had stopped.

I took the envelope back off his blotter, to give him something to look at that wasn't me, and held it up to the rain-light as I had held it in my own office on the sixth. Three letters stood in the paper, faint as a thumbprint on glass. "C.E.T.," I said. "What's it stand for?"

"Is a watermark a confession?"

"Is it an answer?"

"Calder Estate Trust." He said it lightly. "Owners of the hotel and of the ground under Pier Six, of which the Councilman's company is the tenant. You knew that, Miss Szymanski. Would you have come up four flights otherwise?"

"Pier Six, lessee Calder Pier and Terminal Company, E. Plum, president," I said. "Lessor, Calder Estate Trust, by G. Swain, attorney-in-fact. Trustee, see Instrument of 1938."

I had had it at half past eight from Lucille, down the telephone, in her switchboard voice. *See Instrument,* she'd said. *I've seen instruments, Ruth. This one's got a hat on.*

"The Instrument," said Swain, "is sealed at the Recorder's, and the trustee is, if you'll forgive a lawyer, a sealed instrument himself. He acts through me. By his own direction I am his face." He lifted his chin. "Shall I tell you whether I'm fond of the arrangement?"

"Are you?"

"I'm paid," said Mr. Swain, and for the first time that morning he did not make it a question.

I asked him where he had been between one and four on the morning of the fifth, since I was there and the question was free. He drew himself up. Would a man who sat down to chops with the District Attorney's own secretary, a Miss Aldous, at the Elks Lodge at half past one in the morning, have leisure to go about killing people? The waiter's name was Emmett, and he had disliked them both, and he had kept the check. It came out in a single breath, like a thing rehearsed in front of a mirror.

"Did Mr. Maher leave any papers?" he said, when I had my hand on the door.

I turned. "Did he?"

"A tally clerk collects paper. The Councilman is anxious that his company's name not turn up in someone's drawer." The cufflink went round again. "I only ask."

"Say again," I said. "You only ask."

He didn't. Behind me Miss Tuohy had started up once more, and she was whispering the salutation as I went down the stairs, the way a clerk does to keep her place. *Dear Mr. Quill. Dear Mr. Quill. Dear Mr. Quill.*

* * *

Yvette had given me the address in my mother's kitchen, along with her sixteen envelopes. Mrs. Abbott's stood on Capstan Row, a three-storey frame box four doors up from the Shape Hall, so that Declan Maher had lodged for six years in sight of the man he was bleeding and could watch him hire. A card in the window said ROOMS, and under it, smaller, NO COOKING, NO LADIES, NO SINGING.

Mrs. Gertrude Abbott was sixty and built like a bolster, in a flowered pinafore over a man's cardigan, with her hair in a net and a cold pipe in her pocket. She asked who wanted the room.

"I don't want a room. My name is Ruth Szymanski. I think Mr. Maher mentioned it."

She looked me over from the hat down, taking her time, and stopped at my left wrist, where my father's Waltham sat with its face turned in against the bone. "Nurse's way," she said. "He said a long woman with a grey streak, and her watch on like a ward sister. Come in out of that."

The parlour had antimacassars, a sampler that read GOD BLESS OUR HOME AND THE PLUMBING, and a stand in the hall with seven umbrellas in it, none of them anyone's. She gave me tea I hadn't asked for in a cup with a chip at the rim. She had been waiting a long time for somebody to ask her something, I think, and had got out of the habit of being asked.

"Six years he was here. Quiet. Paid on Fridays. Best handwriting I ever saw." She poured. "Then at the end of October he cheered up. In a man like that it's a bad sign. Paid a week in advance on the Friday, which he didn't owe, and showed me a railway ticket. One way, north, Thursday the sixth, ten past six in the evening. 'Gertie,' he said, and nobody calls me Gertie, 'I'm off to my sister in Portland.' He hasn't got a sister. They bring them to the door at Christmas."

"Mrs. Abbott," I said, "the Beacon's been printing a name for two weeks. Delaney. A man who died at the Harbor Hotel on the fifth."

"I don't know any Delaney."

"His name was Maher."

She did not say anything. She set the teapot down on its crocheted mat, exactly in the middle, and sat on the arm of the nearest chair, which was not where she'd meant to sit.

"He kissed me," she said at last. "On the Friday. On the cheek, in the hall, with his hat on. Six years and never so much as a Merry Christmas. I thought, there goes a man in love." She looked at her hands. "It was only that he was frightened."

I let her have the quiet. Out in the hall one of the umbrellas fell out of the stand, and neither of us went to it.

"He left something for you," she said, and put her hand under the knitting in the basket. "The Monday night, the third, when he came for his shaving things. 'If I'm not back by Wednesday noon, Gertie, a woman will come and ask. Long, grey streak, watch on the inside. You give it to her and nobody else.' Wednesday noon came. I waited till Friday. Then I went on waiting because I'd got used to it."

The envelope was brown, the size of a man's hand, sealed with gum and a dab of candle wax with a thumbprint pressed in it. I broke the wax. There were two things inside.

The first was a pawn ticket from a loan office on Capstan Row, dated the thirtieth of October: one gold-filled wristwatch, engraved, nine dollars. The engraving was copied out on the ticket: *D.F.M. Customs Service 1941.* The second was a photograph.

Two men stood on a church step in their Sunday coats, squinting at an Easter sun. The one on the left was heavy and square in a sexton's long black coat, with a pair of hands like spades hanging out of the sleeves and his hat jammed down as though it had offended him. The one on the right was thin and pale and younger than I'd ever seen him, holding a grey felt hat in front of him in both hands like a plate. He was laughing. In the cut edge of the frame, between them and a little behind, there was a hem of black skirt and the corner of a black handbag held in two hands, and nothing else of the woman at all.

On the back, in the best handwriting I ever saw: *Easter 1947. S.K. and me. The only one who never asked me for anything.*

"Is that all?" said Mrs. Abbott.

"It's a good deal," I said, and found I'd put my glove flat over the face of the man laughing, so that I could look at the handbag. I would have known it blind.

* * *

In November the parish gives its Wednesday evenings to the dead. There is a Mass at seven for the Holy Souls, said in a nave with twenty-odd people in it, mostly widows, each of them holding somebody, and my mother had not missed one since 1941. I went with her that week because I had sat three days in a kitchen with a book, and the book had begun to look back.

Before we left she put it in the oven, flat on the rack under the roasting pan, and shut the door with her hip. "In church a pocket can be picked," said Helena Szymanska, who had never in her life been robbed in a church.

We walked home under one umbrella, arguing about whether a sexton can be a saint, and climbed the fourteen stairs of 1127, where the bulb on the landing had gone out. A shape rose from the top step with a broom across its knees, like a lamplighter who has been told the lamps are gone.

"Mrs. Szymanska. Miss. Do not be frightened." It was Mr. Vaitkus, the cobbler from below, in carpet slippers. "I have telephoned the police from my shop. I did not go in. I am a cobbler, not a policeman."

The door stood open a hand's width on the dark. I put an arm across my mother, which she disliked, and went in first, and the floor under my shoes was soft.

Flour. The bin had been emptied down the hall, all twenty-five pounds, and walked through, so that it lay in drifts against the skirting like snow in a bakery. Every drawer was out. The pillows had been opened with a knife, and a goose's worth of feathers was still coming down through the lamplight to settle on the flour. Anton's wedding photograph lay face down with the glass starred, and the Sacred Heart beside it, face up, regarding the ceiling as though it had expected nothing better.

My mother stood in the doorway in her hat with her missal in her hand, shaking from the scarf to the shoes, as I had seen her do only once, the day they brought my father home in a blanket. She said something in Polish, a long sentence that began quietly and did not end where it stopped. Then, in English: "In my kitchen. With boots."

She walked through the flour to the stove, looking at none of the drawers. She opened the oven, lifted the roasting pan, and took the black book out as one takes out a loaf, and held it against her chest in both arms.

"The oven," she said, "is where we put the things we are too proud to say are broken. A cracked platter. A husband's boots. Nobody looks in a Polish oven. They think, what is in there? Only bread."

It was not the first thing she had hidden in that kitchen, I thought, and put the thought where I'd put the page she wouldn't read.

"At eight o'clock," said Mr. Vaitkus, in the voice of a witness who has rehearsed on a stair for two hours, "I hear boots over my head. Many nails. I know the ladies are in church. I take the broom and I bang the ceiling, so, and I sing, because a man who sings is a man with company. The Šešupė song, very loud. They stop. They go down the back stair, running. I think they do not like Lithuanian."

"I don't blame them," said my mother. It was the first time she had smiled, and it was not much of a smile, but it moved the scarf.

At midnight, when the stove was lit and my mother had stopped shaking long enough to make tea, since she could not be expected to be both shaken and inhospitable, the policeman came. He climbed the fourteen stairs with his hat in his hand and had flour on his shoes before he reached the door.

Dolan stood on the threshold for as long as it took the matchstick to cross his mouth.

"I saw the address on the night sheet," he said.

"You read the night sheet."

"Not as a rule." He came in. My mother said "Sergeant, you will have tea," in a tone that was not a question, and he drank it standing in the wreck, like a man at a graveside. Then he crouched and read the floor.

"Hall boots," he said. "Dock nails. You could stand outside the Shape Hall at six tomorrow and pick out twenty pair."

"Ferrante told me he'd called his boys off."

"Ferrante tells a lot of people a lot of things. He peels an orange while he does it."

"It had crossed my mind."

He turned the matchstick over. "The forty-eight hours I asked you for are gone, and three days more. I bought nothing. Rafferty won't sign. Rafferty has a cottage in Santa Cruz and a fishing rod, and he's not putting his name to a paper that touches the Calder Estate on the word of a dead clerk and a dead porter. The man who's got his chair, Haskell, was a quartermaster for twenty years. He thinks a warrant is a kind of coupon."

"And you?"

"I count to nine now, Ruth. I stood in Front Street and counted nine, and I haven't been able to count to eight since." He took the matchstick out and looked at it. "That's the best I can do tonight. There's a singer I'd like to talk to. Marchand. The Deacon says she hasn't been near the club since Monday, and a woman who sings for scale doesn't walk off a job she needs."

"She left here Monday morning with a suitcase and my mother's second-best coat," I said. "She didn't say where."

She was at that moment asleep on Fifth Avenue in the Flats, under a quilt belonging to Deacon Tolliver's sister, who had asked her nothing but whether she took sugar. Dolan looked at me for the time it takes to read a plate number. He looked at my mother, who stood in front of the oven with the poker in her hand, a woman of seventy defending a stove. Then he put the matchstick back.

"All right," he said. "All right, Ruth." And he let me have it, God help us both, and went down the fourteen stairs with the flour printing his heels.

After he had gone I did what I would have done in the wire room with a bad line: I stopped listening to the noise and listened to the pattern. The flour had kept everything. Two pairs of boots, each sole a diamond of hobnails, one pair with the iron missing from the left heel. They had gone through the bedrooms, the cupboards, the bin, the bread box. Then the prints crossed the kitchen in a straight line, side by side, the line of two men who have just remembered something about a Polish house. They stopped a hand's breadth from the oven door. Beyond that, nothing. Then both pairs had turned together and run, scoring the linoleum under the white all the way to the back stair.

They had come within a hand of it. Mr. Vaitkus's broom had saved the book, and an old woman's pride about broken things, in about equal parts.

From the floor below, two knocks came up through the boards, the way you knock to ask whether all's well. I stamped back. Twice.
