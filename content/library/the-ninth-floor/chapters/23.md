# A Cane Without a Scuff

Mr. Sorkin took one pencil from behind each ear, laid them side by side on the glass of the prescription counter, and said that he preferred to begin with his instruments.

It was nine on Tuesday morning, the twenty-fifth, and Sorkin's Pharmacy on Front Street smelled of camphor and liquorice and wet umbrellas. Two great carboys of coloured water glowed in the window, one red and one green, as they had since before the war. He was fifty-five, narrow and exact, in a white coat buttoned to the collar, with a blue pencil behind the left ear and a red one behind the right, and he looked at the two of us over a pair of half-glasses the way a man looks at a prescription in a doctor's bad hand.

"Miss Fong, I know. Your father buys my tincture of benzoin and does not say thank you, which in his case is a mark of respect."

"He says it with his eyes," said Lucille. She had her tortoiseshell glasses pushed up on her forehead, which she does when she means to be persuasive, and a pencil in her hair. "I told Mr. Pruitt my aunt was dying. I've used the aunt four times. He thinks my family is made of paper."

"This is Ruth Szymanski," I said. "I'm a private investigator, or I was till Thursday. Mr. Sorkin, I want to describe a label to you."

I described it. *Sorkin's Pharmacy, Front St. Rx 4187*, and a figure torn off. *Oct 28.* One capsule at bedtime. He heard me to the end without a word, and then he turned and ran a finger along the shelf behind him, where black cloth ledgers stood in a row with the years painted on their spines in white, 1931 to 1952, like a family of identical sons. He took down the last. He wet his thumb.

"Forty-one thousand eight hundred and seventy-one," he said. "Mrs. Dibble. A tonic for her nerves. Forty-one eight seventy-three, Mr. Okonski, for his stomach, and I am sorry for his stomach." His finger stopped. "And between them. Forty-one eight seventy-two."

"Who?"

"A. Quill. Resident of the Harbor Hotel. Chloral hydrate, five-grain capsules, number sixty. Dr. Hartigan's prescription, written the ninth of February, and refilled seven times, the last on the twenty-eighth of October." He closed the ledger on his finger. "Eight bottles of sixty since the winter. A great many nights without sleep for one old gentleman. I thought, here is a man with a conscience. I was wrong, perhaps, about what kind."

"What's he like?"

"A very correct gentleman. Pays on the first of the month, in cash, to the cent. When I have the rubber heels in, for the old gentlemen who wear overshoes, he asks for a pair to put on his own. He cannot abide a scuff." He took his glasses off. "Detective Karras came on Friday with half a label in an envelope. A young man, the Greek, with clean nails. I said I should wish a day to check the refills. He said Monday. On Saturday the Beacon told me the case was closed, and nobody came back."

"Why didn't you telephone?"

"A pharmacist telephones nobody, Miss Szymanski. The police come to him." He replaced the blue pencil behind his left ear with the care of a man laying down a good knife. "I read that the clerk died in his cell. I thought: a man's pills are the last thing anybody looks at in a hotel. And I sold the bottle."

"Will you say that to a judge?"

"I will say it to a judge, to a priest and to my wife," said Sorkin. "They will each ask a different question, and I shall have the same answer for all of them."

* * *

The Pacific Telephone building stands on Third with a brass door that has not been properly shut since 1931. The exchange is on the third floor, one long room with a ceiling lost in cigarette smoke, and as we came out of the stairwell it hit me like the smell of my own coat from a trunk: forty headsets, forty hands, the soft crack of cords and the murmuring of forty women saying *Number, please* to the whole of Port Calder. I stood in the doorway until Lucille took my elbow. I had a groove in my hair, once, that matched every one of theirs.

Mrs. Dorothea Nugent was the night supervisor and had no business being there at noon. She was fifty-nine, small and square, with a grey roll of hair and a cardigan the colour of a pigeon, and she had come in because she had not slept in the daytime since 1941 and Lucille had asked. She took us to a glass cubicle at the end of the board and shut the door on the noise. From a steel drawer she took a bundle of buff tickets with a rubber band round them.

"November the fifth, Port Calder to Santa Barbara. Station to station. Originating, Harbor Hotel, room eight-oh-six." She laid the ticket on the desk between us. "Started two-oh-five a.m. Ended three-twenty. Seventy-five minutes. Eighteen dollars seventy-five."

Lucille whistled through her chipped tooth.

"A call that long, somebody talks," said Mrs. Nugent. "I've supervised this board for nineteen years and I can tell you what a seventy-five-minute call sounds like. This one didn't. At twenty to three my girl on position nine flagged it to me: a Santa Barbara line open and quiet. I plugged in on the monitor key. At the Santa Barbara end, an old lady, asleep or very nearly. At the Port Calder end," and she turned her head a little, as if the sound were still coming down the wire, "a clock, and nobody breathing."

"A clock," I said.

"A good one, with a tick like a man tapping a pencil. I'd know it on a hundred lines. Nobody in that room. A receiver laid down on a table, and a person elsewhere."

"Mrs. Nugent," said Lucille, "I need a long-distance line to a lady in Santa Barbara, and I need to be the Telephone Company."

"Number, please," said Mrs. Nugent, and lifted a receiver.

I will not forget Lucille Fong with her glasses pushed up, bent over the desk in that glass box with the receiver in both hands, using her best voice, the one from the old days, the one that could soothe a drunk off a ledge by telephone. "Miss Honor Calder? Pacific Telephone, Customer Accounts, ma'am. I've a query on a long-distance charge to your number on the fifth of November. From the Harbor Hotel, Port Calder." The line crackled. She listened. "No, ma'am, *Harbor.* HARBOR. — Yes, ma'am, a Mr. Quill. — I'm sorry, ma'am, would you say that again? — SEVENTY-FIVE MINUTES." A pause. Her eyebrows went up into her fringe. "Ten? You're quite sure it was ten?"

I could hear the voice from where I stood, small and cracked and far away, like a record played in another house.

"*Ambrose rings when he can't sleep, dear.* He talks of the Christmas accounts, ten minutes, and then he says he'll let me read the figures, and I say yes, Ambrose, and I put it on the pillow, dear, and that's the last I know. I'm seventy-eight. He was always so good to us. He held the plate when they took the photograph."

"The plate," said Lucille faintly.

"*At the piano. Such a steady hand.*"

Lucille thanked her, hung up very gently and sat down on the corner of the desk. "She put the receiver on her pillow," she said, "and a man left a clock to talk to her for seventy-five minutes. Ruth, I could cry. She likes him."

"Everyone does," I said. "It's how he works."

On the stairs afterward, where the smoke thinned, she stopped on the landing and said, "What have you got in that pocket? You've had your hand on it since Sorkin's."

I took out the book. The brass clasp was warm from my hip. She didn't ask to open it. She put her palm flat on the cover, the way you lay a hand on a child's head or a table that is being dragged away, and held it there.

"You saw the certificate on my father's wall," she said. "Ruth. Whatever you do, it isn't going in a file."

"It isn't."

"Say it again."

"It isn't going in a file, Lucille."

She nodded. She took her hand away and pushed the glasses back down onto her nose, and became the Senior Clerk of Vessel Records once more. "Then I'll take you to my father."

* * *

The certificate was gone from the wall at Fong and Son. Where it had hung there was a pale oblong on the paper, edged in dust, the size of a man's regret. Walter Fong saw me look at it. "I have taken it down for cleaning," he said, and not one muscle in his face moved, and the dry joke arrived a beat after it, as his always did. "It has been taking a long time."

He measured me with his eyes first, which I had been expecting. "You have lost four pounds since the seventh," he said. "In the left shoulder, where it always goes." Then he went into the back of the shop and came out with a ledger as long as my forearm, bound in black oilcloth, with March 1948 on a paper tag.

He opened it on the cutting table under the lamp. Down the left side ran thirty-one lines in a hand like copperplate. *Pea coat, 38 reg. Pea coat, 40 long. Pea coat, 36 short.* A watch cap size for each. At the head of the page: *14 March. Pier 6 shed. Cash.* And along the foot, in the same small perfect writing: *Paid from Pier 6 office. Order of the Estate.*

"I wrote what the voice told me," said Walter Fong. "I did not ask which estate. In my trade one learns not to look at the customer. One learns, however, to write down what he says."

"Mr. Fong, may I photograph this?"

He considered it with his head a little on one side, the half-glasses at the end of his nose. "A ledger is a thing meant to be looked at," he said. "It is only people that I would prefer not to have photographed."

I had a Brownie Hawkeye with a flash gun, bought in a pawn shop for four-fifty the year I got my licence and used until then for nothing but divorces. I used the whole roll. Lucille held the lamp. Her father stood at the end of the table with his hands folded over his tape measure, looking at his own handwriting, and when the last bulb flashed and popped he said, to the lines, not to us, "They were all sizes. I remember the small ones best."

I sat for a while afterward on the step in Anchor Alley, where the laundry steam came out of the vent in the wall and wandered up past the fire escapes. The rain had thinned to a mist. I took the pink eviction notice from my pocket, turned it over to the back, where I had written *2:05, Santa Barbara, 806, lamp 2:45, dark 3:30* a fortnight before, and I took out a stub of pencil, and I put the things in a column underneath.

*Cane: no wear.*

*Gown, red, quilted, shiny at the shoulders. On a hook in 806. Yvette: tall, old, fast.*

*Ink: rust-brown. The pass he signed me. The slip in the hat.*

*"Mezzanine."*

*The dial over his door. The toggle under the glass.*

*Clock, no breathing. 75 minutes. 10.*

*Rx 41872.*

I looked at it for as long as it takes the Heifer to sound twice. Each thing, alone, was only a man's habit: a cane he carried for show, a gown he had been given, a word he'd picked up in a card room twenty years ago. A man may own a thing and still be innocent of it. But a column is not a pile, and it ran down the back of that notice in a straight line to the one place there was room for a name. I wrote it. A trust has no face, a lawyer had told Ferrante at the end of a pier. It had a silver head, parted exactly in the middle. I wrote underneath: *Dear Mr. Quill.*

And then, because the Waltham's face was turned to my wrist and I had to turn my wrist to read it, I read what I had known for an hour and did not want to know. It was twenty to five. I was out of a job, a licence, a client and a friend, and I had a pharmacist's book, a clock on a pillow and a ledger of sizes, none of which would hang a man who sends flowers.

* * *

At a quarter to six I went back to Sorkin's and asked to use the telephone booth. He had already written it out, in his small clear hand, on a sheet of the shop's paper, with the date and the number and the quantity: *I, Abel Sorkin, being a registered pharmacist.* He signed it with his fountain pen and then, because the blue pencil was nearer, ruled a line under the name with it. He gave me the pencil to take down what I was told.

Dolan answered on the second ring. "Dolan."

"I have a pharmacist and a phone line," I said. "Sorkin's book puts the bottle in A. Quill's name, Rx 41872, chloral hydrate, the seventh refill on the twenty-eighth of October. The toll ticket says seventy-five minutes on the line from 806, and the lady in Santa Barbara says he talked for ten. There was a clock at his end of the line and nobody breathing. I need the paper."

A long, careful silence with a matchstick in it. "Which paper?"

"The one Maher died for. The master's copy of the *Esther Vane*, with a signature on it that hangs the House. Without it all I've got is an insomniac's prescription and an old lady on a pillow."

"That's a good deal, Ruth."

"It's a good deal for a lawyer to make into a mood. It isn't enough to hang a man who sends wreaths."

"No," said Frank Dolan. I could hear him breathe. I could hear, behind him, the Detective Bureau going about its evening, a typewriter, a door, somebody laughing at nothing. "No, it isn't. Then get me the paper."

I hung up. I stood in the booth for a time with the receiver in my hand and the dial tone going, and then I put the receiver back in its hook. When I came out Mr. Sorkin was waiting at the counter with his hand out. I gave him back the blue pencil. He turned it once in his fingers, as if to make sure it was the same, and put it behind his left ear, where it fitted into a groove worn for it.
