# The Frieze

The building permit had been rolled for twenty-six years and resented being unrolled. It lay along Tommy's lunch counter in a long blue curl, pinned at one end by the ketchup bottle and at the other by a sugar dispenser with a dent in its shoulder, and every time Tommy wiped his way down the Formica it tried to spring shut like a window blind.

"If that's a menu," Tommy said, "I want it known I never heard of half the dishes."

"It's a blueprint," said Lucille Fong. "Keep the egg off it."

She had come straight from the Commission, where Saturday hours end at noon in the spirit of a favour, and she had carried the roll up Front Street inside a folded Courier, walking like a woman with a salami up her sleeve. Now she sat on the stool beside mine with her tortoiseshell glasses pushed into her hair and her pencil stuck in behind them, and in front of her a slice of lemon meringue that I had paid for and she had described as a rumour.

Tommy ran the counter across Front Street from the Harbor Hotel. He was a big, slow, damp man with a dishrag on one shoulder and a paper cap that had stopped being white somewhere in the Truman administration. The shop had twelve stools, a coffee urn the size of a fire hydrant, and a window that steamed from the inside. At one o'clock I had wiped a porthole in the steam with my sleeve, and through it, across a street running with rain, the Harbor had been standing with its doors shut for an hour, doing nothing with such concentration that I'd begun to trust it less. I had spent the morning putting four drawers of paper back in the order I keep it, and screwing a Yale lock to the door of 309. The lock cost two dollars forty and was the first thing that week to do what it said.

"Eight storeys," Lucille said, tapping the first sheet with the eraser end of her pencil. "Permit seven-three-one-two, Bureau of Buildings, January 1926. Lindqvist and Pratt, architects. Owner, the Calder Estate. Say it with me. Eight."

"Eight."

The elevation was drawn in white on blue, the way they print them: eight rows of windows ruled off with a straightedge, the lobby row taller than the rest, and over the eighth a deep band of waves with fish swimming in it, put in with the loving fussiness of a man paid by the line. A scale bar ran along the foot of the sheet. One-eighth of an inch to the foot.

"Lend me your rule."

She kept a six-inch steel rule in her handbag, between the compact and the spare pencil. I laid it on the cornice. "One and three-quarter inches, lip to parapet. At an eighth to the foot, that's fourteen."

"Fourteen what?"

"Feet. Of fish."

"That's a lot of fish," said Tommy, going by with the pot.

"Show me the cut," I said, and Lucille turned the sheet.

The second drawing was a section, the hotel sliced down the middle so that you could see the floors stacked inside it, each one marked 10'-6" in a draughtsman's capitals, and the elevator shaft running up through them in two heavy lines. Where the eighth floor stopped, under the cornice, sat a long box with a hatched lid. ATTIC, it said. TANKS & MACHINERY. 9'-0" CLEAR.

I put my thumb over it. Nine feet of clear air. Under it a floor, call it a foot and a half of beam and board; over it a roof and a parapet, call it three and a half. I did the sum twice and it came to fourteen both times, which is the only quality I ask of a sum.

"Nine feet clear," I said. "A tank doesn't need headroom. A tank needs a floor and a man with a wrench."

"Maybe it's a very large tank."

"Look at the plan."

The third sheet showed the attic from above: a rectangle the full width of the building, with two small squares drawn into one corner and labelled TANK. The rest was left as blank as a draughtsman leaves a space when he doesn't expect to be asked about it.

"A storey in a fish hat," said Lucille.

"Who passes a plan like that?"

"The Bureau of Buildings, on a Tuesday, in January 1926. The inspector's initials are in the corner. Don't look at me. I was seven." She took the pencil out of her hair, considered it, and put it back. "Why do you want a hotel's attic, Ruth?"

"I've a client who thinks his building has a floor too many."

"Does it?"

"It has fourteen feet of terracotta on top that nobody's accounted for."

She didn't answer. Her glasses came down out of her hair and onto her nose, which is where she keeps her worry, and she squared the three sheets with the edge of her hand.

"I signed this out as 'fireboat easement, clearance check,'" she said, low. "The fireboat's crane swings over the corner of that cornice, or says it does on days when somebody needs a reason. If anyone pulls the chit they'll find a dull one. I'm very good at dull."

"I know you are."

"The hotel belongs to the Estate, Ruth. So does the ground under Pier 6, and I had to sign the deed index for that as well, which puts my name next to the Estate's twice in one afternoon. I don't like twice." She slid the roll into the Courier. "I have a father and a desk, and a Chief Clerk who'd be glad of a reason. Don't make me file for you again."

Calder. Everything in Port Calder that stood still long enough had the name on it: the Sound, the Point, the Heights, a laundry on Third. I wrote Estate in my notebook beside it, without knowing yet how much of the town I had just underlined.

"Somebody turned my office over on Friday night," I said. "They took my address book. You're not in it any more."

"I've always wanted to be unlisted." But she looked at me over the glasses the way she used to look over a dropped call, and then she put her hand flat on my sleeve, once, and took it away. "Say it to the pie. Say you won't make me."

"I won't make you."

"You will." She ate the rumour in four bites and left at three with the roll under her coat.

I kept my stool and the porthole. Tommy came down the counter and began to polish the urn, which is what a man does when he wants to be asked something.

"You were open Tuesday night," I said. "For the returns."

"Till two. I sold more coffee than the night the war ended. Lamplighter Hill came down in a body and sat right where you're sitting, telling me who'd won what." He put his cheek to the urn and examined himself in it. "Only odd thing was the gal out front."

"Which gal?"

"White gloves. Stood across from the hotel doors, right under my awning, where you'd stand if you were working up to jump in the Sound. Ten minutes, in that rain, by the pie clock. I went to the door. I said, miss, you want coffee or you want to be rained on? She said neither, thank you, and she crossed over."

"What time?"

"Midnight. A little after. The pie clock runs fast, so take it with salt." He breathed on the urn. "I know the gloves. She sings at the Cormorant, up on Brine Street. Marchand. The wife made me take her for our anniversary, and I sat through a whole song about a sailor. It was in French, so I couldn't tell you if it was dirty."

"Marchand," I said.

"Like I told you. French."

I wrote it on the back of the pink notice, under Estate, and went off to find something to do with six hours.

* * *

By nine the rain was coming straight down in rods, with no wind behind it, which meant that a hat was no use and an umbrella was an advertisement. I wore the navy raincoat belted, the felt hat, and the flat shoes that can run.

The kitchen yard of the Harbor opens off Ferry Slip Row through a wooden gate that no one had thought worth locking, since the yard held only bins and the bins were spoken for. A line of galvanized cans stood along the wall under a lean-to of corrugated iron, with the kitchen door at one end throwing a yellow oblong onto the wet. Inside, a radio was playing a dance band, and a man was shouting about oysters. Over my head the rain on the iron made the noise of a thousand people clapping politely, which is the best cover God makes. A woman could take a piano apart under it.

Above the yard the back of the hotel went up around the light well, four brick walls pocked with small windows, and on the north wall a fire escape zigzagged into the dark, one flight to a storey. Its lowest stair was a counterweighted drop ladder, hooked up eight feet over the lean-to, out of reach of children and burglars. I am neither. I stood on the lid of a bin and then on the lean-to, where the iron bowed under me and boomed and nobody came out, and I took the bottom rung in both hands and hung my weight on it. The stair came down with the groan of a gate in a ghost story. The rain ate the groan.

Seven flights, thirteen steps to a flight, ninety-one in all. I count because counting is what my feet do instead of talking. On two there was steam at a window and a smell of onions. On four a radio gave the college football scores to nobody. On six a man snored so thoroughly that I heard him through glass and rain, and on seven I passed a curtained window with a woman's shadow hanging a stocking on a nail, and kept my face to the brick like a bad child.

The eighth-floor landing was a grating three feet by five outside the little window at the end of the corridor. Through its chicken-wire glass I could see oxblood carpet, one bulb, and a cream door lettered LINEN with a padlock on it the size of a hen's egg. The corridor was empty. The fire escape ended here. The building didn't.

Bolted to the brick beside the window ran a ladder of plain iron rungs, painted black, that climbed past the lip of the cornice and ended in a staple. A painters' ladder. Somebody has to reach the fish.

Eleven rungs. I counted them aloud under my breath, and the count was a rope. At the top the Frieze hung over me, and from underneath, with the rain running off its lip in cords, it looked less like ornament than like the hull of a boat turned over: terracotta waves curling above my hat, glazed green and gold, and in them the fish, each with its mouth open as though it had just been told the price. Level with my eyes, in the band above the waves, a row of louvres ran away along the wall, slats of the same clay set slantwise, shining wet, and behind every slat a black so solid that it didn't look like a room at all, only like the bottom of something.

I put my eye to the third louvre and saw black. The fourth, black. At the fifth, in the lower corner of the lowest slat, someone had scraped the paint away from the inside, a patch no bigger than a postage stamp, and through it came a thread of yellow light as fine as a hair.

I laid my cheek on the wet clay and looked.

A lantern stood on a card table in a long low room, and it lit very little: a cloth of green baize gone grey, a ring of dust, a stack of folded tables against the far wall, and Joe Lambert's back. He was in his porter's grey, bent over the stack, running his palm along the underside of one table, lifting it down, starting on the next. He was humming. I couldn't hear it through the clay, but I could see it in his shoulders, the slow lift and fall that goes with "Abide with Me." Past the lantern's reach the dark began, and in the dark there was somebody else. I couldn't see him. I could see where the dark was thicker.

I could hear him. He cleared his throat twice, dry and short, and I want it noted that I did nothing with it. Half the men in Port Calder had a cough that week. The damp does it.

"It isn't on him," said the voice, level and unhurried, with the edges knocked off it by brick. "It isn't in the room, and it isn't up here. Paper doesn't walk off, Joe. Somebody put it somewhere."

"Yes, sir," said Lambert.

Paper. I had a carbon in a typewriter case and an office that had been emptied onto its own floor for something that would fit in a drawer. Everyone in this business wanted paper, and I was the only one who knew what it cost to hold some.

The dry little click came twice again. "Mind the stair."

Lambert straightened with a card table in his arms and turned toward the dark, and I leaned to follow him, and the foot I had on the wet rung went out from under me.

It is not a loud thing, a boot sole going down an iron rung. But a louvre is a kind of ear and a light well is a kind of horn, and all of it went in. The lantern went out.

I hung by both hands with my feet kicking at rain and eight storeys of nothing under them, and I did what I do, and counted. One. Behind the clay, nothing: a man standing in the dark with his hand over a flame. Six. Somewhere beneath me, deep in the building, a door shut with the heavy, well-fitted sound of a good door in an old house, and then there was no sound at all. Nineteen. I had sat at a desk for nine years, and my hands knew it.

At thirty-one I got a boot on the iron and came down the ladder like a bad idea. The drop stair took me to its last step and left me dangling over the lean-to, and the roof bowed, and I slid down the wet slope of it with my coat riding up and went off the eave into the nearest bin, six feet below, which was the one for potatoes.

I sat in it to the waist. There were peelings in my collar and an onion skin on my eyelash, and the kitchen radio played on with no idea. The Heifer moaned at Calder Point, two long notes with a hole between them, and I began to count to forty.

At twenty-six the rain slackened, and from eight storeys above me, from behind the black paint and the terracotta fish, a sound came down the light well the way sound comes down a chimney, small and clear and in no hurry. It was a key turning in a lock, once.

Somebody had stood in the dark until he was sure I was gone, and now he was locking up.
