# What the Keeper Keeps

My hands were wrapped to the wrists in gauze, so that I held the fork between two bandaged fingers the way a man holds a cigarette in a boxing glove, and it went toward the plate and missed.

"Open," said my mother, and I opened, and a piece of turkey arrived.

She had refused on principle to cook a bird on Thursday, a holiday for Presbyterians, and had bought one on Friday morning at twenty-nine cents the pound, the price having fallen overnight with the patriotism. It was a good bird. I was thirty-six years old and had walked out along a cornice in a fog, and I sat at my mother's table with a napkin tucked into my collar and was fed like a canary, and I have known worse mornings. The rain had come back in the night, like a relative. The Beacon lay on the third chair with its front page folded in, because Helena would not have QUILL HELD in the room while we ate.

"Eat," she said, with the fork. "A woman who walks on cornices should have something under her."

* * *

At ten I sat on the steps of Central Station beside Frank Dolan, under the stone lintel that keeps off half the rain, and he put a cruller between two of my bandaged fingers as he had put doughnuts on my desk in November, and neither of us mentioned it.

I had told him on Thursday night, in a ward at Municipal, with my hands in gauze the colour of tea. *The Royal. Under the felt.* I did not say take it. I said where it was, which for four years I had told no living person, and it came to the same thing. He went himself. The superintendent of the Selkirk stood by in his slippers and signed across the flap of the envelope, and Dolan said the felt had been warm, which I said was impossible, and he said he knew.

"Barnwell has it in the safe beside the manifest," he said now. "He read it standing up. He said it was a very well typed log."

"It's a good machine."

"He said it would do." He counted on his freckled fingers, as he does, with the Beacon folded on his knee. "Quill. Arraigned at eight, held without bail for Maher, for Lambert, for the fire and for the fraud, which Barnwell has a long word for. Tea in his cell. He asked for a pen and they gave him a pencil. He looked at it a good while and wrote nothing."

"Swain."

"Came in at eight with a lawyer of his own and his cufflinks in his pocket. Talked three hours and never asked a question, which I'm told is a first. Two hall men, Mulcahy and Reese, hired through a hiring boss at Quill's word to turn your mother's flat inside out. Those are your boots." He moved to the next finger. "Plum resigned by telegram to the City Clerk at six this morning, owing to a conflagration of circumstance. He used inadvertical twice. Ferrante walked up to Barnwell's front door yesterday, between the turkey and the pie, with an orange and a lawyer, in that order, and the grand jury sat at ten. He'll go down for the hall's share and talk for the rest, and Barnwell says the orange was the most honest thing he has been handed this year. Pomeroy has sent the Estate's receiver a cheque for sixty-three dollars and fourteen cents."

"For what?"

"Fourteen hams. Eleven pounds apiece, at forty-one cents the pound. There's a letter on Division stationery. The receiver has never in his life been sent a cheque for ham."

He unfolded the Beacon across his knee and showed me the foot of the front page. WOZNIAK CLEARED, it said, in the same type they had used for CONFESSED SLAYING TWO, over four lines of regret that did not use the word sorry. "They set it the same size," said Dolan. "I'll give them that."

"And yours?"

"Your licence. Swain withdrew the complaint at nine, and the Board's letter is on its way to the Selkirk by the hand of the thin woman in the grey suit, who I'm told is mortified and doesn't look it." The matchstick went from one side of his mouth to the other. "Rafferty is being served this afternoon in Santa Cruz, with any luck holding a fishing rod. And Haskell called me in at eight. For the good of the service. I go at Christmas, with a letter. The pension goes with the badge. The Board's rule is that a man who takes three hundred dollars out of a captain's hand has no claim on the fund."

"Is that a rule?"

"It is now. I'd have written it myself."

The rain came off the lintel in a thin curtain a yard in front of our shoes. Beyond the side gate lay forty yards of wet brick under an arc lamp, and I did not look at it.

"Didn't you want to be a lieutenant?" I said.

"I wanted to be somebody I could stand." He considered the matchstick. "It's a smaller ambition, and a good deal harder to make rank in."

I ate the cruller. It took some time. He waited, and did not help.

* * *

Union Station at eleven on Saturday night is a hall of varnished benches under a ceiling that has been collecting soot since 1911, and the night train north stood at Track Four with its steam going up into the roof like a cloud that had lost its way. Yvette waited by the gate in a coat the colour of oatmeal, bought off a rack on Third with Deacon Tolliver's sister standing guard. She had a cardboard suitcase, a ticket, and no gloves.

I had seen her bare hands in my mother's kitchen, the bitten nails, but never in a public place, never held out before her like something she was carrying. She turned them over once, as though to check that they were hers.

"You won't tell me," I said. "The name."

"I will tell no one. That is the point of it, Miss Szymanski." The Polish l came through on the word, and she let it. She smiled, the first whole smile I had seen from her, and it made the scar on her chin go crooked. "Marchand, like people who sell things. I have sold what I had. Write it however you like. I have written it myself."

My mother, who had stood all this time with a parcel in both arms like a woman at a christening, pushed it at her. "Bread. A person who goes north without bread arrives in a bad mood." Yvette took the parcel and then took my mother, and the two of them stood in the middle of Union Station in their coats, the tall one bent, the short one on tiptoe, saying things to each other in Polish that I did not translate for myself. My mother's glasses were pushed up on her forehead. She was crying without noise, in the Polish manner, as a tap runs.

Yvette gave me her gloves. They were the white cotton ones she had sung in, washed and starched and folded into a square. I tried to take them with my bandaged fingers and could not, and she laughed, a low surprised laugh, and put them in the pocket of Walter Fong's coat and patted the pocket flat.

She did not wave from the window. She laid her bare hands on the glass and kept them there while the train took her out from under the roof. Lucille, who had come straight from the Commission with her pencil in her hair, stood beside me and watched the red lamp on the last car go out in the dark.

"A paper son's daughter would keep it," she said, to the track. "I'm only saying."

"I know."

"You don't. I've never said it. I want to hear if it works out loud." She lifted her chin. "Lau. That's the name. It's been Lau since a boat in 1909, and I have not said it in this city. Say it back to me. So it's not lost."

I knew what she was asking. We had sat eight years on one headset, and she knew that I knew. "Lau," I said. "Say again. Lau."

"Received," said Lucille Fong, and took off her glasses and wiped them on her sleeve, and did not look at me for the whole length of the platform.

* * *

The first Sunday of Advent at St. Casimir's is purple and rain and one candle. Frank Dolan stood at the top of the church steps through the whole of the High Mass, the sexton told my mother afterward, with his hat in both hands and a brown paper bag in the crook of his arm, and was wet to the knee. He does not go into churches. He stands where he can see the street.

Bronia came out on my mother's arm with the handbag held in both hands, and Dolan went down a step to meet her. "Mrs. Kowal."

She stopped. He held out the bag. It had a property tag on a string, T. WOZNIAK, in a clerk's hand. She took it and opened it and looked in, and took out a tin pillbox, small and round and dented at the lid, and held it in her palm. Then she shook it once, gently, the way you shake a purse to learn whether a coin remains, and it rattled. She said nothing. She opened the steel clasp of the handbag and put the tin inside, in the place where the book had been.

"He said you were a polite policeman," she said.

"I was a slow one," said Dolan.

"Yes," said Bronia Kowal. She did not say it unkindly. It was an entry, and she was a woman who had kept a ledger.

Then she turned to me and took my bandaged wrists, one in each small hand, as carefully as if I were a pair of eggs. "A book wants a keeper," she said, "not a shelf."

"I'll put it where it can't be found."

"By them." Her rimless spectacles caught the rain. "Not by you."

Dolan put on his hat and went down the steps and into the street without another word, which for him was a speech.

Odessa Lambert stood at the iron gate in her black felt hat, with her hands folded over a handbag of her own, as straight as she had stood at Calvary. She had been waiting, I think, since the nine o'clock. *Joe taught me to wait,* she had said, and I had promised her a sentence to go with a piece of brass.

I gave her the key. It hung on the shoelace he had worn for twenty years, and it had lain in my pocket for seventeen days, against a clasp and a glove. Then I told her all of it, there at the gate, with the Sunday traffic going by: the cab, the plug, the ninth floor and who had lived on it, the night he carried a man down a stair, what he said to me on the telephone from the drugstore booth, and the oiler, and the pit, and what I took from his neck. I did not make it shorter. She listened without moving, the left forefinger with its needle callus lying along the clasp of her bag.

When I had finished she took the shoelace and let the key hang in the rain, turning.

"He hummed 'Abide with Me' for six years straight," said Odessa Lambert, "and I never once asked him why."

I had no answer to that, and she did not wait for one. She closed her hand on the key.

That evening my mother set the first wafer of Advent on the oilcloth and broke it with me, as she has every year since I was four, and wished me health, and that I eat, and did not look at my hands.

* * *

At nine I took the book up to the roof of 1127 Hake. It was fourteen stairs to the landing and eight iron rungs to the scuttle, and I counted them and did not mind. I carried the book against my chest in the crook of both arms, as my mother had carried it out of the oven.

It had stopped raining for an hour, as it does before it starts again. The roof was tar and gravel and wet, with a chimney stack I sat in the lee of, and the Flats lay below me in their small lights, and past them Front Street, and past that the Sound. There was no fog, so there was no Heifer. I found that I missed it. Ten blocks off the Harbor stood with its eight rows of windows, and the black band above them was dark all the way along, with sky in it where the louvres had been.

Calder Point had its light. Every twelve seconds it came round across the Flats, white and quick, and for the length of a breath the roof of 1127 Hake lit like a stage and the page in my lap whitened. I turned the book to it and read what it gave me, and waited for the next.

*Pod Twoją Obronę.* Under Your Protection. On the first of the blank leaves at the back, in pencil, in the upright hand of a sexton who believed that a man who writes a false name in a holy book lies to God: *Nowak, four souls. Aegina. St Lucy's day. Nine days upstairs. Offering, none.* And under it, as small as a thing said under the breath: *Pan Bóg zapłać.* May the Lord repay it.

I counted the names. Twenty-eight to a page, eleven full pages and three lines on the twelfth, the sum that Teddy had given me in his flat over the bakery with his back turned, boiling milk. Three hundred and eleven. I ran my bandaged finger down every line and the number came out right.

I stopped. A number is what you give a policeman. It is what Quill gave the Estate, and what the pier shed gave Ferrante; it is the thing you say when you have stopped looking at the face. I shut the book on my finger, and the light came round and went, and in the dark I sat with the rain starting again on my hat.

Then I opened it at the front and began again. This time I did not count. I read them aloud, one at a time, to the roofs and the lamps and the Sound, the way you read an address back on the wire so that the man at the other end knows it has been received.

"Nowak," I said. "Say again. Nowak."

* * *

A week later the State Division of Elevators sent Hubert Pomeroy to the Harbor with a clipboard, a screwdriver and a new frame under his arm. He stood in the lobby on the first dry morning in a fortnight and watched a man from Otis cut a ninth button into the car panel, beneath the 8, where the plug had been: round, brass, the size of a half dollar, with a numeral on its face. A second man with a fine brush painted a 9 on the dial over the door, where for twenty-six years the needle had swung onto nothing. Pomeroy read the certificate aloud to nobody, in the voice of a man who has been frightened and found it clarifying. LANDINGS SERVED: 9. FLOORS OPEN TO THE PUBLIC: 8. The second line stayed, because the ninth was a floor of black boards and bare iron with the sky showing through the louvres. The first line was no longer a secret. He said it was the only certificate he had ever signed that he had read.

The Estate was in the hands of a receiver, a nervous man named Gruber, who had found within an hour of arriving that the only person in the building who knew where anything was kept was the head housekeeper. By the Tuesday Pearl Hightower was acting manager. She rode up in the car with Pomeroy to see. I did not go in. I stood on the pavement of Front Street in the thin sun, with my hands in the pockets of Walter Fong's coat, one of them closed on a pair of white gloves, and looked up.

From the kerb where I had counted, the hotel stood with its eight rows of windows clean in the light, and above them the cornice, and in the band of the Frieze the louvres were gone and the open holes ran along the whole front like the eyes of a mask, with sky in them. Sparrows had already found the sills. The waves and the fish still ran beneath, carp with their lips pursed, a foot long, in perfect order. A man beside me looked where I was looking and said to his wife that he had never noticed the top of that building before, and that it was quite a handsome piece of work.

A quarter of an hour later Pearl came out through the revolving door. The ring of forty keys was on her wrist, and she did not hold it in her fist. They chimed at every step, all forty, as loud as a sleigh in the dry cold, down the length of the steps and along the kerb. She stopped beside me and looked up where I was looking.

"The floor will want new boards," she said. "The copper is sound."

"Is that all?"

"I do not guess, Miss Szymanski. I counted." She did not say what. She went on along Front Street toward the kitchen door with the keys going, and a passer-by turned to stare, and she let him.

I stayed where I was a good while, in the thin sun. Eight rows of windows. The Frieze. The sky in the Frieze. There was nothing to add up. A man went by with a pram, and a streetcar, and out beyond the ferry slip a ferry sounded, low and long. I let it go by. I did not count.
