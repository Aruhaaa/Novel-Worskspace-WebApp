# Armistice Night

A drum corps came down Front Street in the rain, forty boys in borrowed tunics beating their drums as though the war had been personally rude to them, and on the kerb outside the Customs Annex a man in a garrison cap stood holding an umbrella over a boy with his one good hand. The boy was eight or so. The umbrella was a man's, and large, and it covered the boy entirely and the man's cap not at all, so that the rain ran off the visor and down his face and into his collar, and neither of them seemed to think this a matter for comment. His other sleeve was pinned across his chest beside a ribbon.

Lucille stood next to me under a lady's umbrella the size of a dinner plate, which she held at the height of her own comfort, so that the spokes lay across my eyebrows and a steady drip came off the ferrule into my left ear. She had on her good navy coat and a hat with a cherry on it. It was Armistice Day, and the Harbor Commission had given its clerks the holiday, and she had spent the first part of it in the records room, "because Chief Clerks like to see who goes in anyway."

Legionnaires in overseas caps went by in the step of men who had stopped counting at the second war. Girl Scouts in yellow oilskin capes. A Ford truck of Spanish War veterans under a banner, one of them asleep. A Sea Scout band came behind them playing "Anchors Aweigh" half a tone flat, with a tuba that was plainly the one in charge.

"Ruth," said Lucille, "I have to tell you something and I need you not to say anything clever."

"I'll try."

"The Esther Vane sheet is gone."

"Gone."

"The arrival sheet. The only paper in that folder. I locked the room at five on Monday with the folder in its drawer, and at eight this morning it was in the same drawer with its five tabs, all five of them empty." The glasses came down onto her nose. "The lock wasn't forced. There are three keys to that room: mine, Mr. Pruitt's, and the night watchman's, who is seventy-one and sleeps with his mouth open. And nobody breaks into a records room to take one sheet of paper. They take it because they know which drawer."

"Who knew you'd pulled it?"

"You. Me. Tibbets, and Tibbets tells his barber." She looked at the drum corps instead of at me. "I've filed for twelve years, and I'll tell you what a stolen sheet sounds like. It sounds like nothing. A drawer with nothing in it where something was. It sounds like a switchboard with all the plugs out."

"I saw it. Seventy-one crew. Thirty-one discharged ashore by mutual consent."

"You saw it. I saw it. Which means that if anyone asks me whether the Commission ever held such a sheet, I am going to say what a Senior Clerk says, that I can't confirm a document I can't produce." She turned the umbrella, and the drip moved from my left ear to my right. "Don't say it. Don't say how unlike me that is. I know exactly how unlike me it is."

I didn't say it. I took her arm instead, which she permitted for as long as it took the Sea Scouts to find the end of the song.

A black-and-white Ford came down the far side of Front Street behind the Legion float, slowly, with Karras at the wheel and Dolan in the passenger seat staring at the back of the float with the attention of a man reading a document written on the road. Karras saw me and lifted two fingers from the wheel. Dolan, I would have sworn, saw me in the wing mirror. He did not turn his head. The matchstick went from one side of his mouth to the other, and the car went on past the Annex, and the one-armed man lowered his umbrella an inch to let it by.

* * *

By half past eleven that night the rain had gone and the fog had come in behind it, up from the Sound, with the Heifer sounding every forty seconds from the Point, two long notes and a gap. Ferry Slip Row had vanished except for one bulb over the kitchen gate, with a halo round it the size of a hat. I went in past the bins and did not look at the potato one. The kitchen door had a brass bell-pull on a wire, put in for deliveries, and I pulled it and heard it clank somewhere deep in the building, and the Heifer came and went twice before the door was opened.

Teddy stood in it in his maroon jacket, with the brass nameplate and a pencil behind his ear and a face the colour of candle wax. "Miss Szymanski," he said. "It is half past eleven."

"Joe Lambert asked me to be here at midnight. He said to ring the kitchen bell."

He did not move. The gold tooth caught the bulb. "Joe asked you."

"He telephoned yesterday. He said he carried him."

"Carried," said Teddy. His hand went to his waistcoat, and the pillbox rattled once, and he took the hand away from it as a man takes his hand out of a fire he has found he is holding. "Please. Come in. Come in quickly."

He led me through the dark kitchen, where a tap dripped and a row of copper pans hung on the wall like faces, down a passage and out into the lobby, which at that hour was a cave of black and white marble lit by one lamp on the desk. The clock said eleven thirty-two, which made it eleven thirty-five. Teddy took the brass bell off the counter and set it face down on the blotter, the only concession I think he had made to the dark in thirteen years. "A guest who finds a clerk missing waits," he said. "A guest who finds the bell face down goes to bed."

Over the lift doors the brass repeater hung with its needle stopped between the 3 and the 4, on neither, in the gap where a needle has no business to stay. Across the doors, held by a drawing pin to the oak, a leaf torn from the register said in pencil OUT OF ORDER.

"Since seven," said Teddy. "Joe said the guide shoes. He said he would be in the pit by ten, and I was to send the guests to the service lift." He looked at the needle. "I have never seen it do that. It goes to a floor. It does not go between."

"Where is Miss Hightower?"

"Miss Hightower went off at ten. She said she had a headache." He rubbed his eyebrow with one knuckle. "In thirteen years I have not heard her say that word."

Down the passage behind the desk was the housekeeper's room, where on other nights, I would have bet, a lamp stayed lit until the last linen cart came down. It was dark. A straight chair stood by the door with its seat pushed square to the wall, as though someone had tidied it for an inspection.

The basement stair went down beside the service lift. Teddy took it ahead of me, one hand on the wall, calling "Joe?" into the dark below in a voice that seemed to be asking permission.

The boiler room was a low brick vault as hot as a bread oven, with the boiler squatting in the middle of it and whitewashed pipes sweating overhead. Against the far wall stood an iron cot with a grey blanket folded at its foot, and beside it a crate with a thermos on it, the cup standing by, the coffee in the cup gone cold with a skin. A hurricane lantern hung lit on a nail. Under the nail on the flags lay a porter's grey cap, crown down, as if it had fallen off a head that had not meant to leave it.

I had known Joe Lambert for an hour of one morning, and I would have bet my father's watch that he was a man who capped his thermos.

"Joe," said Teddy. "Joe, it is Teddy."

The pipes took it and passed it along.

Behind the boiler, in the north wall, was an iron door at chest height, the kind you find on a locomotive's firebox, standing a hand's breadth open. A draught came out of it, cold, smelling of oil and iron and something under the oil.

"The pit," Teddy said. "For the guide shoes. He goes in at night when the car is stopped. But he would not leave the lantern lit, and he would not..."

"Hold this."

He took the lantern off the nail and held it. I pulled the door. It swung on its hinge with a long iron note, and the dark beyond went up, a throat of it, as far as the light would carry: ten feet of guide rail black with old oil, a ladder bolted to the wall and, far above, so far that it might have been a playing card dropped down a well, the pale underside of the car, stopped between floors. I looked down.

Seven feet below the sill a trouble-lamp hung from a hook on the pit wall at the end of its cord, swinging a little in the draught, and in the circle of it lay a pair of shoes. I counted them, because I count everything. Two. They were big and black and cracked across the toe, and blacked over the cracks, and they pointed at the roof of the world.

"Wait here," I said.

"Miss Szymanski..."

"Wait."

There were seven rungs, and I counted them aloud going down, in the voice of a man reading a list at a railway station.

The floor of the pit was concrete with a skin of oil on it and a squat iron buffer at the middle like a footstool for a giant. Joe Lambert lay on his back between the buffer and the wall with his arms out from his sides, as if he had been put down by someone who wished him to be comfortable. His head was turned further than a head turns. I did not look at it long. His grey hair, cropped short, looked like frost in the lamp. Beside his right hand stood a heavy brass oiler with a long spout, upright, as though he had set it down so as not to spill it. I did not touch it.

I knelt. I put two fingers under the angle of his jaw, because you do, and there was nothing to find, and the skin was cool at the edge and warm in the crease. A black shoelace ran round his neck and down inside the collar of his shirt. I had seen it on the Wednesday and hadn't asked. I lifted the lace on one finger and drew up what hung at the end of it: a long brass key with a square head, stamped OTIS INSP. It was warm from him, which was the worst thing yet.

I lifted the lace over his head. I didn't decide to. My hand did it, the way it used to reach for a pencil when a call came in, before the rest of me had heard the address. I put the key and the lace in the pocket of my raincoat and stood up with them.

Above me, in the square of the open door, Teddy stood with the lantern, a small stooped shape with the light behind him. He had said nothing since I went down. Now I heard him. "Joe," he said. And then, quietly, again: "Joe." It is a thing I had heard on the wire from people who found the one they had called about, the name said over and over in the voice of a man trying to wake someone in the next room.

I went up the ladder with the key against my hip. It was warm in my pocket. Above me, Teddy went on saying the name to the dark.
