# What Joe Said About the Frame

The oven door stood open for warmth, as it does in our kitchen when the coal runs short, and my mother sat asleep in front of it with her glasses on their string and her hands folded on a bread board in her lap, as if she had been interrupted in the middle of a conversation with it.

It was midnight on Tuesday. I had walked the floor between the table and the window for an hour and a half, counting boards, and had got as far as a hundred and seventy of them, which is a good many more than a kitchen has. The pink notice lay on the oilcloth with its column of words. I had read the column so often it had become wallpaper.

"Rutka." She had not opened her eyes. "You walk like a horse."

"I'm sorry, Mama."

"A horse that walks all night is a horse that doesn't know where the stable is." She opened one eye and the glasses slid down. "So. You know who."

"I know who. I can't show it. I have a pharmacist and a telephone operator and a sizing book, and not one of them has ever seen him do anything. The paper's what hangs him, and the paper's gone. He turned the whole of the ninth floor over, Mama, and the Frieze, and Maher's room, and he didn't find it. Neither did anyone else."

My mother took the bread board off her lap and set it on the table, square. "A man who cannot be shown," she said, "has put the proof somewhere. When a thing is hidden, ask who walks past it every day and sees nothing."

I was standing with my hand on the back of a chair. In my head I was standing in the cab of the Harbor's elevator on the fifth of November, between the oak and the brass, with the floor going up under my shoes, reading the only thing there was to read.

The car had had a lean. I remembered it now. The first morning I had put it down to my own tiredness, but it ran a quarter inch to port, the oak panels a hair out of true, the button plate sagging on its screws, the brass rail dipping at one end like a mouth. Lambert had said it was getting old. *That cab's got moods, Miss.* And between those crooked panels, at about the height of a tall man's eyes, a frame hung as true as a plumb line. I had read the certificate as the car climbed, *Landings served: 9, Floors open to public: 8,* and thought nothing of the frame. It was the one square thing in that cab. It was the only thing in there that did not lean.

*Fellow in 807 read it a good minute, Miss, on the Monday. Called it the only honest thing in the building.*

A good minute. A man alone with a porter whose back is turned to the brass and whose mind is on a hymn. A man who has the length of a ride and a pocketful of thumbtacks. Who looks at a man reading a certificate in an elevator? Nobody. Everybody has stood in that cab, and read that frame, and gone out at their floor, for twenty-six years. It hangs in front of the whole of Port Calder and not one of them has ever lifted it.

"Mama," I said. "I'm going out."

"Take the scarf," said Helena Szymanska, and shut her eyes. "And the other one. For the neck."

* * *

I rang the Harbor's service entrance from the booth on Eleventh at half past midnight. A kitchen porter fetched Miss Hightower, and I heard a dishwasher going somewhere behind them, and a tap. "It's Ruth Szymanski," I said. "I'm looking for the honest thing in the building."

A silence, with water in it. Then Pearl: "I do not guess. Two o'clock. The kitchen yard." She hung up.

I walked down to Front Street in the rain with my collar turned and my father's Waltham ticking against the bone. There is a quarter of an hour on the way, between the ferry slip and Anchor Alley, when the whole of Port Calder is asleep and one is the only person left awake in it, and I counted the lamp posts, and there were sixty-one. The kitchen yard on Ferry Slip Row was as I had left it a fortnight before, but the bin where I had landed in the peelings was empty and clean, and I felt unreasonably let down.

Pearl stood in the kitchen door in a white apron over her black dress, the ring of forty keys held in her fist so that nothing chimed, and between her finger and thumb a different key, long and black, old as the building. "The boiler door," she said. "I have not turned it in ten years. Joe used it to go out and smoke where Mr. Quill could not smell it. Come."

We went down. The boiler room was as it had been on the eleventh. A thermos stood on the bench where Joe had left it, and his chair stood at the end of it, and nobody had moved either. Beyond that a stair came out through a door behind the laundry chute, and at its foot Pearl bent, and caught the neck of a canvas sack taller than a child, full of soiled linen, and began to drag it toward the service lift.

"For cover," she said. "A housekeeper with a sack at three in the morning is a housekeeper doing her work. The van for the laundry comes at five." She did not look round. "Mr. Okonski is on the sixth floor with a flashlight and a face. A maid of mine has been very convincing about the radiator."

"Does he know about—"

"Mr. Okonski knows about radiators."

The lobby was dark except for the one lamp on the desk, and the fern, and the three-minutes-slow clock, and the brass arc over the cab door, where the needle lay quiet on the L. The desk stood empty with Teddy's blotter squared on it for no one. It is a terrible thing to see a man's habit going on without him. I did not look at it twice.

Pearl dragged the sack into the car after me and let the gates close. "Five," she said, and I pressed it. The car began to lift, with its long soft sigh, and for the first time in my life I stood in a cab with the lights on and my hands free and the only thing in front of me was a piece of framed paper.

State Division of Elevators. Certificate of Inspection No. 4471. The type was a little blurred behind the glass. It hung dead straight. I put my fingers under the lower edge of the frame and lifted, and it came away from the wall on its two brass screws with no more effort than a coat from a peg. The screws had turned too easily. Somebody had drawn them lately and had not set them hard.

Behind the frame, held against the oak by two thumbtacks, was a Manila envelope.

I do not know how long I stood with it in my hand. I know the car had stopped, and that Pearl had turned off the motor with the little brass lever, and that the lamp in the ceiling gave a faint tick, as if it were counting. The flap was not sealed. Inside lay two sheets. The first was the *Esther Vane*'s manifest, the master's copy, in the thin grey type of the Antwerp agent: forty crew, by name and rating, and the figure forty ruled off at the foot and initialled. The second was a typed half-sheet on a different paper, white, heavy, with a watermark I could read against the cab lamp: three letters, C.E.T.

*Received for the Calder Estate Trust: 31 cases, personal effects. Pier 6, Slip 3. 14th March 1948.*

And below it, in a brown the colour of an old scab, a signature in a hand as upright as a row of fence posts: *A. Quill, Trustee.*

I knew the colour before I read the name. It was the pass he had signed for me at his desk on the fifth, and the slip in the lining of a dead man's hat. It had been the green-black of a bottle on a desk in 1948, and the years had dried it to this. Behind the manifest, folded once, was a half-sheet torn from a tally pad, and a line of clerk's writing, the best I ever saw, slanting a little to the right as if the man wrote with the wind behind him.

*Miss Szymanski. If you're reading this I was a fool. The count is thirty-one. The signature is his own. Don't let them make the others pay.*

"Give it to me," said Pearl.

I had not heard her move. She stood at my elbow with her head lifted, like a woman listening for a kettle. "Listen."

I heard nothing, and said so.

"A bell. Very high. It tinks up the shaft like a spoon on a glass." She waited. "Mr. Quill has had a bell put in 806, I think, since the Thursday you went up. It rings when this car leaves the lobby after midnight. We left the lobby four minutes ago."

I should have thought of it. I had been so occupied with the frame that I had put the dial and the bell and the man behind them out of my mind like a bad tooth. "He'll be on the stairs."

"He will be at the lobby by the time you reach it. He is a man who likes to be waiting." She held out her hand. "Give me the envelope. Whatever you hold, he will see it, Miss Szymanski. He reads a face like a column of figures. Mine he reads and sees nothing, because I have been invisible to him for twenty-six years, and that is the best thing about a housekeeper."

I gave it to her. She dropped it into the sack among the soiled sheets and thrust it down with her forearm to the shoulder, and tied the neck again with the same knot. "Where does the linen van go?"

"One-one-two-seven Hake Street. Second floor, over the cobbler."

"The Harbor sends its good tablecloths to be hand-done by the women of the Flats before the holiday. There is a ticket for each." She wrote on a laundry slip with a pencil from her apron, *Mrs. H. Szymanska, 12 damask, hand-press*, and tucked it into the neck of the sack. "Your mother will be surprised at six in the morning. She will be a Christian about it. Now you will put that frame back where it hangs, and you will ride down alone with nothing in your hands, and you will not look back at me."

I lifted the frame onto its two screws with fingers that had stopped being steady. I did not get it straight. Pearl pressed the brass lever, and the car came to life under us and began to sink; she stopped it at the fifth with her hand. The gates rattled. She dragged her sack out onto the carpet and into the dark of a corridor with a sound like a very large and very patient animal, and she did not look at me, and the gates shut on her back.

I rode down alone. Thirty-eight seconds is a long time with a bell ringing somewhere above your head.

* * *

The doors opened on the lobby. He was waiting.

He stood on the marble beyond the threshold in his dark red quilted dressing gown, with his cane over his forearm and his silver hair parted in the middle, as composed as if he had been there all night. Behind him, at the desk, Mr. Okonski held a flashlight in both hands like a man who has been interrupted at something he did not understand. The lamp lit Quill from below and made his pale eyes pale.

"My dear Miss Szymanski." He cleared his throat, twice. "It is very late for a visit."

"I was looking for the honest thing in the building."

"And did you find it?"

"Not tonight."

He smiled. It was the smile he had given Teddy on his knee on the lobby floor, kind and sorrowful and entirely without error. He did not say anything. He did not ask what I had in my pockets, and I will say for him that he never once made the vulgar mistake of searching a woman. His eyes left my face. They went past my shoulder into the cab, to the oak wall and its brass, and came to rest on the frame, hanging crooked.

It was the only crooked thing in a cab where everything leaned. He did not need to cross the floor. He read it from the door, as he reads everything, as a ledger with one figure out of line.
