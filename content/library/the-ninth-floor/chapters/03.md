# Property of the Deceased

Orville Pugh peeled a pickled egg over a canvas body bag, and the bag, being the one party in the room that wouldn't mind, took the shell. He was a long, stooped man in his sixties, in a rubber apron over a brown cardigan, with a scalp the colour of a candle and a tin sandwich box open on the trolley beside the dead. The room was white tile to the ceiling, with a drain in the middle of the floor and three rows of steel doors along the east wall, twelve to a row, each with a brass frame for a card. It smelled of carbolic and cold brine. The egg smelled of vinegar and was the more cheerful of the two.

"Wipe your feet," he said to me, who had. "Don't touch the drawers," he said to Dolan, who hadn't. He pointed the egg at each of us in turn. "I'll tell you what I tell everybody. I only talk to people who don't interrupt."

"I never interrupt," Dolan said.

"He interrupts," Pugh told me.

Frank Dolan took the matchstick out of his mouth, looked at it, and put it back on the other side. He had driven me up Lamplighter Hill in the Plymouth without a word, the heater blowing on his side of the car and not on mine, the way a man drives a witness he hasn't made up his mind about. All he'd said, when I climbed in on Cutter Street, was: "You called twice yesterday, Ruthie. A woman who calls twice is working. I'd rather know who for." I said a friend. He said a friend was what a lawyer called a client, and I wasn't a lawyer, and he would show me one thing and then I could go back to finding husbands.

Now he stood by the drain with his freckled forearms folded, and the plain gold ring he wears on a chain round his neck had swung out of his collar. He tucked it back with one finger. He does that whenever he leans over something dead.

"Ostrander's report," he said, and held out a sheet of paper.

I read it under the lamp over the trolley. Cause of death, cardiac failure. Contributing, acute alcoholism. Manner, natural. It ran a page and a half, and the half was the signature. Dr. Lyman Ostrander is seventy, and the county pays him by the certificate, which makes him the fastest pen in Port Calder.

"Did he open him?"

"Why would he open him? Heart and a skinful. You could smell the skinful from the door."

"Doc opened him a little," Pugh said. He had taken a bite and spoke round it, to me. "A little is what the county pays for. He takes the stomach contents and puts them in a jar on the shelf, same as any man who comes in smelling of a bottle. A week on the shelf, in case anybody wants to know what the fellow had for his supper." He swallowed. "Nobody has asked for a jar since the war."

"Orville," Dolan said.

Pugh stopped. He wrapped what was left of the egg in its paper, laid it in the sandwich box, closed the lid, and turned his face to the ceiling like a man who hears a telephone ringing in another house.

"He's interrupted me," he said to the ceiling.

"I'll make it up to you," I said. "Which drawer?"

It took him a second to decide I was sincere. Then he crossed to the east wall and laid his palm flat on a door in the middle row. The card in the brass frame said CARDINALS.

"I don't care for numbers," he said. "A number's a cold thing to be. Top row is the National League, middle row the American, and the bottom row is the Coast League, which is where I put anybody that's come out of the bay. The good clubs go to men who come in with a decent suit." He rapped the steel once with a knuckle, politely, the way you'd knock on a neighbour's door. "Mr. Delaney had a decent suit. Mr. Delaney is a Cardinal."

The drawer ran out on its rollers with a noise like a streetcar braking a long way off, and Pugh folded the sheet to the dead man's breastbone.

I looked at the hands first. They lay at his sides, small and clean, the nails trimmed to the quick, and the fingers curled a little, as though they had been about to straighten something. On the second finger of the right hand there was a callus on the side from a pen held hard, with a stain of blue in it that a lifetime of soap had not got out. The face above them was forty-seven by the card and looked it, in the way of men who have spent their years in rooms with the lights on. Dolan looked at the drain. I noticed that he looked at the drain.

"Pull the sheet off his feet," I said.

"Why?" said Dolan.

"Habit."

Pugh lifted the sheet from the bottom like a man turning down a bed. The left heel was very white. Teddy Wozniak had said that a man's heel is a private thing and that he had looked at this one longer than at the face. I gave it a count of five.

"You and your shoes," Dolan said.

"Nobody's got any on."

"They're in the envelope." He took a manila envelope from Pugh, who held it out by one corner as if it might leak, and tipped it onto the steel table by the sink. "Which is the one thing I brought you up here to see, so look at it, and then say thank you."

The wallet came first, brown pigskin, worn pale at the fold. Two hundred and twelve dollars: ten twenties, a ten, two ones. "Nobody robbed him," Dolan said. A comb. A handkerchief with a laundry mark. A grey felt hat in a paper bag, which Pugh would not let me take out, because he did not allow hats on the table; hats, he said, were bad luck, and he wasn't a superstitious man. A pawn ticket from a loan office on Front Street, for one pair of field glasses, seven by fifty, fourteen dollars, dated the twenty-eighth of October and not redeemed. A card in a stiff cardboard sleeve, gate pass, Pier 6, Calder Pier & Terminal Company, good through the thirty-first of December this year.

"Pier 6," I said.

The matchstick stopped moving. It lasted about as long as it takes to say so. Then it went on, from one side of Dolan's mouth to the other. "Passes go round like handbills," he said. "Plum's company gives them to anybody who'll wave at a ship."

"This one's current."

"Ruth."

I let that sit, and picked up the last thing in the pile, a folding leather case with a brass snap. Inside was a card with a photograph on it, a man with a pale, worried face under a Customs cap, and typed across the bottom, UNITED STATES CUSTOMS SERVICE, PORT OF CALDER. DECLAN F. MAHER, TALLY CLERK. Over the typing someone had stamped a purple SEPARATED 9-50.

"Your Delaney," Dolan said, "isn't."

"Maher."

"Declan Maher, forty-seven, a tally clerk, let go in September of 'fifty for irregularities, which is the word they use when they can't prove it and can't stand the smell. I found him in the Customs file at seven last night. I've got a call in with a man there who is having a very long lunch." He watched me. "Mean anything?"

"No," I said, which was true as far as it went. The name knocked at something in the back of my head, the way a delivery boy knocks, and I didn't answer the door. It would come back to me, as such things do, when I was doing something else and wasn't ready.

The last of the envelope was a pair of black cap-toed shoes, size nine, resoled once. Pugh had stuffed a pair of grey wool socks into one of them at intake, and I shook them out. The left sock had a hole in the heel you could have posted a letter through. Under the hole the skin of the heel had been very white; I'd just seen it. I turned both socks over to the soles, and the soles were grey. Not dirty. Grey and felted, as if they'd been walked through a place nobody had swept in twenty years.

"Mattress dust," Dolan said, over my shoulder.

"Pearl Hightower makes the beds in that hotel," I said. "There's no dust in them."

"Who?"

"A woman I met." I set the socks down. "His shoes are on, Frank."

"They're in a bag, Ruth."

"They were on him when they found him. Tied. And the spread over his legs, smoothed. You'd have to be tucked in to look like that."

"Men wear shoes." The matchstick went across. "Half the men who die in hotels die in them. They come in tight and lie down on the bed and the shoes go with them."

"Tight men don't tie their shoes."

"Then somebody was sober enough to do it. A bellhop. A maid. Hotels are full of people who go round tying things." He gathered the pawn ticket and the pass and the card back into the envelope, one at a time, in no particular hurry. "You want to see a fool, hold a man's shoes up and ask the wrong question about them. He died in his bed, Ruth. The heart goes when it goes."

I looked at the shoes again, because he'd told me not to. Leather laces keep every knot they have ever held. These had two pairs of old creases, a bow's, tidy on either side, the marks of a man who tied his shoes the same way every morning for forty years, and across them lay a granny knot, square and crooked and tight, like a stranger's signature on somebody else's cheque.

I didn't mention Teddy Wozniak. I didn't mention the stocking feet on a cot, or the two glasses, or a floor that the Board of Underwriters said wasn't there. It was the second day in a row I'd kept my mouth shut for a client, and I kept it shut while Pugh slid the Cardinal home, and while Dolan drove me down the hill with the heater on his side, and while he let me out at Cutter and Second with the words, "Go and find somebody's husband."

* * *

At ten to three I climbed the Selkirk's fifty-two steps and found my office door standing open and the glass swept clean where the pink notice had been.

A man sat in my chair. He sat in it the way an owner sits in a rented one, with his hat on his knee and his ankles crossed, and he rose, a little, when I came in, like a man getting up for a lady in a streetcar and not meaning it. Forty or so, plump through the cheeks and narrow through the eyes. His hair had been oiled until it shone like a patent shoe. The room smelled of bay rum. A pair of small gold anchors held his cuffs shut, and I looked at his shoes, which were tan and polished and had never once been to the Flats.

"Miss Szymanski. Gilbert Swain. I hope you will forgive the liberty." He had a soft voice with an upward turn at the end of every sentence, as if each one were a question he didn't need answered. "Your superintendent let me in. He has a weakness for lawyers. Would a man in my profession wait on a stair in this building? Would the stair survive it?"

"Get out of my chair."

"Of course." He rose all the way and moved round the desk to the client's chair and sat there instead, and it was a good deal lower than mine. I took mine back. "I act for Councilman Plum," he said, "and, in certain matters, for the owners of the Harbor Hotel. The Councilman is distressed. A man dies at the Harbor on the very night of his victory. Do you know what the *Beacon* does with a coincidence, Miss Szymanski? It marries it."

"The owners. The Estate."

"Estates are shy. Shall we say the owners?" He smiled. "I understand that Mr. Wozniak, a valued employee, has spoken to you. Mr. Quill mentioned that a lady had called. Is it so remarkable that a hotel talks to itself?"

"What do the owners want?"

"What does anyone want? A little peace." He took an envelope from his breast pocket, a thick one of cream-coloured paper, and set it on the blotter between us without letting go of the corner. "Mr. Wozniak is a good man and has had a very long night. The owners would be grateful for a short, independent report from a licensed investigator, setting down that he was honestly mistaken. Tired, in good faith, nothing sinister. A few pages for the file, and a letter to be shown to the Board of Underwriters if it should come to that. Five hundred dollars. Would that be unreasonable?"

"It's more than I make in a month," I said. "Which is a funny thing to pay for a report you've already written."

"The conclusion is not for sale, Miss Szymanski. It comes free with the looking. Does anyone ever look into this and come away astonished?" He let go of the corner. "You are, of course, at liberty to say that Mr. Wozniak is right. I would be astonished. I would also be paying you."

I turned the envelope over. The flap was blank, no name, no crest. Inside were ten fifties, crisp but not new, the kind you can spend at a grocer's without a question. I counted them, because I count, and put them back. My landlord wanted eighty-four dollars. My mother's gas meter wanted a quarter every four days and had begun to sulk about it. I told myself that a woman could serve two clients, up to the moment one of them asked her for a lie, and that I would know that moment when it arrived.

"I'll write what I find," I said.

"That is all anyone has ever asked of you."

"And if I find the night clerk is right?"

"Then you will write it, and I shall be very much surprised." He picked up his hat, and then, as an afterthought he had plainly carried up the stairs with him, put it down again. "One small thing. The Councilman gives a luncheon tomorrow for the Friends of the Waterfront, at the Elks on Lamplighter Hill, half past one. Two hundred of his friends and a great deal of chicken. He would like to meet you. Wear your best hat; he likes to be photographed beside a hat."

"Why would he want to meet me?"

"Why do men give luncheons, Miss Szymanski? Because they have nowhere better to put the chicken." He took up the hat again. At the door he stopped, with his back half turned. "Would I pay you five hundred dollars to look, Miss Szymanski, if there were anything to find?"

He went down the stairs one flight at a time, and I listened to his shoes all the way to the street.

I held the envelope up to the window to see whether anything else was in the corners. There was something in the paper itself, three pale letters woven into the weave like the ghost of a thumbprint on a glass: C.E.T. A stationer's mark, I thought. I put it in the desk drawer with the pink notice and did not think about it again that afternoon.

* * *

When the street lamps came on, I lifted the typewriter out of its case, set it on the blotter, and pressed my thumb into the corner of the felt. The board beneath it lifted. I have opened that case perhaps a dozen times in four years, and each time I had the sense of lifting the lid on something I had nailed shut myself.

Three sheets of onionskin, folded in four, soft as cloth from the folding, and the colour of a bruise: the blue-violet carbon of a wire-room log. Station copy sheet six of nine. At the head, in my own capitals, SUNDAY 14 MARCH 1948. Two lines above the one I wanted there was an entry I did not read, because I did not need to; I had taken it down word for word on a night when a good many cars had been told to be somewhere else. I put my finger below it.

2314 Caller D. Maher, Customs tally, asks if cars will clear Pier 6 gate.

My typing, four years old, with the capital R that always sat a hair too high. A sad, soft, frightened voice came back with it, a voice that had held a telephone in both hands and asked for a friend. I had advised him there was no information. He had said thank you. He had been very polite.

Outside the rain started again, and in the pipe under the window the water took up a small, steady tapping, like someone at the door who was in no hurry at all.
