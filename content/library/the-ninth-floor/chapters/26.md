# The Safe in the Card Room

Lamp oil in a closed place does not drift. It settles, like damp in a coat. By the ninth step I could taste it, flat and sweet, a coin held under the tongue, and the beam of Pearl's flashlight went on ahead of me up the last five treads of bare pine and found at the head of them the edge of an oak panel standing open, and beyond it, low on the boards, a light the colour of weak tea.

I stopped with one foot on the top step.

"The sixth, I think," said a voice in the room beyond. It was not raised. It did not need to be; the stair carried it down to me as a pipe carries water. "I have always meant to have it seen to."

I switched off the flashlight. There was no use in it. He had kept a lantern lit for the best part of an hour, and he had known since the cab at four that morning, or since the day I came up his front steps with a pass signed in green-black ink, that I would be the one to climb. I put my hand flat on the oak and went through.

The Calder Club room is thirty-six feet by twenty, and I had seen it once, by the nickel glare of a torch, a dead room under grey sheets. Now it had been lit for company. A hurricane lantern stood on the boards beside the nearest of the card tables, turned low, and the circle it made took in a cane-bottomed chair, a pair of black oxfords, the lower half of an ebony cane, and the legs of a table with a square of green baize on it. The rest of the room stood back in the dark the way an audience stands back from a stage: the stacked tables along the wall, the chandelier in its sheet like a bell under a cover, the long black mouth of the corridor at the far end. Behind my shoulder, in the east wall, the safe stood open with its door flung wide and the gold lettering worn to a ghost, and nothing inside it but pigeonholes.

Ambrose Quill sat at the table in a black dinner jacket and a stiff white collar, with his cane across his knees and his silver hair parted exactly in the middle, as composed as if the evening had been arranged for him. In front of him on the baize lay a ledger bound in green cloth, open at a page of small upright figures, with a silver pencil laid across the gutter, parallel to a line he had just ruled. By his right foot stood a five-pint can with a spout. By his left, a leather bag lay empty on its side.

"My dear Miss Szymanski." He cleared his throat, twice. "It was the Mezzanine, in my day."

"Nine," I said. "Pomeroy's register has it as nine, and the Frieze has it as nine, and Sergeant Dolan took his hat off to it in Front Street."

He tilted his head as at a clerk who has been loud. "Do sit down."

"I'll stand."

"As you wish." He did not rise. A gentleman rises for a lady. I put it in the column where I keep such things, and went round the table to the far side, so that the baize and the lantern lay between us.

I had thought for eleven days about this room, what I would say in it and what I would do with my hands. What I did was remember the wire room. You do not argue with a caller. You read it back to him, address by address, until he stops telling you that you have it wrong.

"I'll read it back," I said. "Two-oh-five on the morning of the fifth, the operator rings 806 for Santa Barbara. Seventy-five minutes, eighteen dollars and seventy-five cents. Miss Honor Calder says you talked for ten and she put the receiver on her pillow. Mrs. Nugent at the exchange plugged in at twenty to three. A clock at your end, and nobody breathing."

He took up the silver pencil. "Conjecture," he said, and made a small mark in the margin of the ledger, as though I were a figure he was checking.

A long way off the Heifer called, two notes, low. I counted forty and went on.

"Rx forty-one eight seventy-two. Chloral hydrate, five-grain, number sixty, seventh refill, the twenty-eighth of October. Your name in Mr. Sorkin's book, and the bottle in Teddy Wozniak's locker came off that refill."

"Hearsay." The pencil ticked.

"Your ink. It dries rust-brown. The pass you signed me, the slip in Maher's hatband, the receipt for the *Esther Vane*."

"It is a very old formula." I will swear there was a warmth in it. "Dorset."

"A tall man in a dark red quilted gown, shiny at the shoulders, on the eighth floor at ten past midnight. A tray, two glasses, the key already in his hand. Walking faster than an old man ought."

"Possible."

"Two glasses on nine and none in 807. Maher's shoes off on nine and on in 807. You and Joe put them on him, and you forgot the sock."

"Possible," said Mr. Quill again, and marked the margin.

It was somewhere about the third of these that I understood he was not defending himself. He was auditing me. Each entry I read out he took as a senior takes a junior's column, with courtesy and without hope, sustaining it or striking it, and what I could not forgive was that I would have given most of them the same marks. Everything I had was conjecture, or hearsay, or possible. A good deal of the world is.

"The cane," I said. I looked at it across his knees. "Twenty years of a bad hip, they tell me. A man who leans on a stick wears the tip down on one side. Yours is as round as a new pencil eraser. You have never put your weight on it."

He looked down at it. "I take care of my things."

"Joe Lambert." My mouth had gone dry. "A man who spends his evening greasing the guide rails, with an oiler full to the spout and not a smear on his palms. You hit him with it, and you put him in the pit."

Mr. Quill laid the pencil in the gutter of the ledger and looked at the lantern. For the whole of the Heifer's forty seconds he did not move, not a finger, and I could hear the flame ticking on its wick, and under the floor the old hotel going about its holiday. "I was very fond of Joseph," he said.

It was the only answer of the evening that I felt in my hands.

"The *Esther Vane*," I said, when I could. "Thirty-one cases, personal effects. Received by the Trustee."

"I invoiced them."

"They were people."

"The insurers required a signature."

I would have liked to hit him with something. I looked down and found that my hands were holding my father's Waltham, turning it round and round on the bone with the face to the inside of my wrist. It said twenty-seven minutes past seven.

"And Teddy," I said. "You sat on his bunk with a rug and told him about his sister's papers. He signed with a chewed pencil and died with his knees drawn up."

Mr. Quill took a moment over it, as over a figure he had not expected to find in that column. "The Estate," he said, "has outlived four generations of Calders. It can outlive a night clerk."

The Heifer called, and I let it go by uncounted, because I had run out of ways to ask the same man the same question, and I laid down the only card I had left.

"Sergeant Dolan is outside."

"I doubt it." He closed the ledger over the pencil, gently, as one shuts a prayer book, and laid his long yellow hands on the cover. "Judge Pardoe keeps the holiday with his sister at Stannard's Ferry. She has no telephone; Mr. Swain was good enough to let me know. The sergeant is a punctual man and an honest one, and I think he is in a motor-car on a country road with a District Attorney, waiting to be handed a signature."

"The manifest isn't behind the frame any more," I said. "It's in Hollis Barnwell's safe. You can burn this floor and every ledger in Port Calder, Mr. Quill, and a half-sheet with your name on it will go on being there."

He did not answer. The Heifer called, and I counted forty, the whole of it, and he sat with his hands flat on the green cloth and his pale eyes on the lantern, and I watched him add it up. It took him no time at all. The figure had been in front of him for hours. Then he stood, with a small creak of the knees, and laid the cane over his forearm, and picked up the can.

"A fire in a roof space is a thing one insures against, my dear," he said, and began to pour. He did it as a man waters a plant, unhurried, a little at a time: along the baize, down the leg of the table, across the boards toward the cot that stood pulled out from the wall with a dent in its mattress the shape of a sleeping man. "The brigade will be at the cornice in six minutes."

The smell stopped being a smell. It became the room.

I kicked the lantern. It stood on the floor between his shoes and mine and I had been looking at it for half an hour. It went over on its side with the glass starred, and the flame sat on the wet boards for the length of a breath, and then it ran, quick and blue along the track he had laid, and the baize went up with a sound like a sheet shaken out of a window.

Mr. Quill stepped back out of it with the care of a man avoiding a puddle. There was not a mark on his dinner jacket. He did not hurry. He was through the panel with the cane over his arm before I had got round the table, and the oak swung to behind him, and I heard the iron fall into its brackets with the small bright sound of a latch on a good gate.

The ledger lay on the baize in the path of the fire. I thought of nothing at all and took it, five pounds of green cloth already hot at one corner, and shoved it inside my coat against my ribs. Then I put my shoulder to the panel. It gave the width of a finger and stopped against iron.

Down in the stair, as if down a well, the cane began to go.

I counted it. There is nothing else to do with a sound like that. A man who is hurried drags, and a man who limps puts the stick down early to take the weight. This was neither. The tip met every tread flat and level with a pause between that a clock would have been proud of, and I have never in my life heard anything go down a stair so well.

One, and I kicked the panel low, where an iron bar is weakest, and the iron was not weak.

Two, and the baize was a sheet of flame to the ceiling and my face tightened in it like a drumhead.

Three was the safe. I put my head into it: pigeonholes, eighteen inches of steel, a door that would swing home on me and be a coffin with a good lock. I took my head out.

Four was the corridor. The oil had run ahead of the fire down the grey felt runner and into the doorways of the dormitories, and when I put the flashlight along it the cots stood in their rows with their blankets shining, rank on rank, forty-two of them, and the flame coming after me down the felt as fast as a man walks.

Five was the wall telephone. I lifted the earpiece and there was not even a hiss. The cord had been cut at the skirting that afternoon, and the copper end shone like a new penny.

Six, and the loose tread spoke under him, a small dry crack like a knuckle, exactly as it had spoken under me. The cane did not falter.

Seven was the kitchenette. A laundry copper bolted to the floor, a bench, a gas ring, a row of enamel mugs with names inked on their sides, and above the bench a louvre painted black on the inside, a yard above my reach.

Eight, and the first of the cots caught. It took the flame with a cough, and then, a second later, with another.

Nine was the wall. The left side of the corridor, where the names were. The fire reached the plaster and the lime on it browned, and the chalk went from white to the colour of weak tea: Wacław, Halina, Józef, Rūta; Irena, with her date; JANEK, BASIA and ZUZIA under their drawing of a ship. I had held a torch to them once for as long as it takes to say a rosary and had not copied one down. They turned the colour of old paper. Then they turned the colour of nothing.

Ten, eleven and twelve were smoke. I went down on my knees where the air was, a hand's breadth off the boards, and I thought of Teddy squaring a pencil on a blotter, and of my mother's oven, and of nothing.

Thirteen, and I came back to the panel on my hands and knees, since it was the one place on that floor I knew.

Fourteen, and I laid my ear against the oak and heard the last of them, far below, on the tile of the linen cupboard, and then a door.

I know it took longer than fourteen steps. A man cannot walk down a stair in the time it takes to watch a wall of names go out. But that is how I have kept it, the whole of that minute, put in order by a stick: fourteen taps, level as a metronome, the loose tread taken without a hitch. A man who leans on a cane wears the tip down on one side. He had never leaned on his in his life. There was no limp in him anywhere.
