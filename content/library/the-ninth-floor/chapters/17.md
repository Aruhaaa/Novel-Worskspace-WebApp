# Fish Market, Before Light

Crushed ice slid off a shovel onto a halibut, and the gulls went up from the pilings in a single white argument and came down again. Under a row of kerosene lamps swinging from a beam, the municipal fish market was already at work at four in the morning: bay boats unloading, men in oilskins throwing crab baskets, and at the end of the third steel table, in a rubber apron over his good dark coat, with his hat brim snapped down, Aldo Ferrante was cleaning a cod.

He did it with a short curved knife and no wasted motion. He laid the fish on its back, ran the blade from vent to gill in one pull, and took out the insides in a single handful, which he gave to a gull as a man hands a coin to a child. The top joint of his left ring finger was missing, and the stump worked among the other fingers like a person who knows the way.

"You came alone," he said, without looking up.

"I came with a torch and my mother's blessing."

"Then you came with company." He rinsed the fish in a bucket of seawater. "My mother eats cod on Sundays. In Calabria she'd have waited for the salt kind, hung up like washing. Here she gets it fresh, and complains. I clean it myself. A man who lets somebody else clean his fish is a man who is going to be told lies at dinner." He glanced at me then, from under the brim, and went back to the knife. "Stand where I can see you. People get nervous of a woman at their back."

I stood by the ice. My shoes were wet through already, and the cold came up from the concrete and settled somewhere near the knees. I had slept two hours.

"I didn't kill Maher," Ferrante said. "I didn't kill the porter. I tell you before you ask, so you can stop asking it with your face."

"I'm not asking."

"You're Anton's girl. You've never in your life asked anything with your mouth first." He wiped the knife on the apron. "Last night you heard me tell a lawyer to show me a face. You heard him say a trust has none. I'd like you to know I'm a man who was told, in 1948, he was doing business with a family. The Calders, old money, church money. The House."

"The House."

"That's what Stefan's people called it. Stefan Kowal. The sexton." He started on a second cod, a smaller one. "You know who he was?"

"Teddy Wozniak's brother-in-law."

"A man with a heart like a church and a temper like a stove. He came to the Shape Hall in the winter of '46 with a Pole off a Greek boat and asked could the Local put a man to work. I gave the man a chit. Then he came with another. For nearly two years it was a favour, you understand, a kindness between Catholics. Then in March of '48 a Liberty ship docked at Slip 3 with thirty-one people on it the Customs was not going to count."

"The Esther Vane."

"You know the name." He did not seem surprised. "A tally clerk had to alter a manifest. A pier had to look the other way. A hall had to find thirty-one men's worth of papers, and a place to put them." The knife went into the fish. "That's when it became a thing with a price, Ruth. I won't say I'm proud. Four hundred dollars a head. A hundred and fifty to me for the hall. A hundred to Plum's company for the pier. A hundred and fifty to the House."

"For what?"

"For the roof. Whatever roof it was. I didn't ask whose." He lifted his chin toward the gulls. "In my line you don't ask the name of the hand that takes your money. You ask the sum, and you ask if it will be there next week. I told myself it was Plum's wife's cousins, in Calder Heights, in their furs." He shook his head over the fish. "Four hundred dollars. A man in a camp in Germany would have paid four thousand. I never thought of it as selling. I called it the fare."

"And Maher?"

"Maher was a tally clerk who had the arithmetic and the nerves. The week they let him go in 1950 he sat in my office and told me that if I didn't pay him a hundred dollars a month, he would write out the whole list for the Immigration, in his own hand, to the minute." The blade turned. "He wasn't wrong. Three hundred and eleven people, Ruth. Do you know what the Immigration does with three hundred and eleven? You think a deportation is a train ride. It's a door. Some of them have no country left to be sent to. I've seen a man held on a freighter for a year because nobody would sign for him."

He did not say it with any heat. A man in a rubber apron at four in the morning, telling a woman what he'd done.

"Last week he came for two thousand," I said.

"He said he had the ship's own paper. The master's copy, with a signature on it that would hang the House. I told him a man can't hang a house." He laid the knife down. "I'll tell you a thing. That day I thought about it. I thought about a pier, and a bad step in the fog. I'm telling you I thought it because you will hear that I did, and I'd rather you heard it from me. But somebody got there first, and I was at the Hall with forty men and a radio, and you've asked them."

"I have."

"Then you know." He took a cloth from his belt and cleaned his hands, one finger at a time, including the short one. "Now I tell you the other thing. I think I'm next. Every man on the list of mouths. Maher, then the porter, who had no part in anything but a key. Who has the paper? The paper is a rope, and the rope has two ends. Somebody is going round the ring with a pair of scissors."

"There's a list?"

"There's a book. Stefan kept the names. *God keeps accounts,* he used to say, and he wrote them in a prayer book in pencil, so that God could read them. I never saw it. I know what it is by the way a man looks when I ask about it." He took out an orange from the coat pocket under the apron, and the pocketknife from the other, and looked at the two of them, and put the orange away again. "That envelope last night is what the Estate pays when it wants Maher's paper found and my mouth shut. It's been paying since the fourth. My boys turned your office on the seventh. They turned over the Kowal woman's flat on the Tuesday and again on the Thursday. Nobody found anything. I've called them off. Don't thank me. I want it understood, that's all."

"Why tell me?"

He leaned both hands on the steel and looked at the gutted fish, and the gulls, and the long grey line coming up at the end of the pier where the sky would be.

"Because your father is on my conscience, and he's the only one on it who's earned his place there." The soft voice lost a little of its courtesy. "And because there's a night in March four years ago that you and I both remember, Ruth, and I'd rather talk about it myself. Your wire went quiet for ninety minutes because I paid Rafferty. Fifteen hundred dollars. I didn't ask who he paid after."

I said nothing. The lamps swung. A boy came by with a barrow of ice and looked at neither of us.

"Say something," said Ferrante.

"I'm counting."

"What?"

"It's been four years, eight months and two days," I said. "I'm trying to get to the end of it."

He let me. He took the cod by the gills, wrapped it in the *Courier,* and put it into a paper sack with a handful of ice; he did it with great gentleness, as a man would wrap something that had been alive that morning. "Go to your mother's," he said. "Go to church. The Polish women will know where the book is. Mrs. Kowal has the look of a woman who has swallowed somebody's secret and is waiting for the priest."

* * *

My mother had left a note on the table in her large flat hand. *Mrs. Wach says the Kowal door has been kicked. I go with bread. You come when you wake. EAT FIRST. H.* There was a boiled egg under a cup. I ate it standing, and went.

The Kowal flat was on Eleventh Avenue behind the church, up an outside stair, three rooms over a cobbler's. The door had been kicked in once, long enough ago to have been mended with a plank. I knocked, and my mother's voice said, "It's open. It is always open. Look where that has got us."

The front room had been put through a mangle. Every chair had been slit down the back and the horsehair dragged out in grey tufts. The carpet was rolled against the wall, the boards underneath lifted in two places. The Sacred Heart lay face-down on the floor. On a chest of drawers someone had stood Stefan Kowal's photograph back upright with its glass cracked across, and in front of it a candle burned in a jam jar.

Bronia Kowal sat at the kitchen table in the wreck of it, in black, with her rimless spectacles and her rosary wound twice round her wrist and her handbag in her lap, held in both hands. She was making tea on a gas ring without looking at it. She looked at me.

"Miss Szymanski," she said.

"Mrs. Kowal."

"They are not Poles," said Bronia. "A Pole would have taken the sugar." She did not smile. "They came on the Tuesday. Then they came on the Thursday. They did not break the plates. This I notice."

My mother stood at the sink with her sleeves rolled and a loaf of Sobczak's rye under her arm like a rifle. She had been scrubbing something, I saw, since eight, and had found nothing she could scrub. "The pot has a chip," she said to me. "She uses the chipped one. I told her."

"It has always been chipped. Stefan's sister gave it."

"Stefan's sister did not know good china."

"Helena Szymanska has not, in twenty years, said a word about my pot that was not an insult."

"This is because the pot is insulting."

It would have been a joke in any other room. Bronia poured, and the three cups went down in front of us on a dish towel, and the steam went up over the cracked glass, and for a minute the two of them looked at each other across the table with the plain hostility of two women who have been at the same coffee urn since the Depression. Then my mother put the bread on the table and sat down and took Bronia's hand, and Bronia let her.

"Teddy," said Bronia, "is a good boy. He is fifty-four. He is not a clever boy. A clever boy would have gone to America and stayed there." She drew the handbag toward her. "Stefan said, if there is trouble, give it to a woman who has never let anything go. I thought of Pani Wach's sister. I thought of the Lasota girl. Then I thought of you, Helena. There is not a grudge in this parish you have not kept."

"I let things go," said my mother. "I let Anton go."

"And then you went to the cemetery every Sunday until Easter." Bronia took the steel clasp of the handbag between finger and thumb. It opened like a small mouth. "This is not a thing for a person who forgives. This is for a person who keeps."

She laid it in my mother's hands.

It was a small book, black leather, no bigger than a man's palm and an inch thick, with a brass clasp that had been rubbed to the colour of weak tea and a spine that had cracked and been mended with thread. A modlitewnik, a prayer book, printed in Kraków in the year before my mother left it. My mother held it in both hands as one holds a baby that is not one's own. Her glasses hung against her chest on their string. She did not open it. She lifted her head and looked at me, and I found that I was looking at the book.

"It will be well," said Bronia. "It will be as God arranges." Her voice stayed even. It was the evenness of a woman who has been carrying a bomb to Mass for sixteen months and has finally put it in a basket. "You will read it, Helena. You have the old hand. Not the girl. She would read it with her head."

"I read with my head," I said.

"I know," said Bronia Kowal. "That is what I fear."

I took the book. My mother's hands let it go, with a small effort, and I saw that she was crying quietly into the neck of her scarf and that neither of the other women had said a word about it.

* * *

On the corner of Eleventh and Hake a man in a long dark coat stood reading yesterday's *Courier* upside down.

I may have been wrong about that. The light was poor, and I'd had two hours of sleep. But a man's hat is a statement, and I couldn't have told you whose it was, and when the Number 14 came rolling up the street I put out my arm and boarded it with a dozen farmers' wives and a boy with a trombone case.

Seven cents. I took the long seat at the back by the window and sat with the book on my lap in both hands, the way Bronia held her bag. We rolled up Front Street. The Sound went by on my left and the hotel went by on my right, eight rows of windows and a Frieze, and the conductor rang his bell at every stop. The man in the long coat did not get on. Nobody who did looked at me twice.

Ferrante had told me what it was. Teddy had told me what it was, in a kitchen, in a sentence that he had shut his mouth on. I had seen it held against a chest at the Gospel. I rode up Front Street to the end of the line with it unopened in my lap, three hundred and eleven names in a stranger's pencil, and I thought: a book you haven't opened is still a prayer book.

At the terminus I got off and waited for the car back. Then I got on again, and sat, and put the book in my coat pocket for the ride down. The streetcar lurched at the switch, and the clasp clicked against Joe Lambert's key.
