# A Brown Bottle

Boots on marble, a good many of them, before the lobby lights were on.

I was standing at the desk with Teddy in the one lamp he allowed himself until seven. By the lobby clock, which runs three minutes slow, it was twenty-five past six, and I had been there eleven minutes, in which Teddy had thanked me three times and squared the blotter on a desk that was square already. I had come to tell him about the flat. The flour, the feathers, Mr. Vaitkus's broom; the oven, and what had been in it. And Bronia, whose own door had been kicked in twice in a week, and who would be glad to hear that a book in a roasting pan had outlasted two pairs of boots.

"Thank God," he had said. Then, in the voice of a man asking for a transfer on a streetcar: "If I may. A small favour. When this is over, you will walk Bronia to Mass on Sunday. Since Stefan she does not like to go alone. She puts her hand in the bag and there is nothing in it to hold."

"You'll walk her yourself."

"That is what I meant to say." The gold tooth caught the lamp.

Then the street door, and the boots. Dolan came in first with a matchstick in his mouth and his hat pulled down, then Karras with his notebook already open, then two uniformed men who stopped inside the door and looked at the fern. Nobody switched on the chandelier. The lobby stayed brown, and the marble took every step and gave it back a little late.

"Morning, Teddy," said Dolan. Not Mr. Wozniak. I watched Teddy hear that. "Ruth. I won't ask."

"Don't."

"We had a call. Ten past six, to Central. A man who wouldn't leave a name. The desk sergeant said he read it off like he was ordering from a card." Dolan's eyes went to the back of the desk. "Look in the night clerk's locker at the Harbor. That's all. It was enough."

"Of course," said Teddy. He stood very straight in his maroon jacket. "It is in the passage behind the key rack. If I may, I have the key." He drew his watch chain out of his waistcoat, and on the chain, beside the little padlock key, hung another, long, brass, with a square head. Karras looked at it.

"And that one, sir?"

"A cupboard."

"I'll take it for now, if you don't mind." Karras said it as gently as I have ever heard a policeman say anything, and Teddy unhooked it himself and put it in his palm.

The passage behind the rack was a bricked slot with a row of grey tin lockers, each with a paper tag. T. WOZNIAK was fourth along. The padlock hung open on its hasp. And there, as Karras reached for it, was Pearl Hightower, who had come in by the staff door at half past six with her key ring held silent against her apron, and who said, to nobody in particular, "That lock is Mr. Wozniak's own. He bought it at Bergen's hardware in 1939. In thirteen years he has not once left it open."

"It was open, ma'am," said Karras.

"Then it was opened with a key. The management has a master for the staff lockers. I have never touched it." She looked at Dolan with the gold spectacles on their chain. "I do not guess, Sergeant. I tell you what I know."

"Everyone in a hotel has a key to everything," said Dolan, not unkindly.

"Not everyone," said Pearl, "has been told."

The locker held a spare maroon jacket on a hanger, a tin of brass polish, an umbrella with a bent rib, a thermos. Behind the thermos, where a man puts a thing he doesn't mean to drink, stood a brown glass bottle with a cork. On the hook below the jacket's collar hung a brass tag on a ring. Karras lifted the tag with a pencil. 807.

"Teddy," said Dolan.

"It is not mine." He said it carefully, like a man setting down a full glass. "I have never seen that bottle. I did not put the key there. I know how it sounds. Forgive me."

Karras drew the cork with a handkerchief, and the smell came out and reached me at two yards: sweet, then sour behind the sweetness, like pear drops left in a hot pocket. Teddy sat down all at once on the bench by the key rack, as if somebody had taken a stool out from under him.

"That's what the lab found in Maher," Dolan said, to nobody. Then he said the rest of it, the words they teach a man, in the voice of one reading out a bill he didn't run up. Tadeusz Wozniak. In connection with the death of Declan Maher. Central Station. Teddy listened with his head a little to one side, politely, as to a guest explaining the hours of breakfast.

"May I take my—" His hand had gone to the waistcoat pocket, the small round tin, the little rattle.

"I'll have to take that, sir," said Karras. "Property." He held up a brown paper bag and opened it, and Teddy took out the pillbox and gave it to him. It went into the bag with the wallet, the watch and the long brass key. "They give it all back at the desk. It'll be listed."

"I would like it listed," said Teddy. "If you will be so kind."

I heard the car before I saw it. The brass arc over the cab door had been dark since I came in; now the needle swung off the eight and came down the dial in a long smooth slide, thirty-eight seconds, and the doors opened on Ambrose Quill in his dark red quilted dressing gown, with his cane over his forearm like an umbrella and his silver hair parted exactly in the middle.

"Sergeant," he said. He cleared his throat, twice. "Forgive the costume. I was told there were policemen in my lobby, and one doesn't dress for policemen."

He did not look at the bag, or at the bottle. He crossed the marble in his red leather slippers, knelt on one knee beside the bench, and put one long yellow-white hand on Teddy's knee. "My dear Teddy. My dear fellow. I will see that you have counsel. You shall not want for it."

"You are very good, sir," said Teddy. His eyes were wet. He was grateful. I watched it happen in his face, and it was the worst thing in the room.

I looked at the hand on the knee, as I do, and at the slippers, with soles as clean as the day they were bought. And I looked at the shoulders of the gown, because they were in the way of everything else, and under the brown lobby light they shone. It's a good quilted silk. It shines wherever it's worn. I had drunk this man's tea and been asked by him, in the voice of a man asking after a lost glove, to find whoever had killed his porter, and a great many men own a dark red gown. I put the thought down. I noticed that it stayed within reach.

* * *

Central Station at eight in the morning smells of burned coffee and other people's coats. I sat on the bench outside the Detective Bureau until Dolan let me in, and when he did, it was to a desk with the brown bottle on it in a paper bag, and Karras in the next room with his door open.

"Don't touch," said Dolan.

"I'm not touching."

He took the bottle out on the blotter. It stood about three inches tall. Eleven fat capsules the colour of weak tea lay in the bottom, and I counted them through the glass. The label was the size of a playing card and had been torn across the lower half, so that what remained read, in a druggist's careful type: *...kin's Pharmacy, Front St. Rx 4187... Oct 28. One capsule at bed...* The patient's name had gone with the tear. The last figure of the number was a small curled stroke that might have been a 2, a 3, or a 9.

"Forty-one eighty-seven," I said, under my breath, as I'd have said an address off the wire. "Forty-one eighty-seven."

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Do what you do." He leaned back. The chain with Moira's ring on it swung out from his collar and back. "It's a number on a piece of paper that I'm going to have to put in a bag. If a woman I used to work with says it under her breath in my office, I'm going to have to remember I heard her."

"It's only a number."

"That's what I'm afraid of." He pushed the bottle back into its bag and the telephone on the desk rang before I could answer. He listened, and I watched his face do what it did when the voice on the other end rose a note at the end of every sentence.

"Mr. Swain," said Dolan. "No. He hasn't asked for one. — He hasn't asked for anything. — I'll tell him. — I'll tell him that too." He covered the mouthpiece with a freckled hand. "The Estate is anxious that its employee should have counsel. Mr. Swain will act himself. At no charge to the clerk."

"He's never exchanged ten words with Teddy in his life."

"That's what I told him." He lifted the receiver again. "He said, would a man who wished the fellow harm offer?"

I did not say that it was the kind of question a man asks who has been given the answer in advance. I said it to myself, with my fingers on my knee, and I noticed there was only one pharmacy on Front Street whose name ended in *kin's*, and that I bought my aspirin there. I did not tell Dolan. Karras was in the next room with his door open and his notebook in his hand, and a number is only a number until someone says it to a policeman.

"Can I see him?"

"Not today," said Dolan.

* * *

The State Board of Licences sits on the fourth floor of the State Building, above the Division of Elevators. As I climbed past the third-floor landing I heard, faint behind a frosted door, the whistle of a very small locomotive going into a tunnel. I went on up.

The hearing room had a long varnished table, a flag, and three men behind the table who looked at their watches the way other men look at a hymnal. Nobody had told me to bring counsel. Nobody had told me anything but the hour: a process server had found me at noon at the Selkirk with a paper and a smell of damp wool. At the end of the table sat a thin woman in a grey suit with a folder, and when the chairman nodded to her she opened it and read the order in a voice as flat as a plate.

"Pursuant to the Act, the licence of Ruth Anne Szymanski, private investigator, of room three-oh-nine, Selkirk Building, is suspended forthwith pending a hearing of the complaint of Gilbert Swain, attorney at law, alleging conduct inconsistent with the licence, to wit..." She read the Shape Hall, the overcoat, the longshoremen, the union officer. She read it without a flicker. If she had been reading the names of the dead I could not have told the difference. "The hearing will be held on Monday, the first of December, at ten o'clock. The licensee may be represented. The licensee will surrender her card."

"I may be represented by a great many people," I said. "Is there a fee for being suspended?"

The chairman looked at his watch. The thin woman held out her hand.

The card came out of my pocket as soft as flannel. It had my name on it and a number, and on the sponsor's line, in a hand like a man pulling a splinter, F. X. Dolan. She put it in her folder and shut the folder, and that was all there was to it. I had been in a building with a Frieze on it and a floor nobody built and a dead man in a cab, and nothing had made me feel as light as that: a card the size of a playing card, gone, and all of me still standing.

* * *

At twenty to five Dolan let me look at Teddy for one minute by the clock, and told me I hadn't been there.

They kept him in the holding room off the Bureau, a square of green wall with a bench and a barred door. He was sitting on the bench with his hands on his knees and his back straight, a small man in a waistcoat with no tie and no laces in his shoes, and he looked like someone waiting to be called in to be fitted for a suit. His eyes were calm. His moth-wing eyebrows had been combed.

"Miss Szymanski." He did not get up. "Do not worry about me. Worry about Bronia."

I stood with my hands round two of the bars. "Who told you to say that?"

He shook his head. It was the gentlest refusal I ever had. His right hand went to the breast pocket of his waistcoat, found nothing there, and lay down again on his knee.

"Time," said Dolan, behind me.

I went out onto the Central Station steps in the last of the light. It had not rained all day, a grey, dry, bitter day with a lid on it, and the lamps along Cutter Street were coming on one by one. A black Packard stood at the kerb by the side gate with its rear curtains drawn. As I looked it pulled out, softly, without hurry, and went down the hill. I saw only the dark of the back seat and, for the length of a lamp post, a pale oval at the window that might have been a face or might have been a folded newspaper. It was Swain's Packard, I would have sworn to the grille. I could not have said who rode in the back.

I went round to the side gate and stood at the iron railing. Beyond it lay the yard that joined the Bureau to the cell block, forty yards of wet brick under one arc lamp. Two men came out of a door at the far end, and between them walked a small stooped man without a tie, and I watched him go across the yard in the white light, his unlaced shoes slipping at the heels, and halfway across he stopped, and patted his breast pocket, and patted it again, where the pillbox used to be.
