# What the Certificate Says

A toy locomotive came out of a mountain of inspection files, drew four freight cars and a caboose along the edge of the desk blotter, and stopped with a small electric click at the foot of the telephone.

"The Overland Limited," said Hubert Pomeroy, without looking up from a transformer the size of a bread box. "Due in Walla Walla at ten past nine. You'll forgive me, Miss Szymanski. If I take my hand off the throttle she goes over the edge into the Division's cabinet, and the cabinet holds the boilers for 1939."

The State Division of Elevators occupied the third floor of the State Building behind a door lettered in gilt, and Mr. Pomeroy occupied most of the Division. He was a stout man of fifty-eight with a pink scalp, a watch chain that ended in a small brass elevator car, and the hands of someone who handles delicate machinery and cannot be trusted with a teacup. His office had no floor to speak of. A sheet of plywood the size of a door lay across two filing cabinets and a pair of sawhorses and carried a layout: a balsa station marked WALLA WALLA, a water tower, three tiny trees, a man the size of a clothes-peg waving a red lantern at nothing, and a tunnel built out of stacked State inspection folders, Form E-12, through which the Overland went in and out of the passenger elevators of Port Calder like a thought.

I'd come up in the State Building's own car. Its certificate said *Landings served: 6* and the panel had six buttons. I counted them twice. It was a habit that had been getting worse for about nine days.

"I'm here about certificate 4471," I said.

"I have no certificate 4471." He said it to the transformer, and a good deal too fast. "That is to say I have every certificate in the district, in a manner of speaking, so it's difficult to be certain which — mind the signal, you're standing on Pendleton. Sit. No, not there, that's the siding. There."

"The Harbor Hotel. Front Street and Ferry Slip Row. An Otis of 1926, converted in '41. You signed it last March."

The locomotive did a complete lap of the blotter. Then Hubert Pomeroy sat down on a stool with a train under his elbow and put both pink hands flat on the plywood, and the little man with the lantern shook.

"Joseph Lambert," he said. "I read it in the *Beacon.* Page nine, under a notice about a spaniel. 'Porter Dies in Fall.' I had shaken that man's hand every March since 1938, Miss Szymanski. He gave me a butterscotch. He hummed a hymn the whole way up, and I can't hear that hymn now without wanting to examine a governor rope." He swallowed. "Mrs. Pomeroy says I have not eaten since Wednesday. She says it as if I were doing it to annoy her."

He got up, found a green cloth register on the shelf behind the Overland's water tower, and opened it to the H's with a hand that took two goes. *Passenger Elevators, District 4.* The Harbor Hotel stood at the top of a page in a careful engineer's script. Under it ran the years, one line apiece, from 1938 to this March, and on every line the same two figures in the column headed *Landings.*

"Nine," I said.

"Nine landings, eight floors open to the public. The Division counts landings. The Building Department counts floors." He lifted his hands in a gesture of the purest helplessness. "We do not read each other's mail. It's a principle of good government. If the two ever met in a drawer, Miss Szymanski, there would be an inquiry, and an inquiry is a thing that happens to men with train sets."

"Did you go to nine?"

"I rode to it. Every March." He answered the question the way a man steps onto ice, with a foot at a time. "Joe turned a key and held the button for a count. We went on past the eighth for what I should call six seconds. The door opened on a very dark corridor and a very clean floor. I tested the door interlock, the buffer, the governor, and the gate, and I signed. I did not step out. I'm not a man who steps out. I am hired to look at ropes, and what is at the other end of a rope is the owner's affair."

"You never asked what was there."

"Once. In 1939. Joe said it was machinery. A gentleman on the Estate's staff said it was tanks. A week later, a ham arrived." He closed his eyes. "Every December since. An eleven-pound Virginia ham with a card, *with the compliments of the Trustee,* and Mrs. Pomeroy glazes it with cloves. I do not say it is a bribe. A bribe is something you can be accused of. A ham is a courtesy."

"That's a long run of courtesy."

"She makes a very good glaze." He wiped his lip with his thumb. "Miss Szymanski, a man dies falling off a ladder he has climbed for twenty-two years. I wake at three and see the rails. I do not sleep. I know very well what I've been counting." The Overland, which had been idling, he nudged into motion again with one finger, and watched it go as another man might watch a lit window across a street. "When I am frightened I run the Overland. She gets to Walla Walla every time."

"I need you to say it to a judge."

"If my name is not in the papers."

"A court keeps a record, Mr. Pomeroy."

"Then in a very small court." He found a pencil and discovered he didn't need it. "Give me ten minutes."

It took him eleven, at a typewriter older than mine with two fingers and a rising colour, and at the end of it I held a single sheet on Division letterhead, certified a true extract from the register, signed *H. Pomeroy, Inspector,* and stamped with a seal. He breathed on the stamp afterward, as though it might hatch.

* * *

I telephoned Central Station from a booth in the State Building lobby. Dolan was out; Karras took the message in the voice of a young man who has been told to write down everything, and said he would give it to the Sergeant personally. The message was *the arithmetic.* At twenty past twelve a black-and-white Ford drew up at the kerb on Front Street in the rain, opposite the Harbor, and Dolan climbed out of the passenger side with his collar up and a matchstick in the corner of his mouth.

"The arithmetic," he said.

"Read this."

He read it under the lamp-post, holding it over his hat to keep the wet off, and then he read it again, slowly, the way a man reads a menu in a foreign language. A flat brown evidence envelope stood a corner out of his breast pocket; I knew the shape as I know a Christmas card. When he put the page in beside it he touched the two of them, twice, like a man checking for his wallet.

"A paperwork matter," he said.

"Count it, Frank."

"Count what?"

"The hotel. From the kerb. Count the rows of windows, and when you've done, count it again and include the cake."

"The what?"

"The cornice. Fourteen feet of terracotta waves and fish. Count the fish."

He took the matchstick out of his mouth. He turned his head from left to right in the rain, from the lobby row up, one, two, the way a man goes up a flight of stairs in the dark, with his lips moving. I stood beside him. I'd stood at that exact spot nine days before and arrived at nine on my second try. He got to eight. He stopped at the line of louvres under the terracotta and looked at it for the length of a long breath. Then his eyes went up and across the whole fourteen feet of the Frieze, all the dolphins and rippling waves, and I heard him say *nine* as softly as a man saying a name in church. He took off his hat.

The rain fell on his thin sandy hair. Karras, in the car, lowered his window an inch.

"Twenty-two years," said Dolan. "I've been drunk under that building, and sober under it, and in a wedding party under it, and I would have sworn to eight in front of a judge."

"You'd have been right about the windows."

"I've been walking under a fish for twenty years." He put the hat back on. "All right. Let's go and ask the man."

We went in. Dorrie Nash was behind the brass rail with a black ribbon pinned to her collar and her gum stopped in her cheek, and she said *good afternoon* to us both in the voice of someone who had been told to. Before we had crossed the marble, the door of the office behind the desk opened, as though the room had been waiting for him to come out of it, and Ambrose Quill stepped through with the cane over his left forearm and his silver hair parted as exactly as ever.

He was in the charcoal suit and the high collar. He came toward us, and halfway across the lobby he put the cane down on the marble and tapped it, once, softly, with a rubber tip that made no sound at all but a kind of apology. He had not tapped it for me.

"Sergeant Dolan. Miss Szymanski. How kind." He cleared his throat twice. "You will take tea."

"No, thank you, sir. I want to see the ninth floor."

"The ninth floor," said Quill, and his face rearranged itself with the mildest sadness. "Ah. Miss Szymanski has been telling you of the Mezzanine. I feared I had been indiscreet, Sergeant, in a moment of grief, with a young woman who has been a good deal tried this week. It is a tank loft. The State is so good as to call it a landing, since the car goes up to it for service, and the Certificate says so in a manner that I have always thought rather fanciful. The licence says eight floors open to the public, and that is the fact, because the public has never in its history been admitted. I gave a pet name to a machinery space to a lady who was distressed, and I regret that she took me for a man with a secret."

"I'd like to see it," said Dolan.

"Naturally. Come, and see the whole of what the car does."

We rode up, the three of us, Quill pressing the 8 with the silver collar of his cane. The cab smelled of floor polish and warm brass. I stood nearest the panel, and as the car began to climb, I put out my thumb and found the plug.

It was polished. It had been cream; now it was brass, bright, clean as a new button, and it sat dry in its hole. I lifted the edge with my nail, and it came up the way a cork comes out of a bottle that has been uncorked before. Under it, flush with the panel, shone a second disc of brass, solid, filed smooth, with a few fine scratches round the rim where the file had slipped. There was no keyway. There was nothing for a key to go into at all.

"Mr. Quill?" said Dolan.

"The Otis people sealed it on Thursday morning, Sergeant." Quill spoke gently, to the Certificate on the wall. "After poor Joe, I could not sleep for thinking of a stranger with a key in a moving car. I rang the depot at eight, and a man came at nine. And since Joe's own key has not been found," he turned his pale eyes on me for exactly as long as courtesy allows and perhaps a second beyond it, "I do hope it is in kind hands."

Thursday morning. The car had gone up at one o'clock, with me in it, and by nine someone had filled the hole with brass and filed it.

I said nothing. The needle swung to the eight, a hair short of the mark, and the car stopped. There was no stir beyond it. Dolan stood in the corridor of the eighth floor in his wet hat, looking at a wall of oxblood carpet, a row of doors, a window onto the light well, and a cream door at the end lettered LINEN. He knocked at the nearest wall with his knuckle, once, and listened to it, and I watched him realise that listening to a wall tells you about the wall.

When we came down, Gilbert Swain was standing on the lobby marble with his hat in his hand.

I have never learned where he comes from. The cuffs were anchors, the hair oil was fresh, and the bay rum arrived about two seconds before he did. "Sergeant!" He had the smile of a man delighted to find that nobody has been hurt. "Would the Department inspect a bank without papers? Would it examine the Governor's mansion on the word of a lady with a ledger? Any examination of these premises will be on a warrant, signed by a judge, and the Estate will cooperate in every particular. Naturally."

"Gilbert," said Quill, "the Sergeant is our guest."

"The Sergeant is a public officer, Mr. Quill, and the Estate has insurers." Swain's eyes found mine and slid off like a coin off a wet table. "Would we want it said the Estate had permitted an unauthorised search? Would anyone?"

Quill spread his long yellow-white hands, a man overruled by his lawyer, which is the natural order, and he looked at Dolan with the look of a vicar who has been caught in a draught. "You see how it is, Sergeant. I am only an auditor."

Dolan said, "Thank you for the tea I didn't have, Mr. Quill," and walked out into the rain, and I went after him.

He stopped under the awning at the kerb with his back to the hotel doors. Karras had put the window up again.

"I need a judge, Ruth," he said, low. "I'd need a judge if I had a floor. And all I've got is a ledger page that says a hotel has an extra landing, which is a paperwork matter, and a trespass you can't admit to."

I kept my face where it was. It wasn't enough, not for a man who had read it across a switchboard for eight years. "I haven't said I was anywhere."

"You didn't have to. You've been looking at that brass like it owed you money." The matchstick went across. "The Deputy Chief retires tomorrow. Rafferty wants quiet till he's got his clock. Give me forty-eight hours. I'll try to get him to sign a request. I'll try. I'm not promising a thing, and you're not to touch that lift."

"What lift?"

"That one." He nodded across the street. "Tomorrow night they're feeding him at the Harbor. The Pacific Room, eight o'clock, chicken. I've got a spare seat, and I'd rather it was yours than the Deputy Chief's brother-in-law's. Wear a dress."

"Frank."

"I know." He turned up his collar. "I know."

He gave me nothing else. He got into the Ford, and Karras pulled out into Front Street, and I stood under the awning with my hands in my pockets and Joe Lambert's key lying in the left one, a bit of brass that fitted nothing.

* * *

At six I walked to Tommy's. The pie clock on the wall ran twenty minutes fast, as it had run all week, and the counterman had a ladle in each hand and no expression at all. I took the stool at the window, ordered a coffee, and watched the hotel across Front Street through my own reflection.

The windows went up, row on row, lit and dark and lit, and above the eighth row the Frieze stood in the lamplight with its waves and its fish. For a while there was nothing. Then, at the far end of the cornice, behind a louvre, a small yellow light came on and moved left to right along the black band, and stopped, and moved again.

It was a lantern. Someone had gone up by a stair I now knew the number of and was walking the Calder Club with a light, looking for a piece of paper that did not walk off.

Across the street, at the kerb, a black-and-white Ford sat with its engine off. Karras was in it. He had the notebook on his knee, closed, with the rubber band round it, the way a man sits when he has seen a thing and does not yet know how to spell it.

In the cab of the lift on the other side of that wall, I thought, a coin of new brass was shining where there had been a hole. The coffee went cold in front of me. I counted the fish on the Frieze, and then, to be sure, I counted the light.
