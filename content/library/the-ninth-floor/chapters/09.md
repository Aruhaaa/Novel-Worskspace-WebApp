# High Mass

The bell was still going when my mother took me by the sleeve and towed me up the nave of St. Casimir's like a tug with a barge, and the smell that came with us was wet wool, three hundred coats of it, steaming under the candles.

"Do not look at the people," Helena said, in the voice she keeps for churches and customs officers, a whisper with a bayonet in it. "They have seen you. They are waiting to see if you will look."

"Mama, I'm thirty-six."

"Then you will know how." She hauled me left into the fourth pew, the Szymanski pew since 1912, with a worn place at the aisle end where my father's elbow used to go, and she genuflected on one knee with a creak like a barn door.

St. Casimir's is brick outside and plaster Gothic within, with a ceiling of gold stars gone brown at the edges where the damp has found them, and over the side altar a Madonna with two scars on her cheek, who has been looking at the Flats for forty years with the patient contempt of a woman who has seen the bills. The choir sang the Kyrie from the loft in six parts, five of them sharp. The incense went up. My mother, beside me, put her lips to her missal and her eyes to the back of Mrs. Dobek's hat.

Father Dziedzic climbed into the pulpit by eleven steps and spread his hands on the rail. He was a round, weary man with a pink face and grey under the eyes, the sort of priest you would pick out of a line for how little he wanted to be one that morning. He preached in Polish for the old and in English for the young, in that order, and in both he preached on the stranger within your gates. Thou shalt not oppress him, for you know the heart of a stranger, seeing you also were strangers in the land of Egypt. In Polish it rolled out whole, and my mother nodded at every comma. In English it came out smaller. He said a stranger might knock at any door at any hour, and that a Christian did not ask what it would cost before he opened.

His eyes travelled the church the way a priest's do, row by row, and reached the fourth pew and stopped on me. I watched him find me and look at the red lamp over the altar instead.

Across the aisle, in the pew where I would have bet the Kowals had sat since the church had a roof, a small woman in black held a handbag on her lap with both hands. Rimless spectacles, a black felt hat with a pin, a mother-of-pearl rosary wound twice round one wrist like a bracelet. I had a photograph of her in my head from Teddy's table, and she was smaller than the photograph. At the Gospel the congregation stood with a sound like surf, and she undid the steel clasp of the bag, which I heard from across the aisle as a click like a knitting needle, and took out a small black book with a brass clasp of its own. She held it shut against her chest through the whole of St. Matthew. She didn't read from it. Holding it was the prayer. The clasp had been rubbed by thumbs until one side of the brass had gone the colour of a new penny.

I found Teddy by the rattle. In the hush after the Elevation, when the little bell is rung and nobody breathes, a pillbox shook in the last pew like a mouse in a biscuit tin, and I knew without turning round who sat there by the door with his hat on his knee, rattling in time with the Latin.

* * *

The basement of St. Casimir's is a long low room under the church, with green wainscot, steam pipes, and eleven tables covered in oilcloth. By ten past eleven it held the entire parish and most of the Flats' opinions. There was plum cake in slabs, and an urn the size of a boiler tended by a committee of four women, and at the table nearest the stairs three of them were arguing about Mrs. Eisenhower's bangs: whether a woman who is going to be First Lady is entitled to bangs, and whether it was vanity or only Iowa.

My mother handed me a plate of cake as though she were arming me and went to the urn. Bronia Kowal stood at it with a cup in each hand, pouring for nobody. The two of them have been rivals at that urn for twenty years. It is the only war I know of in which both sides have always won.

"Pani Kowal," said Helena.

"Pani Szymańska."

"The coffee is thin."

"It is the same coffee as last Sunday."

"Then last Sunday was thin also." My mother took a cup out of Bronia's hand and drank from it, in the manner of an inspector, and found nothing to say, which she took as a victory.

Bronia turned to me. Close to, she was sixty-two and looked older in the face and younger in the hands, which were small and square and quite steady. She had the handbag hooked over her left forearm and held to her ribs with the elbow.

"You are the daughter who is a detective," she said.

"Ruth. Mrs. Kowal, I'm sorry about your husband."

"Seventeen months. It is kind." She said it as though kindness were a coin she had been handed in the wrong currency. "Teddy is a good boy. He is fifty-four and a good boy. Leave him be."

"He came to me, Mrs. Kowal."

"He came to you because he is a good boy and did not know where else to go. Now he knows. Leave him be."

I looked at the handbag. She saw me look, and moved it to the other arm, as you shift a child that has been stared at too long, and she said "Excuse me, Pani Szymańska, the milk," and went away down the room between the tables, small and black and straight, and I watched her all the way to the kitchen door.

Father Dziedzic had come down the stairs in his cassock, still blotting his forehead, with a plate of cake in his hand that he did not mean to eat. I caught him by the sugar bowl.

"Father. The box in front of St. Raphael, the oak one with the iron bands. What is it collected for?"

"Travellers' Masses," he said at once; too at once, like a thing rehearsed in a confessional mirror. "A few dollars. For the sailors."

"Which travellers?"

He put the cake down. "Stefan Kowal, God rest him, did many good works, Miss Szymanski. He emptied that box every Friday, and what he did with it is between him and his Maker, and I trust his Maker has settled the account. Excuse me. Mrs. Dobek has a grandson to baptise and a daughter-in-law to forgive." He took his handkerchief and left the cake, and went off up the room at the speed of a round man in a cassock who has remembered an emergency.

* * *

My mother makes a pork roast on Sundays the way other people make a point. We ate in the kitchen at 1127, at the table with the good cloth, with the coal range ticking and the window over the sink turned grey with rain, and she talked about Mrs. Dobek's cinnamon (too much), the tenor (he sings like a man looking for his hat), and the price of lard. Then, in the voice she uses to bring up a subject she has been carrying for hours, she said, "In forty-nine, Rutka, there was talk."

"What talk?"

"In the parish. Women's talk, over the urn. That there was a house for newcomers. For people who came off the ships with nothing. One woman said, I took a loaf, and another said, I took blankets, and the first one said, to where? And nobody said where." She cut a potato in half to look at the inside of it. "Nobody said where and nobody asked. That was the sense of it. A quiet house. The kind where you carry a loaf to the door and not past it."

I put it down beside the other things I'd been carrying about since Thursday: the light sliding along the black band over the hotel, and the snapshot in Yvette Marchand's mirror, and a rail of coat hooks at the height of my knee. They lay down together very comfortably. They had been waiting for each other.

"Mama. Was there a house?"

My mother rose to see to the gravy. The gravy needed nothing. It sat in its boat in the middle of the table where it had been the whole meal, and she carried it to the range anyway and stood over it with the ladle and her back to me. Below us the Lithuanian cobbler had begun to sing, a long sad thing in a language of vowels.

"Mama."

"The gravy is cold," Helena said.

It was not cold. I could see the skin on it. I have watched my mother answer everything: the price of lard, the character of Roosevelt, the sins of the only young man I ever brought home. It was the first time in thirty-six years that I had watched her not answer. She stirred until the gravy was warm, and then she stirred some more.

My mother takes a walk round the block after Sunday dinner in all weathers, on the theory that pork not walked off turns to sin. We went under her umbrella, which she holds like a mast, down Hake Street to Eleventh and back past the church and the bakery, and she talked the whole way about coal and said nothing at all.

Someone was waiting in the doorway of 1127. He stood in the lee of the lintel with his hat in both hands and a furled black umbrella hung over his arm that he had never put up. The rain had got through his overcoat at the shoulders. How long he had been there I couldn't tell, but his shoes had made two dark prints on the step, and the prints had begun to fill.

"Pan Wozniak," said my mother. "You will come up and have pork."

"Thank you, madam. No." He did not look at her. "A word with your daughter, if I may."

Helena looked at him, and then at me, and went past him and up the stairs with the umbrella, and her back said everything her mouth had decided to keep. I heard her door. I heard the third stair, which sings.

Teddy hung the umbrella on the iron rail of the stoop to free his hands. He squared his cuffs, one and then the other, though they were already square.

"Leave Bronia out," he said.

"Out of what, Mr. Wozniak?"

He shook his head. The white eyebrows were beaded with rain. "She is sixty-two. She has lost a husband. If you go to her again I will... " He did not finish it. "Please. I ask it as a man who asks very little."

"Tell me what I'm leaving her out of."

He looked at me then, the gold tooth showing a little as his mouth worked, and I thought for a second that he would tell me. But he said "Forgive me," as he says it for everything, and went down the step and out into Hake Street.

He left the umbrella on the rail. I watched him walk, small and stooped and bareheaded, with the wet already dark across his back, and three paces on his right hand went into his coat pocket and stayed there. I could not hear it from the stoop. I heard it anyway: the small dry rattle of the tin.
