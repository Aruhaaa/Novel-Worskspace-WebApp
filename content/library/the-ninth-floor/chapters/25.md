# Thanksgiving Eve

Dolan ate my mother's eggs the way he did everything that morning, as if someone might come for the plate.

Three of them, fried in butter until the whites went to lace, and a heel of rye that he tore instead of cutting. Between the first forkful and the last he said nothing at all. It was twenty to seven on Wednesday the twenty-sixth, and rain ran down the kitchen window in the grey that passes for dawn on Hake Street in November. At his elbow, on a clean dish towel, since Helena allows no paper on an oilcloth where there are eggs, lay a manifest in the thin grey type of an Antwerp shipping agent, a half-sheet watermarked with three letters, and a note from a dead man. He had read them standing, twice. Then he had held the half-sheet up to the window, moving his lips over the name at the foot, and sat down and asked if there was anything to eat. It is what a policeman does when he has been handed something he is going to have to carry.

The linen van had come at ten to six, a Dodge with HARBOR HOTEL on the door in gilt, and a driver who passed my mother a slip and a canvas sack as long as a child and left without waiting to be thanked. She signed for twelve damask. She counted twelve damask. She also found, in the middle of them, a Manila envelope rolled in a pillowcase, which she laid on the table without a word before she went back to counting. By the time Dolan's Plymouth stopped below, three of the cloths were in the washtub and she was saying things about the Harbor's gravy in two languages.

He wiped his mouth on the back of his hand and, at a glance from my mother, on the napkin by his plate. "A housekeeper put evidence in the wash," he said to the empty plate. "Barnwell is going to enjoy that. Mrs. Szymanska, I ask your pardon in advance for what I'm about to say."

"Say it," said my mother, without turning from the tub. "In this house a man may swear in any language he has. God has a good ear and a long list."

Dolan swore. He did it quietly, in Irish, which he does not otherwise speak, and it went on about as long as a decade of the rosary. My mother nodded at the end of it, as she does at a sound piece of pastry.

Then he took the evidence envelope from his inside pocket, where it had lived against his ribs for a fortnight, and slid out the slip from Maher's hatband. NINE. HALF PAST MIDNIGHT. BRING IT. He laid it on the towel beside the receipt. One was a clerk's block capitals and the other a gentleman's copperplate, tall and upright as fence posts, but the ink was the same rust brown in both, the colour of a thing that had once been green.

"Ink like old blood," said Dolan.

"You can say that to the Judge."

"I'll say it to Barnwell, and he'll say it to the Judge in better words." He turned the half-sheet over to the blank back. "Why would a man sign it? Thirty-one people, his own name, his own ink. Four years in a drawer."

"Because he's an auditor." I had been awake twenty-six hours and the answer came out whole, the way they do at that stage, like a tooth. "He can steal anything he likes, Frank. He can't leave a receipt unsigned."

On the oilcloth he laid a brown envelope. "Karras developed your roll last night in the station darkroom. Thirty-one lines in a hand like a row of pins, and across the foot, Paid from Pier 6 office, order of the Estate."

* * *

The District Attorney keeps his office on the second floor of the County Building, at the top of Lamplighter Hill, down a corridor of green linoleum that smells of floor wax and other people's trouble. Dolan's Plymouth heats on the driver's side only, so I arrived warm on one side and damp on the other.

In the outer office a woman of forty in a green cardigan was typing with the pleasant fixed face of someone who has been asked to look as though she isn't listening. The plate on her desk said MISS ALDOUS. I had never seen the woman who ate chops with Gilbert Swain at half past one on the morning of the fifth, and I had imagined someone more expensive.

Hollis Barnwell was fifty-two, long and grey and very clean, with heavy lids and a way of turning a page by its corner as though the paper might be hot. His hat sat crown-down on the blotter, so that nothing could fall out of it. He had never lost a case. Port Calder said so with respect, and the Detective Bureau said it with a certain flatness, because everyone knew the reason: he had never taken one that could be lost.

He read for eleven minutes by the clock over the door. He took a glass from his drawer and looked at the signature as a jeweller looks at a flaw, and at Maher's note, and at the watermark against the window, and then he set the glass down square to the blotter and folded his hands on it.

"If this were mine," he said, "I should want to know three things. Forgive me, Miss Szymanski. I understand you are not at present licensed, so I will think of you as a private citizen, which is the more reliable witness. How the paper came to be where it was. How it came to be here. And what, beyond a signature, it proves."

I told him: Maher, the Monday, the cab, the frame, Pearl, the sack.

"A private citizen removed a document from an elevator at three in the morning, with the help of an employee, and it spent three hours in a laundry sack."

"In my mother's kitchen."

"Which is better custody than I have seen the police give." He did not smile, but his eyes took a short rest. "I shall ask the court not to ask. As to what it proves." He laid a finger on the half-sheet. "A Trustee signed for thirty-one cases of personal effects landed at Pier 6 on a night when Customs counted forty crew. That is a receipt. It is not a crime. The crime is what the cases were, and the only persons who can say what they were are persons I understand you will not name." His finger moved to Maher's note. "The others."

"I can't name them."

"Then I shall not ask. A prosecutor who has not asked cannot be made to tell." He folded his hands again. "I have a very short memory for what I am not told. It is my only vice."

Dolan chewed the unlit matchstick. "And Quill?"

"Against Mr. Quill I have a signature, a pharmacist's ledger, an old lady in Santa Barbara who is seventy-eight and kind, and a sedative that half the old gentlemen in the county take for their nights. Against that I have a confession from a man who is dead and so cannot recant." His voice did not rise. It never did; it only grew more exact. "The Beacon has told this city for four days that Wozniak did it. The defendant is sixty-three, with a cane, and has forty old retainers who will weep on the stand and say that he gave them each a ham."

"He knows the frame's empty," I said. "He looked at it at four this morning."

"Then he has been asked a question, and I expect he will answer it politely." Barnwell drew a pad toward him. "What I want is the Estate's books. The receipt ledger that stands behind that half-sheet, in his hand, every item received since 1946. With the ledger this paper is a count. Without it, a scrap. A warrant for the Estate's records at the Calder Building and at the hotel: room 806, and whatever there is above the eighth floor. Judge Pardoe sits until noon, and after noon he keeps the holiday with his sister at Stannard's Ferry. I will have him hear it at his house at four, over coffee, and he will sign if the coffee is good."

"Has the sister got a telephone?" said Dolan.

"I believe she regards it as a vulgarity."

"It's Thanksgiving," Dolan told the ceiling. "Everything in this town is somebody's sister's."

"Until the paper is signed," said Barnwell, "nobody enters that hotel. Nobody asks a bellboy the time." He looked at me. "You have a question."

"If he burns it."

"The ledger?" He considered it with real courtesy. "Miss Szymanski, he is an accountant. A man like that does not burn his books. He files them."

It was the soundest reasoning I heard that day, and I have never entirely forgiven it.

We went out through the outer office. Miss Aldous had the telephone at her ear and her back to us, the cord wound twice round her forefinger. "—not till four," she was saying, in the pleasant voice of a woman declining a second helping. "I told you, Gil, nothing is—" She heard the door and turned the rest of it into a cough.

On the stairs I said, "Gil."

"Half the County Building telephones Swain," said Dolan. "He represents the half that isn't in jail." He put on his brown hat with both hands, which is how he does it when he is angry. "I'll tell Barnwell. He'll move her to the files."

"By Friday," I said, and we went down the rest of the stairs without another word, counting different things.

* * *

I slept in my clothes on top of the bedspread until two, when my mother put a bowl of soup on the chair and a hand on my foot. By half past four I had the Waltham wound and my hat on. She had hung the last of the damask from lines across the kitchen ceiling, so that twelve white cloths dripped into twelve pots like the flags of small countries that had given up. The book was in the oven under the roasting pan, where it lived now.

"You are going to that hotel," said my mother.

"To Tommy's. It's across the street from it."

"Rutka. A street is a very small river." She wound a grey wool scarf round my neck and tied it in the knot she keeps for children and parcels, and I let her. "Come back dry."

Tommy had a card in the window, CLOSED THURS., and eleven pumpkin and six mince chalked on the slate. I took the stool by the window and wiped a porthole in the steam with my sleeve.

The fog came up off the Sound at a quarter past five, the way water comes into a cellar: first at the foot of the street, then in the middle, then everywhere. At twenty to six the Heifer started up at Calder Point, two long low notes, and I counted forty and heard it again. Across the road the Harbor stood with its eight rows of windows lit, and above them the Frieze was a dark band with nothing in it but fog moving along, slowly, the way a hand goes across a slate.

At twenty past six a black Packard slid out of the fog and stopped at the hotel's door.

Gilbert Swain got out in a camel coat with a leather bag held in both arms against his stomach, wearing the face of a man who has been handed a baby to carry on a train. He looked up Front Street, and down, and across at the steam on Tommy's window, and went in. Through the lobby glass I watched him set the bag on the desk before Dorrie Nash and her black ribbon, say something with his chin up, and dab his lip with a handkerchief. Forty seconds, by the Heifer. Then the Packard was gone, and the bag sat on the marble with nobody's hand on it. A lawyer with a secretary, a cold and a car, carrying his own bag. In my experience a man who carries his own bag has told no one what is in it.

At a quarter to seven I saw the light.

It was no more than a thread, low and yellow, in the black band above the eighth floor, behind a louvre near the Ferry Slip end. It moved a few feet toward the west and stopped. Fog came across it and the thread went on burning through the fog, a little duller, like a coal under ash.

I set a nickel on the saucer by the wall telephone and Tommy turned his back, which is as much privacy as a lunch counter has. Dolan came on at the Bureau in the voice of a man who had been kept by a telephone for an hour and had not liked it.

"He's on nine," I said. "Swain brought a bag at twenty past six. At a quarter to seven there's a light in the Frieze."

"The warrant's not signed." He said it fast, like a bad score read out. "Pardoe left for his sister's with the coffee unfinished, and Barnwell's gone after him in his own car with the papers. We'll be there at seven-thirty. Karras and me and two in uniform. You hear me, Ruth? Seven-thirty."

"Say again."

"Seven-thirty. Don't you—"

I hung up on the rest of it. In the wire room you leave a time with someone before a man goes into a building, and if he hasn't called by then, you send. It is the first rule they teach and the only one that ever saved anybody.

I put in a second nickel and gave the operator Anchor Alley. "Fong and Son," said Walter Fong, the way a man reads a name off a stone. I told him to tell Lucille the kitchen yard of the Harbor Hotel, on Ferry Slip Row, at eight o'clock, and to bring a coat.

There was a pause just long enough for a man to look at the ceiling. "Whose coat?"

"Any coat."

"There is no such thing as any coat," said Walter Fong. "I will see to it."

* * *

The kitchen door on Ferry Slip Row stood open on steam. Twenty-two turkeys lay in rows on the long steel table, trussed and pale, like longshoremen laid out for a union meeting, and a cook with a face like a boiled ham was stuffing them at a speed that meant he had somewhere to be. Nobody looked at me. A woman in a hat walking through a hotel kitchen on the eve of a holiday is a delivery.

Pearl Hightower stood by the service stair in a white apron over her black dress, the ring of forty keys in her fist, silent as a stone. She spoke first.

"You have come by the front. The front is the wrong door."

"I came by the side. Where is he?"

"I do not guess." The gold spectacles caught the steam and let it go. "Mr. Quill came out of his office in a dinner jacket, which he does not wear on a Wednesday, and took Mr. Swain's bag himself. From the pantry he took the five-pint can we fill the sconces from in the Pacific Room when the current fails. At twenty to seven he went up in the service lift with the bag in one hand and the can in the other." She breathed once, through her nose. "The bag was not shut. I saw the corners. Green cloth, six or seven, with leather spines."

"The linen cupboard?"

"The padlock hangs open on its hasp. The back of the shelves is not fastened from this side. It never has been."

I knew then, and was ashamed how long it had taken me. A man who wants a ledger burned has a grate in 806. He does not carry lamp oil up fourteen steps for a ledger. He carries it for a floor.

"Ring your bell," I said.

"I will ring it when I smell it, not before. A bell rung for nothing is the end of a housekeeper. I have twenty-six guests, three of them old ladies who will not leave a room without their teeth." She did not move. "The sergeant?"

"Half past seven."

"The kitchen door will be kept open for the sergeant."

I thought of the .32 in the bottom drawer of my desk on Cutter Street, eight minutes off and eight back and a lock to argue with, and of the fact that I had never in my life carried it. Then I put my hand into Pearl's apron pocket and took out a flashlight. She let me. It was nickel-plated, with a dent in the barrel. "The batteries are new," she said. "I put them in on the fourteenth." It took me until the first landing to understand that she had been keeping it twelve days for somebody.

The staff stair is a hundred and thirty-eight steps from the kitchen landing to eight. I counted them to keep my feet in order, the way a man whistles on a dark road. The eighth-floor corridor lay empty and dim, a dance band thin behind somebody's door and a smell of somebody's pork chop. At the east end the linen cupboard stood open, the padlock on its hasp new enough still to shine. I put my palm against the middle of the back of the shelves, and it moved, inward, on a hinge somebody had oiled, like a heavy book opening.

Beyond it was dark, and a smell came down out of the dark, sweet and flat, like a garage on a hot day.

Fourteen steps, Teddy had said, and he had said to count. I put the light on the first.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. On the sixth the tread gave under my shoe with a small dry crack, like a knuckle, as it had given for Teddy, and for Pearl, and for two men carrying a third; I had known it was there for thirteen days, and I stood on it anyway, because I was counting, and counting is something the feet do.

"Six," I said, out loud, to nobody, and nobody answered, and I went on up.
