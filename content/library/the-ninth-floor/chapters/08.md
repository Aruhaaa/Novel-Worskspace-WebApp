# A Name Like Marchand

Somebody in the trumpet section dropped a mute, and it went rolling across the stage of the Cormorant Room in a wobbling circle, a small aluminium cone ringing on the boards, and every head in the house turned to watch it except the singer's. She stood at the microphone with her hands folded at her waist. A spotlight, hunted for in the wings by a boy who had been told to find it and found it late, came up on her blue and stayed, and the first thing it showed was her gloves. They were white cotton, wrist-length, spotless. They did not so much as twitch.

That was how I first saw Yvette Marchand, from the doorway, with half the contents of the Harbor's kitchen bin drying on my coat.

It was twenty-five past eleven. I had walked up Brine Street from the hotel in the three minutes the map allows, and in the rain they had felt like nine. The Cormorant Room had been a ship chandler's once and had kept the bones: brick walls, iron beams hung with brass lamps that had lit the holds of ships, fishing net swagged along the ceiling with cork floats caught in it, a ship's wheel behind the bar. Over the bottles a stuffed cormorant spread its wings, one glass eye gone and a pearl-headed hatpin in its place. The house was full of sailors, dock clerks, a table of dentists in town for a convention, and couples who had come to be seen and stayed to hear.

The girl at the coat check, who had seen all kinds, took my raincoat by the tips of two fingers and turned her head away the way a woman does at the fish counter.

"Potatoes," I said. "I fell in a bin."

"We've had worse," she said, with the generosity of the underpaid. "Not lately."

The man at the piano was sixty or so, in a dinner jacket gone shiny at the elbows, with a pencil moustache and a toothpick in the corner of his mouth. He was marking a chart with a stub of pencil and did not look up when I came to the edge of the stage. The girl had called him Deacon. The sign over the door said CLARENCE TOLLIVER, PROP., in letters smaller than the cormorant's.

"Mr. Tolliver. I'm told you can lend a woman a towel."

"Mm-hm."

"I fell in a bin at the Harbor. I'd like to hear Miss Marchand, and I'd like not to do it smelling like a root cellar."

"Mm-hm."

"I'd also like a word with her afterward."

"Mm-hm." The toothpick travelled from the left corner of his mouth to the right, which I took for punctuation.

"Is that a yes?"

"Mm-hm."

"To which part?"

He lifted one long hand off the keys and took a folded towel from the lid of the piano, where it lay among the sheet music, and held it out without a word. It was white and clean and had the cormorant embroidered in one corner. I dried my face and the hair at my neck and was handing it back when he said, to the chart, "Mm-hm." Since he was looking at the chart it could have meant anything. I kept the towel.

At half past eleven the house lights dropped and somebody found the blue again, properly this time, and Yvette Marchand sang.

She was very pale. She had a low coil of dark hair, a face made of angles and shadow, grey-green eyes that rested on the back wall of the room as if it owed her money, and on her chin a small crescent scar, white as a thumbnail, which the blue turned into a second, smaller smile. She sang in French, low, in a voice with nothing laid on it, no vibrato, no ache for effect, which is why it hurt. The song was about a sailor and a promise. That much French I had from the movies. And on the word for sky, the l went dark and thick in her mouth, the way it does in my mother's, a sound like a coin dropped down a well.

The Cormorant didn't notice. The Cormorant had paid for French.

* * *

The band took a long number without her, and I went through the door beside the stage marked ARTISTES, where the E had been added later in different paint. Nobody stopped me. The staff had taken my measure and decided I smelled too much to be a threat.

The dressing room had been a stockroom and still held the tar and hemp of its first trade in the boards. There was a table with a mirror ringed in bulbs, four of which worked, and round the mirror's frame, tucked into the wood, a litter of cards and keepsakes: a postcard of Nice, a ticket stub, a dried daisy gone the colour of tea. Yvette sat before it in a grey wrapper over the slate gown, with a cup in her hand. She was still wearing the gloves. She looked at my shoes first, which is a thing I do myself, and I'd have liked her better if it hadn't made me notice.

"Miss Marchand. My name is Szymanski. I'd like five minutes."

"Everyone would like five minutes, mon cher. I have forty, and a set at one." Her voice in the room was lower than on the stage and had a ragged edge that the microphone sanded off.

"A man died at the Harbor Hotel on Tuesday night. He was registered as Delaney. His name was Maher. Declan Maher, a Customs tally clerk."

"I do not know such a man. I know sailors, and pianists, and these dentists." She lifted the cup. "Are you the police? You do not smell like police. Police smell of cigars."

"I don't smell like much tonight."

"No." The corner of her mouth moved. "Potatoes."

"You stood across from the Harbor on Tuesday night for ten minutes in the rain," I said. "A man at the lunch counter timed you by his pie clock."

"A man at a lunch counter has been timing women since the Flood."

"Co słychać?" I said.

It is what my mother says to the grocer, to the priest, to a stray cat: how are you, what's the news, what is there to hear. It is the smallest question in the language, and the cup stopped an inch below her lip. Only the cup. The rest of her went on as before, the pale face, the coil of hair, because she was a professional and the mirror was her friend. But I have watched hands all my life, and for the space of one breath that cup did not move at all.

She set it down in its saucer without a sound. "I do not speak this," she said. "You have mistaken me for someone."

"Is Marchand the name or the trade?"

"Marchand." She said it to the glass. "Like people who sell things. Write it however you like, Miss Szymanski. The police do." And my own name had come out of her mouth the way Americans never manage it, the sz soft and thick as butter, the y a short bright i. She heard it too. A muscle moved in the scarred chin.

I looked away from her, into the mirror, because it was the only place in the room where I could look at her face without being seen to. Pushed into the frame beside the postcard of Nice was a snapshot with a scalloped edge. Yvette, younger and thinner, in a man's cardigan, with her hair not yet coiled or dyed, unsmiling, standing on a narrow landing. The ceiling behind her was so low that the plaster seemed to rest on the top of her head. Along the wall at her back ran a rail of coat hooks, and the hooks were set at about the height of my knee.

I studied the ceiling. A ceiling like that you can lay your palm flat against. I studied the hooks. Hooks that low are meant for someone who can't reach higher.

"I have a set at one," Yvette said. In the mirror her eyes had followed mine to the photograph and come back. "I would like you to leave my room."

"Of course."

"And the towel. It is Deacon's. He will want it."

"He gave it to me."

"He gives everyone a towel," she said. "It is how he finds out who gives it back."

* * *

I took coffee at a table behind a pillar and listened to the one o'clock set, because there is nothing else to do in a nightclub when you aren't drinking, and because I wanted to see if she would look for me. She didn't. She looked at the back wall. By the door, at the end of the bar, a man in a dark overcoat sat with a beer he hadn't touched. He had not taken off his hat. I noted the snapped brim and a pair of shoulders that did not belong to a man who listened to French, and I noted nothing more, because the singer was doing the thing with the l again.

At ten to two the house lights came up and the tables began the business of finding coats. Deacon Tolliver rose from the piano, buttoned his jacket over a stomach he carried with dignity, and crossed the room to my pillar.

"Miss Szymanski," he said.

It was a sentence with a name in it, and I took it as a promotion.

"You brought the towel back folded in thirds. You left a dollar with the coat girl, who is saving for a hat. You sat through my whole second set without once looking at your watch, which is more than my wife can claim, and you did not try to buy my pianist a drink. I have been watching you for two and a half hours, which is how long I take to decide about a person who has fallen in a potato bin, and I would like to walk you out the back."

"That's more words than I've had from you all night."

"I was making up my mind. It is slow work. Mm-hm."

We went through the swing door at the side of the stage, past a crate of empty bottles and a rack of music stands, into a corridor that smelled of tar and hot lamps. He walked slowly, with his hands behind him.

"Yvette is a friend of mine," he said, "in the sense that I pay her union scale and she does not drink on the job. She has been with me three years. I do not ask where she was before, and she does not tell me, and we both think it an excellent arrangement. But you said something to her in that room that made her put her tea down. She does not put her tea down."

"Do you know a man named Maher?"

"I know a great many men named something. I own a share of this lease and I pay my band union scale. That is the whole of my interest in other people's business." He took the toothpick out and looked at it. "Tuesday was election night. The room was solid to the rafters. She did her three numbers in the eleven o'clock set, and when the band went into the long one she took her coat and went out by that door at the end. I thought, a walk, the room is hot. She came back at ten past one, as I was calling the last set. Her hair was wet through. She said not a word, and she sang that set like a woman paying off a mortgage."

"Did you tell the police?"

"Nobody asked. When the police do not ask, Miss Szymanski, a man in my business takes it for a courtesy." He put the toothpick back. "Everybody needs a walk. Some folks walk farther."

Behind us the swing door opened. The man in the hat stood in it, with the light of the club at his back and his brim snapped down over his eyes, and he did not say anything at all. Deacon did not turn around. His eyes moved, once, to a door on his left with a round window in it and steam behind the glass.

"Mm-hm," he said, and it was a different sentence this time.

I went through the kitchen door at a walk and then at something faster. The room was a long hot cave of ranges and steam, and a cook in a paper hat stood at a block breaking down a side of pork with a cleaver, with the calm of a man paid by the pound. He looked at me. He looked past me at the man who had come in behind, and he brought the cleaver down into the block so hard that the knives on the rack began to sing.

"My kitchen," he said, "is not a street."

We both heard it as addressed to us. I did not wait to see which of us he meant. I went down the aisle between a cauldron of chowder and a boy scraping plates, through a back door onto a wet flagged passage, and along it into Brine Street, where I could hear, behind me and fading, a man's voice and a cook's voice both saying something unkind about the other's mother.

The Cormorant's front awning was blue and white canvas, with the club's name in script on its scalloped valance and the rain falling off the edge in a bead curtain. Under it, against the wall, with the collar of a grey coat turned up and a cigarette in an ungloved hand, stood Yvette.

The bare hand was small and white and bitten at the nails like a schoolgirl's, and it went with nothing else about her.

"The police do not need to know I walk," she said.

"I'm not the police."

"You were something. It is in the way you stand, as though you were listening to a wire." She drew on the cigarette. The red end showed the scar on her chin and the grey-green of her eyes and then let them go. "I have nothing to tell you about a dead man. I have a night's work done, and I am going home."

"Who else knows?"

She looked at me. I thought for a second that she was going to answer, because her mouth began to make the shape of a word. Then she dropped the cigarette on the pavement and put her heel on it, and turned up her collar a second time, and went out from under the awning into the rain and up Brine Street. I watched her until the dark took her, a slim straight shape with her heels going down hard on the wet, never once looking back, in the manner of a woman who has trained herself not to.

Hanging from the awning rope, where she must have hooked it to light the cigarette, was one white cotton glove. It still held the shape of her hand. It was dripping.
